AI in Disaster Recovery: Co-Pilot, Not Autopilot


Every Product Is "AI-Powered" Now
Sit through any backup, storage or security product briefing this year and count how many times you hear "AI-powered." It is the new sticker on the box. Detection is AI-powered. Recovery is AI-powered. The dashboard probably has a chatbot in the corner.

So the fair question for anyone running a disaster recovery or cyber recovery program is not "should we use AI?" It is "where does AI actually make recovery faster, safer or more honest, and where does it just add a new thing that can break on the worst day of the year?"

That is what this post is about. Not the hype, and not the fear. Just a practical look at where artificial intelligence (some now call it SI, or super intelligence), along with the wider family of intelligent automation around it, fits in DR and CR, what it does well, what it does badly, and how to bring it in without handing the keys to something you cannot explain to an auditor.


Why This Matters in the World of DR
Disaster recovery has always been a race against a clock. What has changed is how many clocks we are racing and how fast the other side is moving.


Attackers are already using it: Phishing is better written, reconnaissance is faster and malware can be adjusted more quickly than before. Defenders who refuse to use the same class of tools are choosing to fight with a slower set of hands.
The scale has outgrown people: Hundreds of applications, thousands of servers, cloud accounts, SaaS platforms and petabytes of backups. No team can manually watch all of it for the early signs of encryption or corruption.
Plans go stale faster than people can update them: Infrastructure changes daily. Peoples thought process can change something for the better, but they didnt think about that for recovery.  Runbooks change when someone remembers. That gap is where recoveries fail.
The skills gap is real: Experienced recovery engineers are hard to hire and harder to keep. Anything that captures their knowledge and puts it in front of the next person at 3 a.m. has value.
Humans get tired: A cyber recovery can run for days. Statistics show that decision quality drops on hour thirty (Yes, hour 30!) of a bridge call, even with personnel rotations occurring. Tools that summarize, track and remind do not get tired.

In my previous post, We Have Two Different RTOs?, I made the case that a cyber RTO could potentially be measured in days and is set by the slowest link in a long chain. AI will not make your network faster or your backup storage read quicker. What it can do is shorten the human parts of that chain: finding the clean copy, deciding what to restore first, checking that what came back is trustworthy and keeping everyone informed while it happens.


Where AI Can Be Used Across DR and CR
This is a broad field, so it helps to break it into areas. Some of these are mature today. Others are early and should be treated as experiments.


  Before the Event (Prepare and Prevent):
Dependency discovery and mapping: Analyzing network flows, configuration data and logs to find which systems actually talk to each other. This feeds the application CMDB and the restore order, and it often finds dependencies nobody wrote down.
Business impact analysis support: Summarizing interviews with application owners, drafting BIA entries and flagging inconsistencies, such as an application marked "Tier 1" whose upstream dependency is marked "Tier 3."
Plan and runbook maintenance: Comparing written runbooks against the live environment and flagging drift: a server that was renamed, a database that moved to the cloud, a step that references a tool the company retired.
Predictive failure analysis: Spotting patterns in hardware telemetry, storage health and capacity trends that suggest a failure is coming, so the operational "normal disaster" never happens.
Restore time forecasting: Using restore test history, data growth and throughput trends to predict when an application's tested cyber RTO will drift past its target, before the next test proves it.
Exercise design: Generating tabletop scenarios, realistic injects and variations so the same team does not see the same exercise twice, and drafting after-action summaries from exercise notes.
  During the Event (Detect and Respond):
Ransomware detection in backup data: Watching backup jobs for signals like sudden changes in data entropy, unusual change rates, mass file renames, new file extensions or large deletions. The backup system becomes an early warning sensor, not just a copy.
Finding the last clean recovery point: Scanning backup history to estimate when the damage started, so the team restores from the newest copy that is actually clean instead of guessing and restoring twice.
Triage and scoping: Correlating alerts and logs across many systems to estimate the blast radius: which applications, which accounts, which data, and which backups may also be affected.
Incident co-pilot: Summarizing a long bridge call for the executive who just joined, keeping a running timeline, tracking open actions and drafting status updates for leadership, customers and regulators for a human to review and approve.
Runbook assistant: Answering "what is the next step to bring back the payments database?" from the approved runbooks, so engineers spend less time searching documents during the event.
  During Recovery (Restore and Validate):
Recovery sequencing: Recommending a restore order based on dependencies, business priority and available restore capacity, and adjusting it as conditions change.
Orchestration with approvals: Driving repeatable recovery steps (rebuild, restore, reconfigure, test) from recovery-as-code, pausing for human approval at each decision point.
Integrity validation: Checking restored systems and data for signs of tampering, persistence or corruption, and comparing restored data against known-good patterns before the business is asked to sign off.
Minimum viable service planning: Helping identify which functions of an application could come back first in a reduced form while the full restore continues.
  After the Event (Learn and Prove):
After-action reporting: Turning chat logs, tickets and timelines into a first draft of the post-incident review, including what took longest and why.
Compliance evidence: Mapping test results and recovery evidence to control requirements in frameworks such as ISO/IEC 27001 or SOC 2, and assembling the audit package.
Trend analysis across tests and incidents: Finding the patterns a person reading one report at a time would miss: the same dependency failing in three exercises, or the same team always waiting on the same approval.


  The Technical View:
There are really two kinds of AI in this list. Machine learning models are good at finding patterns in large amounts of data: entropy shifts, failure trends, unusual behavior. Generative AI and language models are good at reading, writing and summarizing: runbooks, BIAs, status updates and after-action reports. They fail in different ways, so they need different controls. A detection model that misses an attack is a quiet failure. A language model that invents a runbook step is a confident one.
  Leadership Translation:
AI does not replace the recovery plan, the backups or the people. It helps the people find problems sooner, make decisions faster and prove what happened afterward. The value is real, but it shows up as hours saved across a multi-day recovery, not as a magic button that brings the business back.


The Pros: What AI Brings to Recovery
Earlier detection: Patterns in backup and storage data can reveal encryption or corruption hours or days before a ransom note appears. Every hour earlier is an hour less of damage and a newer clean recovery point.
Faster decisions under pressure: Summaries, timelines and recommended restore orders cut the time leaders spend getting up to speed and arguing about what to do next.
Better clean-point selection: Restoring from the wrong backup and having to start over is one of the most expensive mistakes in cyber recovery. Analysis that narrows down the clean point protects the cyber RTO directly.
Living documentation: Plans, dependency maps and CMDB records that are checked against reality continuously, instead of once a year before the audit.
Scale without headcount: A small DR team can watch hundreds of applications, test more often and produce evidence faster than it could by hand.
Knowledge retention: The know-how of senior engineers can be captured in runbooks and assistants that junior staff can use when the expert is on a plane.
More realistic exercises: Varied scenarios and dynamic injects keep tabletop and technical exercises from becoming a script everyone has memorized.
Less toil for application teams: When the program hands owners drafted BIAs, pre-filled records and test results, participation becomes the easy path. That is influence, not mandate.


The Cons: What Can Go Wrong
Confident mistakes: Language models can produce answers that sound right and are FLAT WRONG! A made-up runbook step, a wrong server name or an invented dependency during a live recovery can cost hours or cause damage.
False positives and alert fatigue: A detection model that cries wolf every night teaches the team to ignore it. Then the real alert gets ignored too.
False negatives and false comfort: "The AI didn't flag anything" is not proof a backup is clean. Slow, careful attackers and new techniques can stay under a model's threshold.
A new attack surface: An AI agent with permission to run recovery steps is a privileged identity. It can be tricked with poisoned data or hidden instructions in documents and logs, or simply have its credentials stolen. If it can delete, it can be made to delete.
It may be down when you need it: Many AI tools depend on cloud services, identity platforms and network connectivity. In a real cyber event those may be the very things that are offline or untrusted. A recovery plan that assumes the AI assistant is available may fail on step one.
Data exposure: Feeding logs, configurations, runbooks and incident details into an AI service can expose sensitive information, credentials or regulated data if the service and its data handling are not reviewed first.
Explainability and audit: Auditors and regulators want to know why a decision was made. "The model recommended it" is not an acceptable answer on its own.
Model drift: A model trained on last year's environment and last year's attacks gets less accurate as both change, often without anyone noticing.
Skill atrophy: If the tool always does the thinking, the team slowly loses the ability to recover without it. That is a problem on the day the tool is unavailable.
"AI-washing" and lock-in: Some products label simple rules as AI. Others make it hard to move your data, models or runbooks elsewhere. Both make it harder to know what you are actually paying for.
Cost and complexity: Licensing, compute, integration work and the people needed to tune and govern these tools add up. Not every program needs every feature.


Who Does What: AI Assists, Humans Decide
The simplest way to keep AI useful and safe in recovery is to be clear about who leads at each stage.

          [ Detect ]  ──▶  [ Assess ]  ──▶  [ Decide ]  ──▶  [ Recover ]  ──▶  [ Validate ]  ──▶  [ Learn ]
              │               │               │                │                 │                │
          AI leads        AI assists      Human leads      AI executes       AI checks,       AI drafts,
          Human           Human           AI supplies      approved steps    Human signs      Human
          confirms        scopes          options          Human approves    off              reviews

Notice where the human sits. AI is strongest at the edges: noticing early and summarizing afterward. In the middle, where decisions are made and destructive actions happen, a person stays in charge.


Guardrails That Make AI Safe to Use in Recovery
Keep humans in charge of anything destructive: Deleting data, changing retention, altering immutability, failing over production or restoring over a live system always requires human approval, using credentials the AI does not hold.
Give AI the least privilege possible: Read-only by default. Separate identities for AI agents. No access to backup administration with production credentials.
Log every AI action and recommendation: What it saw, what it suggested, who approved it and what happened. That log is your audit trail and your after-action evidence.
Ground it in approved sources: Runbook assistants should answer only from approved, version-controlled documents and show where each answer came from, so engineers can check it.
Plan for the AI being unavailable: Every AI-assisted step needs a manual fallback. Better yet, decide which AI tools must work inside the isolated recovery environment and test them there.
Measure it like any other control: Track detection accuracy, false positive rates and how often recommendations were overridden. Re-check after major changes to the environment.
Review what data it sees: Involve privacy, legal and security before sending incident data, logs or configurations to any AI service, and know where that data is stored.
Put AI in your exercises: Run tabletops where the AI is wrong, where it is unavailable and where it has been compromised. See how the team reacts before it matters.


  The Technical View:
Treat AI tools as applications in your own DR program. They have dependencies (identity, network, cloud services, model endpoints), they hold sensitive data and they can fail. That means they belong in the BIA and the CMDB like everything else. And yes, if the recovery plan depends on them, they need an operational RTO and a cyber RTO of their own.
  Leadership Translation:
The question to ask any team or vendor proposing AI in recovery is simple: "What happens if it is wrong, and what happens if it is gone?" If there is a clear answer to both, the risk is managed. If there is not, the tool is not ready to be part of the recovery path.


Getting Started: Crawl, Walk, Run
You do not need to bet the recovery program on AI to get value from it. Start where mistakes are cheap and work toward where they are expensive.


Crawl (low risk, quick wins): Use AI to draft documents a human reviews: BIA summaries, runbook updates, exercise scenarios, after-action reports and audit evidence mapping. Nothing it produces runs on its own.
Walk (analytics and detection): Turn on anomaly detection in backup data, dependency discovery and restore time forecasting. Feed the results into the CMDB and dashboards, and tune for accuracy before anyone pages on it.
Run (assisted recovery): Add AI-recommended restore sequencing and orchestration of approved recovery-as-code steps, with human approval gates, full logging and tested manual fallbacks. Prove it in exercises before trusting it in an event.

Bring application owners along the same way you would for any other part of the program. Show them the time it saves, let them see the drafts before anything is published and give their teams credit when the first AI-assisted test goes well.


The Bottom Line
AI is not going to recover your business for you. Tested, isolated, immutable backups and well-practiced people still do that. What AI can do is help those people see trouble sooner, find the clean copy faster, decide with better information and prove afterward what happened.

The risk is not using AI. The risk is using it without guardrails: trusting answers nobody checked, giving an agent permissions nobody would give a new hire, or building a recovery plan around a tool that will not be there when the lights go out.

Use it as a co-pilot, not an autopilot. Keep a human hand on anything that cannot be undone. And test the AI the same way you test everything else in the program, because on the worst day of the year, the only tools that matter are the ones you have proven work.
