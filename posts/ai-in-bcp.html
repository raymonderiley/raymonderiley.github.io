AI in Business Continuity: A Smarter Plan Still Needs a Human Owner


The Same Question, a Different Audience
In my earlier post, AI in Disaster Recovery: Co-Pilot, Not Autopilot, I looked at where artificial intelligence fits in restoring technology: spotting ransomware in backups, finding the clean recovery point, sequencing restores. That post was written for engineers and technology leaders.

This one is for the other side of the house. Business continuity planning (BCP) is about keeping the business running while a disruption lasts, whatever the cause. Its users are department heads, process owners, team leads, frontline staff, HR, facilities, procurement, communications and leadership. Its problems are different: people, places, suppliers, paperwork and decisions made under pressure. So the question is the same, but the answer is different: where does AI (some now call it SI, or super intelligence), along with the wider family of intelligent automation around it, actually help a business keep going, and where does it create new risk?

If you are new to the series, the BCP series summary covers trends, roles, plan components and testing on one page.


Why AI Matters in the World of BCP
Business continuity has always had an information problem. The plan depends on knowing what is critical, what it relies on, who does what and how to reach them, and all of that changes constantly. Most programs struggle not because people do not care, but because there is too much to keep current with too few hands.


Plans go stale faster than people can update them: Staff change roles, suppliers change, processes are redesigned and offices move. A plan written last year describes an organization that has already changed.
The BIA is slow and painful: Business impact analysis means interviewing dozens of process owners, consolidating their answers and reconciling contradictions. It often takes months, and by the time it finishes, parts are out of date.
The risk picture moves daily: Supplier problems, severe weather, regional unrest and infrastructure outages can affect operations with little warning. No continuity team can watch every feed.
Disruptions are longer and more tangled: When several things go wrong at once and last for days, the people running the response need help keeping track of decisions, actions and messages.
Continuity teams are small: Many organizations have one or two continuity professionals supporting hundreds of plan owners. Anything that removes routine work frees them for the conversations only a person can have.
People need plain answers fast: A team lead at 3 a.m. does not want to read 60 pages. They want to know what to do next.

AI does not fix the hard parts of continuity: getting leadership to set priorities, getting owners to take part and getting people to practice. What it can do is take much of the drafting, sorting, watching and summarizing off people's plates, so the human effort goes where it matters most.


Where AI Can Be Used Across BCP
This is a broad field. Some of these uses are practical today. Others are early and should be treated as experiments.


  Understanding the Business (Analyze):
BIA interviews and consolidation: Transcribing and summarizing process owner interviews, drafting BIA entries, and flagging contradictions, such as two teams giving the same process very different recovery priorities.
Process and dependency discovery: Reading procedures, org charts, application inventories and supplier lists to suggest which processes rely on which people, systems, sites and suppliers, for owners to confirm.
Key person and single point of failure detection: Spotting processes that depend on one or two individuals, one location or one supplier, using documentation, access records and team structures.
Risk assessment support: Summarizing internal incident history, industry events and regional risk data into a draft risk profile for each site or service.
  Writing and Maintaining Plans (Plan):
Plan drafting from templates: Turning BIA results and interview notes into a first draft of a business continuity plan in the organization's standard format, for the owner to review and correct.
Plain-language rewrites: Converting policy-style text into short, numbered, action-first steps that a stressed person can follow, and producing one-page quick reference cards from longer plans.
Plan quality checks: Reviewing plans for missing sections, vague triggers, roles without backups, outdated contacts and steps that reference retired systems or former suppliers.
Change-driven updates: Watching HR, procurement, change management and facilities records and flagging which plans are affected when a person leaves, a supplier changes or a team moves.
Translation and accessibility: Producing versions of plans and quick reference cards in the languages staff actually use, and formats that work on a phone.
  Watching for Trouble (Monitor):
Early warning and horizon scanning: Monitoring news, weather, transport, utility and supplier status feeds and alerting the continuity team when something could affect a site, a supplier or a critical process.
Supplier and third-party monitoring: Tracking signs of supplier distress, outages or service changes, and mapping which internal processes would be affected if a provider went down.
Staffing and absence patterns: Noticing when absences, travel or vacations leave a critical team below its minimum level, before a disruption makes it a crisis.
  Responding to a Disruption (Respond):
Response assistant: Answering questions such as "what is the workaround if the order system is down?" from approved plans only, with a link to the source so people can check it.
Situation summaries: Summarizing a long crisis call or chat thread for the leader who just joined, and keeping a running log of decisions, open actions and owners.
Communications drafting: Drafting employee notices, customer updates, supplier messages and regulator notifications from approved templates, for human review and approval.
Staff accounting and check-ins: Helping track responses from mass notification check-ins, identifying who has not responded and summarizing staff availability by team and location.
Workload and staffing rebalancing: Suggesting how to move work between teams or sites when one location or group is unavailable, based on skills and capacity.
  Learning and Proving (Exercise and Improve):
Exercise design: Generating realistic scenarios and injects tailored to each business unit, so the same team does not see the same exercise twice.
Exercise facilitation support: Playing the role of a supplier, customer, reporter or regulator during simulations, so injects feel realistic and the facilitator can focus on the room.
After-action reports: Turning exercise notes, chat logs and timelines into a draft after-action report and a list of proposed actions.
Trend analysis: Finding patterns across many exercises and incidents, such as the same contact list failing in three plans or the same approval delaying every response.
Audit evidence: Mapping plans, test results and closed actions to the requirements of standards and regulations, and assembling the evidence package.
  The Technical View:
Most AI value in BCP comes from language models working with documents, conversations and messages, plus some pattern detection on data feeds. That makes the quality of the source material decisive. If the AI drafts plans from outdated interviews or answers questions from an old version of a plan, it will give confident answers that are wrong. Ground every AI tool in approved, version-controlled sources, and connect it to authoritative systems (HR, procurement, the application inventory or CMDB) instead of copies.
  Leadership Translation:
AI can shrink the slowest parts of business continuity, such as the BIA, plan writing, plan upkeep and reporting, from months to weeks. That frees continuity staff and plan owners to spend their time on decisions, relationships and practice. It does not change who is accountable. Every plan still needs a business owner who has read it, checked it and practiced it.


The Pros: What AI Brings to Business Continuity
Faster BIAs and plans: Work that used to take months of interviews and drafting can be done in weeks, which means more critical processes get a real plan sooner.
Plans that stay current: Automated checks against HR, supplier and system changes catch drift between annual reviews, instead of during the next audit or the next disruption.
More usable plans: Plain-language rewrites, one-page quick reference cards and translations make plans easier to follow under pressure, which is where plans most often fail.
Earlier warning: Continuous monitoring of suppliers, weather and regional events can give hours or days of notice to move staff, contact backup suppliers or warn customers.
Better crisis coordination: Summaries, decision logs and drafted messages reduce the time leaders spend catching up and let them focus on decisions.
More and better exercises: Fresh scenarios, realistic role-play and quick after-action reports make it easier to test more often and learn more from each test.
Scale for small teams: One or two continuity professionals can support far more plan owners when routine drafting, checking and reporting are handled by tools.
Less work for plan owners: When owners receive a drafted plan to review instead of a blank template to fill in, participation goes up. That is influence through convenience, not mandate.


The Cons: What Can Go Wrong
Plans nobody really owns: The biggest risk in BCP is a plan that was generated, approved without reading and never practiced. It looks complete and fails on first contact with reality.
Confident errors: AI can produce plan steps, contacts or recovery priorities that sound right and are wrong. In a live disruption, a wrong phone number or a made-up workaround costs time people do not have.
Generic plans: AI-drafted plans can drift toward generic best practice instead of how this team actually works. A plan that does not match reality will not be followed.
Bad source data in, bad plan out: If interviews were incomplete or records are outdated, the AI will turn them into a polished plan that is still wrong.
Sensitive data exposure: Continuity plans hold personal contact details, home addresses, staff health and availability information, site layouts and supplier contracts. Sending that to an AI service without review creates privacy and security risk.
Unavailable when needed: Many AI tools depend on network access, cloud services and corporate sign-in. In a cyber event or site loss, those may be the first things to go. A plan that only exists inside an AI tool may not be there on the day.
Messages that miss the human tone: Drafted communications to employees and customers during a crisis can sound cold or wrong. People remember how an organization spoke to them in a bad moment.
Too many alerts: Horizon scanning that flags every storm and every supplier news story trains the team to ignore alerts, including the one that matters.
Monitoring that feels like surveillance: Using staff absence, location or activity data to plan continuity can cross lines on privacy and trust, and may break employment or privacy laws if not handled carefully.
Skill loss: If the AI always writes the plan and runs the exercise, plan owners and continuity staff may lose the judgment that comes from doing it themselves.
Cost, lock-in and hype: Some tools label simple automation as AI, others make it hard to export your plans and data, and all of them add licensing, integration and governance costs.


Who Does What: AI Assists, People Own
The safest way to use AI in BCP is to be clear about where AI helps and where a person stays accountable.

    [ Analyze ]    ──▶    [ Plan ]    ──▶    [ Monitor ]    ──▶    [ Respond ]    ──▶    [ Exercise ]    ──▶    [ Improve ]
        │                   │                   │                     │                      │                     │

    Owner confirms       Owner edits,        Team decides          drafts messages        scenarios,            reports,
    priorities          approves and        whether to act          People decide         People take          Owners close

Notice that every column ends with a person(s). AI produces drafts, signals and summaries. People confirm priorities, approve plans and messages, make decisions and practice.


Guardrails for Using AI in BCP
A named human owner for every plan: The owner reviews and approves every AI-drafted section and is accountable for its accuracy. "The AI wrote it" is never an acceptable answer.
Review AI drafts with the people who do the work: Walk through each drafted plan with the team that will use it. They will spot what does not match reality in minutes.
Ground answers in approved sources: Response assistants should answer only from current, approved plans, and show which plan and section the answer came from.
Keep copies outside the AI tool: The authoritative plan and quick reference cards must be available offline, out-of-band or printed, so they work when the network and AI tools do not.
Protect personal and sensitive data: Involve privacy, HR, legal and security before plans, contact lists or staff data are sent to any AI service. Share only what is needed, and know where it is stored.
Humans approve every external message: Customer, regulator, media and all-staff communications are always reviewed and approved by an authorized person before they go out.
Tune the alerts: Set thresholds for horizon scanning that match your sites, suppliers and critical processes, and review false alarms regularly.
Be transparent with staff: If AI is used to analyze staffing, absence or location data for continuity, tell people what is used, why and how it is protected.
Exercise with and without the AI: Run tabletops where the AI tool is wrong, unavailable or compromised, as well as ones where it helps. The team should be able to run the plan either way.
  The Technical View:
Treat AI tools used in business continuity as dependencies in their own right. Record them in the application inventory, note what they depend on and decide what the business does if they are unavailable. If a plan relies on an AI assistant during a disruption, that assistant needs its own recovery target and a manual fallback.
  Leadership Translation:
Ask two questions of any AI proposal in business continuity: "Who owns the output?" and "What happens if the tool is wrong or gone?" If there is a named owner and a tested fallback, the risk is managed. If not, the tool is not ready to be part of the plan.


Getting Started: Crawl, Walk, Run
Crawl (low risk, quick wins): Use AI to summarize BIA interviews, draft plan sections for owner review, rewrite plans into plain language, create quick reference cards and draft exercise scenarios and after-action reports. Nothing goes live without a human edit and approval.
Walk (watching and checking): Add plan quality checks, change-driven alerts from HR, procurement and change management, and horizon scanning for your key sites and suppliers. Tune for accuracy before anyone acts on alerts.
Run (assisted response): Introduce a response assistant grounded in approved plans, situation summaries and drafted communications during exercises first. Use them in real events only after they have proven reliable in practice, with offline fallbacks in place.

As with any part of the program, bring plan owners along through influence. Show them the time AI saves, let them correct the drafts and give their teams credit when the first AI-assisted exercise goes well.


The Bottom Line
AI can make business continuity faster, more current and easier to use. It can turn months of BIA work into weeks, keep plans in step with a changing organization, warn of trouble earlier and give stressed people plain answers when they need them.

But continuity has always been about people: knowing who does what, trusting the plan and having practiced it. AI cannot own a plan, make a judgment call on behalf of the business or build the muscle memory that only exercises create. Used without care, it produces polished plans nobody owns and confident answers nobody checked.

Use AI to do the heavy lifting on drafting, watching and summarizing. Keep people in charge of priorities, approvals, decisions and practice. A smarter plan is a good thing, as long as it still has a human owner who knows what is in it.
