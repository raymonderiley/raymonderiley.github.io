When Infrastructure Fails, Does Your Business?

Business Continuity & Resilience

The past few weeks have shown how many organizations are one bad day away from standing still. This is why business continuity has to be owned by the business, and proven, not just written down.

November 17, 2026 · 10 min read · For technical teams and executives

On November 12, Microsoft 365 went down. Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and Copilot stopped working for businesses and governments around the world, and the disruption lasted roughly a full day. Hospitals, universities and rail operators struggled to communicate. It wasn't a cyberattack. According to Microsoft's preliminary report, the defect behind it had been introduced months earlier and had passed load testing at the time.

That same week brought an FAA ground stop, a widespread system outage at United Airlines, and problems at major banks. Earlier this year, a ransomware attack forced Coca-Cola's Fairlife dairy unit to halt all of its U.S. production for more than a week.

Each of these events has its own cause. Together they make the same point: technology will fail, and the only question is whether your business keeps going when it does. That is what business continuity is about.

What Business Continuity Actually Means

Business continuity is the discipline of keeping a company's most important work going while something is broken. A recovery runbook is only one piece of it. The rest is everything around the technology: the people who know the workarounds, the alternate ways to take an order or pay a supplier, the decisions about what can wait and what can't. The test is simple. If a critical system disappeared this morning, would customers notice? A strong continuity program makes sure the answer is “not much.”

Disaster recovery asks, “How fast can IT restore the system?” Business continuity asks, “How do we keep serving customers and collecting revenue while it's gone?” You need both.

What Matters For Business Continuity

The short version: business continuity is about keeping the services that matter most running at an acceptable level, whatever caused the disruption. Ransomware and physical disasters threaten that in different ways, so a strong plan covers both.

Plan for What You Lose, Not for the Event

Instead of writing a separate plan for every threat, good BCPs plan for losing one of five things:

  • People. Staff are hurt, unavailable, or can't reach work.
  • Facilities. A building is damaged, inaccessible or unsafe.
  • Technology. Systems, networks or applications are down.
  • Data. Information is destroyed, corrupted, encrypted or stolen.
  • Third parties. A key supplier, cloud provider or partner fails.

A hurricane usually takes out facilities and people while systems stay up. Ransomware is the opposite: the building is fine, but technology, data and trust in your environment are gone. Planning around these losses means you're covered for scenarios nobody listed.

Know What Matters Most Before Anything Happens

  • Business impact analysis (BIA). Find your critical business services and what an outage costs over time in money, customers, regulators, safety and reputation.
  • Recovery targets. For each service, set how long you can survive without it (maximum tolerable downtime), the recovery time objective (RTO) and the recovery point objective (RPO).
  • Dependencies. Map what each service needs: people, applications, data, facilities and vendors. Single points of failure and key-person risks show up here.
  • Priority order. In a big event you can't restore everything at once. Leadership should agree the sequence in advance, not argue about it mid-crisis.

Governance and Decisions

  • A named crisis management team, with clear authority and alternates for every role.
  • Clear triggers for who can declare an incident and activate the plan.
  • Pre-approved emergency authority, especially for spending, so recovery isn't held up by normal approvals.
  • A decision log, which matters later for insurance, regulators and litigation.

Communication

  • Out-of-band channels. Ransomware often takes down email, chat, phones tied to the network and the identity systems behind them. You need backup channels set up and tested beforehand.
  • Offline contact lists for staff, executives, vendors, regulators, insurers, outside counsel and law enforcement.
  • Stakeholder plans covering employees, customers, partners, regulators, investors and the media, with pre-drafted holding statements.
  • One source of truth, so mixed messages don't damage trust.

Running in Degraded Mode

This is where many plans fall short.

  • Manual workarounds. How does each critical function keep going without its systems (paper forms, spreadsheets, phone-based processes)?
  • How long they last. Most workarounds hold for days, not weeks. Ransomware outages often run for weeks.
  • Backlog. Work done manually has to be entered and reconciled once systems return, and that effort is usually underestimated.
  • Minimum service levels. Decide in advance what “good enough” looks like for customers during the disruption.

What Makes Ransomware Different

Ransomware is a business crisis, not just an IT outage. Several things set it apart:

  • You can't trust your environment. Systems may still hold attacker access, so you can't just switch to a backup site. You may first need to rebuild identity systems such as Active Directory and recover in a clean environment.
  • Backups get targeted. Attackers go after backups on purpose. Immutable, isolated backups that have been tested are what makes recovery possible.
  • Forensics vs. speed. Investigators want to preserve evidence and find the way in. The business wants systems back. The plan should say how that conflict gets resolved.
  • It's usually a data breach too. Most attacks now steal data before encrypting it. Even with perfect restores you can face extortion, notification duties and lawsuits.
  • Regulatory clocks. Many deadlines start when you detect or determine the impact. Examples include the SEC's four-business-day disclosure rule for material incidents at public companies, GDPR's 72-hour notice, and state, sector and contract requirements.
  • The ransom decision. Whether to pay involves legal counsel, sanctions screening, insurers and executive judgment. Think it through before you're under pressure.
  • Cyber insurance. Policies often require specific breach coaches, forensics firms and negotiators, plus prompt notice. Breaking those terms can put coverage at risk.
  • Long outages. Plan for weeks of disruption, not hours.

People

  • Life safety comes first in physical events.
  • Have backups for key roles, and cross-train before an event so no recovery hinges on one person.
  • Watch for fatigue in long incidents: run shifts, rotate staff and support their wellbeing. Burned-out teams make mistakes.
  • Tell employees clearly what they should and shouldn't do. For example, don't reconnect devices and don't talk to the press.

Third Parties and Supply Chain

  • Know which vendors your critical services depend on, and check whether their continuity holds up.
  • Read the contract terms: SLAs, notification duties and right to audit.
  • Watch for concentration risk, such as one cloud provider or one payment processor behind several critical services.
  • Have pre-arranged alternates or surge support, such as incident response retainers, restoration partners and temporary staffing.

Money

  • Cash and liquidity. Can you keep paying payroll and suppliers and collecting from customers if your finance systems are down?
  • Emergency spending. Have pre-authorized limits and a way to track costs.
  • Claim records. Record losses and costs carefully as they happen, so the insurance claim holds up.

Keep the Plan Usable

  • Accessible offline. A plan stored only on the SharePoint site that just got encrypted is no use. Keep printed or offline copies with key people.
  • Short and action-focused. Checklists and decision trees work better in a crisis than long narrative documents.
  • Exercised regularly. Tabletops and live tests that use realistic assumptions show the gaps that documents hide.
  • Kept up to date. Review the plan after org changes, new systems, vendor changes and every real incident or exercise.

Recovery and Return to Normal

  • Restore in an agreed order tied to business priorities, not whatever IT can bring back first.
  • Have business owners confirm that restored systems and data are correct and complete before declaring them back.
  • Clear the backlog and reconcile records.
  • Watch closely after recovery for reinfection or a return of the attacker.
  • Hold a review afterwards, then update the BIA, plans and controls.

The Most Common Failure Points

  • Plans assume IT will be back in hours when ransomware takes weeks.
  • Nobody can communicate because the communication tools are down.
  • Manual workarounds were never written down or tested.
  • Restore priorities were never agreed, so recovery stalls in debate.
  • Backups exist but were never tested at full scale, or were compromised.
  • Legal, regulatory and insurance obligations get missed in the first 72 hours.
  • Business continuity plans and IT disaster recovery plans were built separately and don't connect.

A BCP succeeds when the business can keep its most important promises to customers, regulators and employees while technology recovers. Restoring servers is only part of that.

The Assumptions That Quietly Break Plans

Most plans don't fail because nobody wrote them. They fail because they rest on assumptions nobody has checked in years. The ones I see most in tabletop exercises:

  • “The infrastructure will just be there.” The business sets an 8-hour recovery target without asking whether identity, networks and recovery capacity can be rebuilt that fast.
  • “The vendor backs up our data.” Many SaaS contracts protect the platform, not your data. The vendor's restore may be slow, costly or all-or-nothing.
  • “The backup job succeeded, so we're covered.” That only proves something was copied, not that it can be restored or that it's clean.
  • “The plan is up to date.” New cloud services and workflows go live every quarter without anyone updating or testing the recovery plan.
  • “People will know what to do.” Not if the procedures and contact lists live on the systems that just went down.

The Four Numbers That Matter

Here is what each measure means, for the engineer and for the executive.

Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

The longest a service can be down before the damage becomes unacceptable.

For the CEO: How long can we be offline before we lose customers or breach contracts, and has anyone proven we can come back that fast, infrastructure included?

Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

How much data you can afford to lose, measured in time.

For the CEO: If we can only lose one hour of transactions, a nightly backup won't do. Closing that gap costs money, and that's a business decision.

Testing Frequency

How often recovery is proven, not just discussed.

For the CEO: Once or twice a year isn't enough when systems change monthly. Application owners should set a schedule that proves the RTO and RPO.

Failover Capability

The ability to move critical work to a redundant system or site when the primary one fails.

For the CEO: Very short recovery targets can't be met by restoring from backup. They need key workflows already running somewhere else.

An RTO that has never been tested end to end is not an objective. It's a hope.

Ransomware Changes the Rules

A cloud outage is a disaster. Ransomware is a disaster with an opponent. Attackers go after backups first, because destroying your way back is how they force you to pay. Surviving that takes a few non-negotiables:

  • Immutable backups that nobody, including your own administrators, can change or delete before they expire.
  • Isolation from the production network and from production logins, so an attacker who owns your environment can't reach your last good copy.
  • Accounted-for credentials: know exactly who can delete or change backups, protect those accounts with strong authentication and a second approver, and review the list often.
  • No blind spots: on-premises, cloud and SaaS data all need a protected copy. One unprotected component can stall the whole recovery.
  • Proof of a clean restore: regular restore testing, integrity checks and malware scanning of recovery points, with retention long enough to reach back before the attacker first got in.

From the Tabletop

In the business-level exercise I built around a total-loss ransomware attack, the gaps are the kind most organizations carry without knowing it: a one-hour data loss promise backed by nightly backups, a tested recovery time more than twice the customer commitment, and a last surviving copy that turns out to contain the attacker's tools. None of it is obvious on paper. All of it is fixable once someone asks the right question.

Five Questions Every CEO Should Ask This Quarter

  1. For our most critical services, is the tested recovery time equal to or less than what we have promised customers?
  2. If ransomware destroyed our production systems and our everyday backups tonight, what protected copy would we restore from, and who can delete it?
  3. Does every critical application have a current recovery plan with a named owner, and when was it last tested?
  4. What is our true data loss tolerance, and are we paying for a backup design that actually meets it?
  5. Can a new workflow or SaaS tool reach production without a recovery test and a plan update?

For the Technical Team: Start Here

  • Map each critical service and its infrastructure dependencies, then compare real recovery times with the business RTO.
  • Find every copy of your data that isn't immutable and isolated, and every account that can delete backups.
  • Bring Data Protection into change management so new systems are protected before go-live.
  • Run a business-level tabletop with the business owner, application owner and Data Protection in the same room.

The Bottom Line

Outages and attacks are now a normal part of running a business on technology you don't fully control. The organizations that come through them aren't the ones with the thickest binders. They know their real recovery times, protect their last good copy like the company's future depends on it, and test often enough to trust the answers.

Business continuity isn't an IT project. It's a promise the business makes to its customers, and the only way to know whether you can keep it is to test it before the next bad day. If it does not pass the test, you'll need to improve your plan and/or spend more resources on it, or the business might need to lower their expectations.
