Business Continuity in 2026: The Trends Reshaping the Plan on the Shelf


The Binder Is Not Dead, But It Has Company
For a long time, business continuity planning meant a binder. Someone in risk or facilities wrote it, the department heads signed it, and it sat on a shelf (or in a shared drive folder nobody could find) until the next audit. If the building flooded, the binder told you where the alternate office was and who to call.

That model was built for a world where the biggest threats were physical: fire, weather, a power outage, a pandemic once in a generation. Those threats have not gone away. But they now share the stage with ransomware that shuts down every location at once, a cloud or software provider outage that takes out half your critical processes in an afternoon, and regulators who no longer accept "we have a plan" as an answer.

This is the first post in a four-part series on business continuity planning (BCP). Before getting into roles, components and testing, it helps to look at where the discipline is heading and why. Here are the trends I see shaping business continuity right now, each with a plain example of what it looks like on the ground.


Trend 1: From Business Continuity to Operational Resilience
The biggest shift is in the question being asked. Traditional BCP asked, "How do we recover each department after a disruption?" Operational resilience asks, "Which services matter most to our customers, how much disruption can they tolerate, and can we prove we stay within that limit?"

That changes the unit of planning. Instead of a plan per department or per building, organizations identify their most important business services (taking a payment, shipping an order, admitting a patient, processing a claim) and map everything that service depends on: people, processes, technology, facilities, data and suppliers. Each service gets a tolerance for disruption, usually a maximum time it can be down before the harm becomes unacceptable.


Example: A regional lender used to have separate continuity plans for its call center, loan operations and IT. Under a service view, it defines "customer can make a loan payment" as one important service. Mapping it shows the service touches the call center, the payment platform, a third-party card processor, the mailroom and two data centers. The plan is now built around keeping that one outcome alive, not around keeping each team busy.

In financial services, regulators have pushed this hardest. The European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act has applied since early 2025, and the UK's regime has moved into its steady state, where firms are expected to show they can stay within their tolerances, not just that they have mapped their services. Other sectors are following the same logic, even where no regulation forces it.


Trend 2: Regulators and Auditors Want Evidence, Not Documents
Closely tied to the first trend is a change in what "compliant" means. A few years ago, a current plan with signatures and a test date was often enough. Today, supervisors, auditors and large customers increasingly ask to see what happened when the plan was tested, what failed, and what was fixed.

New incident reporting and third-party notification rules have appeared in several jurisdictions over the past year, and the direction is consistent: report disruptions quickly, know who your critical suppliers are, and demonstrate that controls work in practice.


Example: During a customer due diligence review, a software provider is asked not only for its BCP but for the results of its last exercise, the list of issues found and the closure date for each. The provider that can hand over a dated after-action report with tracked remediation wins the deal. The one that sends a 60-page plan with no test evidence gets a follow-up questionnaire.


Trend 3: Third-Party and Concentration Risk Move to the Center
Most organizations now run critical processes on platforms they do not own: cloud infrastructure, SaaS applications, payment processors, managed service providers, logistics partners. That is efficient until one of those providers has a bad day. When many organizations depend on the same few providers, a single outage becomes a shared disruption across whole industries.

Continuity planning is responding in three ways. First, by mapping not just suppliers but their suppliers (fourth parties). Second, by asking, for each critical provider, "what do we do if they are unavailable for a day? A week?" Third, by building exit and substitution options before they are needed.


Example: A retailer discovers that its point-of-sale system, online checkout and loyalty program all rely on the same payment gateway. A two-hour gateway outage on a holiday weekend would stop sales in every channel at once. The continuity response is a pre-contracted backup processor, a documented "cash and card imprint" fallback for stores, and a decision tree for when to switch.


Trend 4: Cyber Is the Default Scenario, Not a Special Case
Ransomware and destructive malware have changed what a "worst case" looks like for business continuity. A fire affects one building. A cyberattack can affect every building, every system and every remote worker at the same time, and it can last for weeks.

Many continuity programs now treat a prolonged loss of technology as the scenario every business unit must plan for, regardless of cause. That forces a hard question that IT disaster recovery alone cannot answer: how does the business keep operating, even in a reduced way, while the technology is being rebuilt?


Example: A hospital's continuity plan used to assume the electronic health record would be back within a few hours. After a peer organization spent weeks on paper during a ransomware event, the hospital rewrites its plans around a two-week technology outage: printed downtime forms, pre-staged medication lists, manual lab result runners and a clear process for re-entering paper records once systems return.

This is where my earlier post, We Have Two Different RTOs?, connects to BCP. If the cyber recovery time for a system is measured in days, the business continuity plan has to cover those days.


Trend 5: Manual Workarounds Are Back in Fashion
For years, the trend was to automate everything and assume the systems would be there. Now, organizations are rediscovering the value of knowing how to do critical work without them. The key word is "critical." Nobody is planning to run the whole company on paper, but the few processes that cannot stop need a fallback that people have actually practiced.


Example: A distribution center keeps a small kit for each shift lead: printed pick lists for the top 200 products, pre-filled shipping labels for key customers, a laminated contact sheet and a simple paper log. Twice a year, one shift runs for 30 minutes using only the kit. The first time, it took twice as long as expected. The second time, it was close to normal pace.


Trend 6: People Risk Gets Its Own Line
Pandemic planning taught everyone that a disruption can be about people, not buildings or systems. Hybrid work then spread teams across homes, cities and time zones. Today, people risk shows up in several forms: key individuals who hold knowledge nobody else has, small teams concentrated in one location, burnout during long incidents and the simple difficulty of hiring experienced continuity professionals.


Example: A finance team realizes that only two people know how to run the month-end close in its legacy system, and both sit in the same office and often travel together. The continuity fix is not technical: cross-training a third person, documenting the steps in a short runbook and agreeing that the two experts never travel on the same flight during close week.


Trend 7: Polycrisis and Longer Disruptions
Disruptions increasingly overlap. A severe weather event knocks out power, which delays a supplier, while a cyber incident is still being cleaned up, while staff are dealing with the same storm at home. Geopolitical tension adds trade restrictions, sanctions and regional instability on top. Planners use the word "polycrisis" for this: several problems at once, each making the others worse.

The practical effect is that plans can no longer assume one clean event with a clear start and finish. They need to work when two things go wrong together, and they need to hold up for weeks, not just hours.


Example: A manufacturer's tabletop exercise starts as a regional flood. Halfway through, the facilitator adds an inject: the backup site's internet provider reports an outage, and a key component supplier announces export delays. The team learns that its plan assumed the backup site would always be available and that the alternate supplier list had not been checked in two years.


Trend 8: Climate and Extreme Weather Become Planning Inputs
Heat waves, wildfires, floods and severe storms are hitting places and seasons that historically saw fewer of them. Continuity teams are adding climate data to site risk assessments, rethinking where alternate locations are, and planning for longer utility outages.


Example: A company with two offices 30 miles apart used each as the other's backup. A wildfire season risk review shows both sit in the same high-risk zone and depend on the same regional power grid. The plan now names a third, more distant option, and remote work is formally documented as a recovery strategy with tested equipment and connectivity requirements.


Trend 9: Integrated Programs and Shared Data
Business continuity, IT disaster recovery, cyber incident response, crisis management and third-party risk have often lived in separate teams with separate tools and separate plans. The trend now is to bring them together: one view of critical services, one set of dependencies, one crisis structure and shared exercises.

Alongside this, many organizations are moving off spreadsheets and documents toward dedicated continuity platforms or their existing enterprise architecture and CMDB tools, so dependency maps, contacts and plans stay connected and current.


Example: A software company runs one combined exercise each year instead of three separate ones. The IT team restores systems, the business units run their workarounds, the communications team drafts customer notices and the crisis team makes decisions, all from the same scenario. Gaps that only appear at the hand-offs between teams finally show up.


Trend 10: AI Enters the Continuity Toolkit
AI is showing up in business continuity the same way it is showing up in disaster recovery: drafting and updating plans, summarizing business impact interviews, monitoring supplier and news feeds for early warning, generating exercise scenarios and helping write crisis communications. It is useful, and it comes with the same warnings I covered in AI in Disaster Recovery: Co-Pilot, Not Autopilot. A plan drafted by AI still needs a human owner who has read it, checked it and practiced it.


Example: A continuity manager uses an AI assistant to turn 25 recorded business impact interviews into draft BIA entries in an afternoon, a job that used to take two weeks. Every entry is then reviewed with the process owner, and three of them turn out to have the wrong recovery priority. The time saved goes into that review.
  The Technical View:
These trends share one theme: dependencies. Operational resilience, third-party risk, cyber scenarios and integrated programs all depend on knowing what each critical service relies on and keeping that map current. Organizations that already maintain good application and dependency data in a CMDB or architecture tool have a head start. Those still relying on interviews and spreadsheets will find each of these trends harder.
  Leadership Translation:
Business continuity is no longer a compliance binder. It is how the organization proves to customers, regulators and its own board that the most important services will keep running, or come back within an agreed time, whatever the cause of the disruption. The question leadership should ask is not "do we have a plan?" but "when did we last prove it works, and what did we fix?"


What This Means for Your Program
You do not need to chase every trend at once. A practical starting point for most organizations looks like this:


Pick your most important services: Start with three to five. Map what each depends on, including suppliers.
Plan for the long technology outage: Ask each critical process owner what they would do if systems were unavailable for a week. Write down the answer and test it.
Look hard at your critical suppliers: For each, know what happens if they are down and what your fallback is.
Keep evidence: Every exercise produces an after-action report, tracked issues and closure dates.
Join up the teams: Run at least one exercise a year that brings business continuity, IT recovery, cyber and communications into the same room.


The Bottom Line
The plan on the shelf is not enough anymore, but the reason is not that planning has stopped mattering. It matters more. Disruptions are broader, longer and more connected than the old model assumed, and the people who rely on your organization (customers, regulators, employees) expect proof that you can keep going.
