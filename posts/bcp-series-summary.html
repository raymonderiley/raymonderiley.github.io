Business Continuity, Start to Finish: The Whole Series on One Page


If You only read one post in this four part series, read this one!
Over the last four posts, during the month of June, I have taken business continuity planning (BCP) apart piece by piece: where the discipline is heading, who does what, what belongs in a plan and how to test it. Each post goes fairly deep on its own topic. This one puts the pieces back together.

It is written to stand on its own. If you are a leader who wants the big picture, an application or process owner who has just been handed a continuity plan, or a practitioner looking for a checklist, you should be able to read this page and know what a modern business continuity program looks like and where to start. If you want more detail on any part, the full posts are linked along the way.

Here is the series in one sentence: disruptions have the potential to be broader and longer than they used to be, so the business needs its own plan (not just IT's), that plan has to be usable by stressed people, it has to contain the right answers and stay current, and none of it is real until it has been practiced.

Part 1: Why Business Continuity Is Changing
The classic continuity plan was a binder written for physical disasters: a fire, a flood, a power outage, a building you could not enter. Those risks are still real, but the shape of disruption has changed in ways the binder never anticipated. The full post, Business Continuity in 2026, walks through ten trends. The ones that matter most:


Operational resilience replaces department plans: Instead of a plan per department, organizations identify their most important services (taking a payment, shipping an order, admitting a patient), map everything each depends on and set a limit on how long it can be disrupted. Regulators in financial services now expect this, and other sectors are following.
Evidence over documents: Auditors, regulators and large customers want to see test results, findings and fixes, not just a signed plan.
Third parties and concentration risk: Critical processes run on cloud platforms, SaaS tools and service providers you do not control. When many organizations depend on the same few providers, one outage becomes everyone else's outage too.
Cyber as the default scenario: Ransomware can take every site and system down at once, for days or potentially even weeks. Every critical process needs an answer to "what do we do if the technology is gone for a long time?"
Manual workarounds are back: Not for everything, but the few processes that cannot stop need a practiced fallback that works without systems.
People, overlapping crises and climate: Key person risk, staff concentrated in one place, several disruptions happening at once and more frequent extreme weather all push plans to cover longer and messier events.
Integrated programs and AI: Continuity, IT recovery, cyber response and crisis management are converging into one program with shared data, and AI is starting to help draft plans, analyze impacts and design exercises (with a human still accountable for every answer).

The common thread is dependencies. Almost every trend comes down to knowing what each critical service relies on and keeping that picture current.


Part 2: Who Does What, and Why Usability Wins
Disaster recovery (DR) and business continuity are often confused because they activate on the same bad day. They answer different questions. The full post, DR vs. BCP: Who Does What, covers this in detail. The short version:


Disaster recovery: Restores the technology. Owned by IT, carried out by engineers and application teams, measured by recovery time and recovery point objectives.
Business continuity: Keeps the business delivering its most important services while the disruption lasts, whatever the cause. Owned by the business, carried out by team leads and frontline staff, measured by whether customers are still being served within the tolerable disruption limit.
Crisis management: Decides and communicates. Executives, communications, legal and HR set direction and speak for the organization.

The three must share one set of priorities and one picture of timing. The most dangerous gap is a business plan that assumes a system is back in hours when the tested recovery time, especially after a cyberattack, is days and potentially longer. I covered why those recovery times differ in We Have Two Different RTOs?.

But the most important lesson from Part 2 has nothing to do with org charts. A plan is only useful if people can find it and follow it under pressure. Stress makes people read less, skip steps and wait for permission. A good plan is built for that person:


Findable: Stored where the disruption cannot reach it (offline, out-of-band or printed for critical teams), with a one-page quick reference in people's hands.
Followable: Actions first, background last. Short numbered steps, plain language, roles instead of names, and clear decision points that tell people what they are allowed to do.
Familiar: People in assigned roles are trained and have practiced the first steps, so the real event is not the first time they see the plan.


Part 3: What Goes Into the Plan
A business continuity plan is a set of answers to the questions people will ask on a bad day. The full post, What Goes Into a Business Continuity Plan, describes each component and how to keep it current. In summary, it starts with two foundations:


Business impact analysis (BIA): What is critical, what happens if it stops and how long it can be down before the impact becomes unacceptable.
Risk assessment: What could cause a disruption, how likely and severe each threat is and which impacts they share. Plan for impacts (loss of a site, systems, people or a supplier), not for every individual cause.

Then the plan itself answers these questions:


Does this plan apply? Activation triggers with clear thresholds, and who has authority to activate.
What do we do first? A one-page first-actions checklist for the first 15 to 60 minutes.
Who is in charge of what? Response roles, who fills each one today and at least one backup for each.
What comes back first? Recovery priorities and target times from the BIA, matching IT's restore order.
How do we keep going? Recovery strategies and workarounds: alternate sites, remote work, moving work elsewhere, manual procedures, backup suppliers or a reduced level of service.
What does it rely on? Dependencies on applications, data, facilities, suppliers and other teams.
Who do we call and what do we need? Key contacts and resources.
What do we tell people? A communications plan with audiences, channels, approvers, templates and backup channels.
How do we protect the data? Backup and access for vital records, and how manual records are reconciled afterward.
How do we get back to normal? A controlled return, including catching up on backlogs.

Dependencies deserve special attention because that is where plans usually break. A business process depends on everything its applications depend on, plus people, buildings, suppliers and other teams. Record those links in the same application inventory or CMDB that IT already uses, then compare: if a process can only tolerate four hours of disruption but its key application takes two days to recover, that gap needs a workaround, an investment or a formal risk acceptance.


  Keeping It Current:
A plan starts going stale the day it is approved. Two mechanisms keep it alive: 

    
  First a regular rhythm: monthly contact checks (automated from HR/IT and the application CMDB where possible), quarterly owner reviews, twice-yearly communications tests, an annual BIA refresh, tabletop and formal re-approval, and a larger integrated exercise every one to two years.

    
  Second change-driven updates: when people change roles, applications change, suppliers change or the organization reorganizes, the affected plans are flagged for review, ideally automatically.
  Clear Ownership:
The business owns its plans. The governance behind this is that the continuity program office sets the standard, runs the calendar, facilitates and tracks actions. IT provides accurate recovery times and dependencies. HR, facilities, procurement and communications feed in changes. An executive sponsor decides on risk acceptances, and internal audit checks that it all works as described.


Part 4: Testing Turns a Plan Into a Capability
People fall to the level of their training. The full post, BCP Best Practices: Testing Your Plan Before Reality Does, covers each type of test in detail and includes a copyable exercise template. The key ideas:


Climb the ladder: Start with a plan walkthrough (one to two hours), move to tabletop exercises (two to four hours of facilitated discussion with injects), then functional simulations (half a day to a full day where teams act out their roles in real time) and finally full-scale drills (four hours to several days, with real relocation, real recovery and real work).
Set objectives: Two to four specific, measurable objectives per exercise, so you know what to watch and how to judge success.
Make it safe to fail: Test the plan, not the people. Hidden gaps are the most expensive kind.
Use realistic scenarios and include the hand-offs: The best findings appear where business, IT, communications and suppliers meet.
Rotate participants: Let backups lead. The primary may not be there on the real day.
Test without disrupting the business: Most tests touch no production systems. For live drills, choose quiet periods, get approvals, protect customers and agree on stop conditions in advance.
Close the loop: A hot wash (debrief) right after, an after-action report within a week, every finding tracked to an owner and due date, plan updates within 30 to 45 days (or potentially less if a critical application!) and an emphasis on a re-test of what failed.


  The Technical View:
Combine business and technical tests whenever possible. When IT restores a system during its DR test, have the business validate the restored service and run its workarounds in parallel. One joint test finds the hand-off gaps that two separate tests will miss, and uses less of everyone's time.
  Leadership Translation:
Each step up the testing ladder costs more and proves more. Tabletops prove people understand the plan. Simulations prove they can perform it. Full-scale drills prove the organization can recover. Expect all three over a multi-year cycle, with findings and closed actions reported every time.


Putting It All Together: A Starting Roadmap
If you are building or rebuilding a continuity program, here is a practical order of work that draws on all four posts.


First 30 days: Pick three to five of your most important services. Get an executive sponsor. Agree on who owns each service's plan.
Days 30 to 90: Run a BIA for those services, map their dependencies (including suppliers and applications) and compare the business tolerance for disruption against tested IT recovery times. Document the gaps.
Days 90 to 180: Write or rewrite the plans so they are findable and followable: one-page first actions, clear triggers, named roles with backups, recovery priorities, workarounds and a communications plan. Store copies where a disruption cannot reach them.
Days 180 to 270: Run a tabletop exercise for each plan. Track every finding. Update the plans.
Days 270 to 365: Run your first simulation, ideally joined with an IT recovery test. Set up the regular review rhythm and change-driven triggers so the plans stay current. Report results and open gaps to leadership.
Year two and beyond: Expand to more services, add supplier and cyber scenarios, plan a full-scale drill and keep improving based on what each exercise teaches.


Five Questions to Ask About Any Continuity Program
Whether you are a leader, an auditor or a plan owner, these five questions cut through the paperwork quickly:


Do we know our most important services and what each one depends on, including suppliers?
Do the business's recovery expectations match what IT has actually tested, including after a cyberattack?
Could the person on shift tonight find the plan and follow the first page without help?
When was each critical plan last exercised, and were the findings closed?
What keeps the plan current when people, systems, suppliers or the organization change?

If the answers are clear and backed by evidence, the program is in good shape. If they are vague, you know where to start.


The Bottom Line
Business continuity is not a binder and it is not just an IT problem. It is the organization's ability to keep doing what matters most for its customers when something goes wrong, for as long as it takes to recover.

The trends show why it matters more now: disruptions are broader, longer, more connected and more dependent on others. The roles show that the business must own its plan while working in step with IT and crisis leadership. The components show what a plan must answer and how to keep those answers true. And testing shows whether any of it works.

The organizations that come through disruptions well are not the ones with the thickest plans. They are the ones whose people know where the plan is, know what to do first and have practiced it before they needed it. That is the goal of the whole series, and it is achievable one service, one plan and one exercise at a time.
