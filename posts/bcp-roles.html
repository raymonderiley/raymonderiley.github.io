DR vs. BCP: Who Does What, and Why the Plan Has to Work at 3 a.m.


Two Plans, One Bad Day
Ask ten people in a company what the difference is between disaster recovery and business continuity, and you will probably get ten answers. Some will say they are the same thing. Some will say DR is "the IT one." A few will shrug and say, "Isn't that the binder in the risk team's office?"

The confusion is understandable. Both plans activate on the same bad day. Both talk about recovery times. Both get tested, audited and occasionally forgotten. But they answer different questions, they are owned by different people and, most importantly, they are used by different people under pressure.

This is the second post in my business continuity series. In the first post, I looked at the trends reshaping BCP. Here, I will briefly sort out who does what between DR and BCP, and then spend most of the time on the point that matters more than any org chart: a business continuity plan is only useful if people can find it and follow it when everything is going wrong.


The Short Version: Different Questions
Disaster recovery asks: How do we restore the technology? Which systems come back first, from where, and how fast? It is measured in recovery time and recovery point objectives for applications and infrastructure.
Business continuity asks: How does the business keep delivering its most important services while the disruption lasts, whatever caused it? It covers people, processes, facilities, suppliers and communications, as well as technology.
Crisis management asks: Who is in charge, what decisions need to be made and what do we tell employees, customers, regulators and the public?

A simple way to put it: DR brings the systems back. BCP keeps the business running until they are back, and also when the problem is not technology at all, such as a building closure, a supplier failure or a large number of staff out at once.

How the Roles Differ
The roles are not just different job titles. They differ in who owns the plan, who executes it and what success looks like.


  Who Owns the Plan:
DR: Usually owned by IT, with a DR program manager or technology resilience lead coordinating, and application and infrastructure owners responsible for their own recovery plans and runbooks.
BCP: Owned by the business. Each business unit or service has a plan owner, typically a department head or process owner, with a continuity manager or program office setting the standard, facilitating and keeping the program honest.
  Who Executes It:
DR: Engineers, database administrators, cloud and network teams, backup administrators and security teams. Most of the work happens on consoles, in runbooks and on bridge calls.
BCP: Team leads, supervisors and frontline staff. Customer service reps switching to a manual process, warehouse staff using printed pick lists, finance moving to an alternate approval path, HR managing staff relocation. Most of the work happens on the floor, often without the systems they normally use.
  What Success Looks Like:
DR: The system is restored within its RTO, with data loss within its RPO, and validated by the application owner.
BCP: Customers keep being served, at an acceptable level, within the maximum tolerable disruption, and the business can return to normal operations in a controlled way.

There are overlaps. The business impact analysis (BIA) feeds both plans. Recovery priorities have to agree, or IT will restore systems in an order the business does not need. And both sides need the same incident command structure so decisions are not made twice or not at all.


  The Technical View:
The hand-off between DR and BCP is where most gaps hide. If the BCP assumes a system is back in four hours and the tested cyber recovery time is three days, the business plan has a hole of almost three days that nobody owns. Recovery priorities, recovery times and dependencies should come from one shared source, ideally the application inventory or CMDB, so both plans are built on the same facts.
  Leadership Translation:
DR is a technology responsibility. BCP is a business responsibility. Leadership's job is to make sure both exist, that they agree with each other and that each has a named owner who will be held to it. When a disruption hits, customers do not care which plan failed. They only see that the service stopped.


Why "Can They Find It and Follow It?" Matters Most
Here is the uncomfortable truth about business continuity plans: many of them are well written, approved, audited and completely unusable in a real event.

They are 80 pages long. They are stored on a file share that is unavailable when the network is down. They are written in policy language. The contact list was correct two reorganizations ago. The person who wrote them left last year. And the people who actually need them, the shift supervisor at 3 a.m. or the team lead covering for a manager on vacation, have never seen them.

A plan that cannot be found or followed under pressure has the same value as no plan at all, but with a false sense of security attached.

Stress changes how people think. Under pressure, people read less, skip steps, fall back on habit and look for someone to tell them what to do. A good continuity plan is designed for that person, not for an auditor reading it calmly at a desk.


Making a Plan People Can Find
Store it where the disruption will not reach it: If the plan lives only on the corporate network, a cyber event or network outage takes it away. Keep current copies in at least one location outside the main environment, such as an offline copy, a secured out-of-band platform or printed copies for the most critical teams.
Make it easy to locate: One known place, one name. "The continuity plan is in the red folder at each team lead's desk and on the emergency site" beats "it is somewhere on the risk team's file share."
Put the first page in people's hands: Wallet cards, a one-page quick reference at each workstation, a pinned link in the team chat channel and a copy on the team lead's phone. The full plan can be long. The part people need first must be short and close at hand.
Test that people can find it: In your next exercise, before anything else, ask a team member to pull up the plan without help. Time it. The result is often surprising.


Making a Plan People Can Follow
Lead with actions, not background: The first page should answer: How do I know this plan applies? What do I do in the first 30 minutes? Who do I call? Policy, scope and history go at the back.
Use checklists and short steps: One action per line, written as an instruction. "Call the facilities hotline" not "Facilities should be notified as appropriate."
Write for the role, not the person: Name the role ("shift supervisor," "payments team lead") and keep a separate, maintained list of who fills that role today, with backups. People change jobs. Roles stay.
Include the decision points: Spell out when to escalate, who can approve a workaround and what thresholds trigger the next step. People freeze when they are not sure they are allowed to act.
Keep the language plain: Avoid acronyms that only the continuity team knows. If a new hire would not understand a sentence, rewrite it.
Show where to get help: Every page should make it obvious who to contact if the plan does not fit the situation.


A Quick Before and After
Here is the same instruction, written two ways.

BEFORE:
  In the event of a disruption impacting the primary order management platform,
  the Order Operations function shall, in consultation with relevant stakeholders,
  implement appropriate manual contingency procedures as outlined in Appendix F.

AFTER:
  Order system down for more than 15 minutes?
  1. Tell your shift lead (or call the Ops on-call line: see contact card).
  2. Shift lead: open the red Manual Orders kit at the supervisor desk.
  3. Take orders on Form MO-1. Write the time on every form.
  4. Every hour, send a count of paper orders to the Ops on-call line.
  5. When the system is back, do NOT re-enter orders until the shift lead says go.

Both say roughly the same thing. Only one can be followed by a tired person, at 3 a.m., with customers waiting.


Practice Turns a Document Into a Habit
Even the best-written plan will not be followed if people meet it for the first time during a real event. The goal of training and exercises is to make the first steps familiar enough that people do them without hesitation.


Train people in their role: Each person named in the plan should know what their role does in the first hour, where the plan is and who they report to. A 15-minute walkthrough once or twice a year goes a long way.
Exercise the hand-offs: Run scenarios where the business continuity team, IT recovery team and crisis team must work together, so everyone sees how their part connects.
Rotate who leads: Let backups and deputies run part of an exercise. The primary will not always be available on the real day.
Fix what confused people: Every question asked during an exercise ("where is the form?", "who approves this?") is a change the plan needs.

The Bottom Line
Disaster recovery and business continuity are partners, not rivals. DR restores the technology. BCP keeps the business delivering for its customers while that happens, and when the problem is not technology at all. Each needs its own owners, and both need to agree on priorities and timing.

But no matter who owns it, a business continuity plan earns its value in one moment: when a stressed person under pressure needs to know what to do next. If they can find it, understand it and follow it, the plan works. If they cannot, it does not matter how good it looked during the audit.
