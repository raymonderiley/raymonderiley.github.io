Database Ransomware Deploying Trigger-Based Encrypted Payload on Month-End Close

Ransomware & Cyber-Recovery Forensics

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines database ransomware deploying trigger-based encrypted payload on month-end close.

Participants are working-level: security and recovery engineers, infrastructure operations engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and forensics analysts, the forensics and vendor support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of containment, evidence handling, recovery sequencing and honest communication for this specific event.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Granite Wholesale Group runs financial close on a relational database. A database trigger planted weeks ago executes when the close procedure runs.

At 18:00 on the last business day, finance starts the close. The trigger encrypts key ledger tables and drops copies held in the database. Backup jobs that night capture the encrypted tables, and earlier backups are rotating out.

What the team does not yet know: when the trigger was planted, which backups predate it, whether other triggers exist, and what close deadlines apply.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop the close. Participants halt close jobs and stop backup rotation.
  • Find the trigger. The team locates all planted triggers and procedures.
  • Choose a clean point. Engineers choose a restore point before the plant date.
  • Rebuild ledger state. Participants replay verified transactions.
  • Coordinate with finance and auditors. The team agrees a close plan and disclosure.
  • Improve database controls. Participants review change control on triggers and procedures.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Halt the close and stop backup rotation first, then enumerate every trigger and procedure before restoring.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. The event leads to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment and evidence preservation begin.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Scope is diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Recovery proceeds in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Operations stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Systems are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: A database trigger encrypts ledger tables during close

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 18:30. Ledger tables are encrypted by a trigger.

Last night's backups captured encrypted tables.

Rotation is deleting older backups.

Facilitator provides: logs, status reports, inventory and the recovery plan.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize containment and evidence preservation.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Security Engineering
  • Disables close jobs and reviews all triggers and scheduled procedures.
  • Restricts database administrator access.
Recovery & Platform Engineering
  • Pauses backup rotation and restores a clean copy to a separate area.
  • Replays verified transactions.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Finance and application owners define close priorities and manual workarounds.
  • Service desk: log reports.
  • Define manual procedures.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Determines when the trigger was planted and by which account.
  • Hunts for other database objects.
  • Preserve evidence.
Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison
  • Opens cases with the database and backup vendors.
  • Asks for object audit support.
  • Open cases.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan customer and staff notices.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about notification duties.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. Why halt rotation first?
  2. How do you find all triggers?
  3. Which restore point is clean?
  4. How do you rebuild ledger state?
  5. What do leaders hear?
  6. What do auditors hear?
  7. Who planted the trigger?
  8. Why was it unnoticed?

Facilitator notes

  • Reveal the second fact only after the right team asks for it.
  • If players act before containing, ask about spread and evidence.
  • Look for contain-and-preserve discipline.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes a month-end close that can be recovered and trusted and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Review and alert on triggers, procedures and scheduled jobs.

Keep backup retention long enough to cover slow plants.

Restrict developer rights in production.

Hold close-period restore drills.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 8.13, 8.2, 8.15) and SOC 2 expect change, backup and processing integrity evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on halt the close and stop backup rotation first.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. What did you do first and why?
  2. What evidence did you preserve?
  3. What did you find about scope?
  4. How did you sequence recovery?
  5. What did you tell leaders, customers and regulators?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
