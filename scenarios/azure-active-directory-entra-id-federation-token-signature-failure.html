Azure Active Directory / Entra ID Federation Token Signature Failure

Azure Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a federation outage in which applications and partner services reject Entra ID tokens or SAML assertions because signing keys rolled over and relying parties still trust the old certificate. Users can authenticate to Entra ID but fail at the application, producing intermittent and application-specific login errors.

Participants are working-level: identity and Entra ID engineers, cloud platform and application engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and monitoring staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of signing key rollover, metadata refresh, SAML certificate expiry, conditional access and break-glass accounts.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Seaborne Logistics federates 140 enterprise applications with Entra ID using OpenID Connect and SAML. A set of SAML apps use certificates managed manually by application owners, and some third-party SaaS and a partner portal cache metadata for days. Break-glass cloud-only accounts exist, protected by hardware keys and excluded from conditional access.

At 07:20 on Monday, a SAML signing certificate for a custom enterprise application expires and a key rollover also occurs for the tenant-wide OIDC signing key. Applications that cache the JWKS endpoint for 24 hours reject new tokens with 'signature validation failed'. SAML apps with the expired certificate return 'AADSTS' errors. Within 30 minutes, the service desk is flooded: logins fail for the finance ERP, the dispatch portal and a partner supply chain portal.

What the team does not yet know: the certificate owners for six apps left the company, one partner portal needs the partner to import new metadata, conditional access policies require a compliant device that prevents some engineers from reaching the admin portal, and the break-glass account password was changed during a recent audit but never retested.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize token and signature failures. Participants interpret sign-in logs, error codes and application logs to separate authentication success from application rejection.
  • Restore trust safely. The team refreshes metadata, extends or rolls certificates and handles cached keys without weakening validation.
  • Protect administrative access. Engineers verify break-glass accounts and conditional access exclusions.
  • Prioritize applications. Participants rank apps by business impact and coordinate with owners and partners.
  • Avoid insecure shortcuts. The team refuses to disable signature validation or bypass MFA, and documents any exception.
  • Improve identity lifecycle. Participants review certificate inventories, ownership, monitoring and break-glass testing.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Do not weaken token validation or MFA as a workaround; any proposal to do so is recorded and escalated.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Login failures and signature errors lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Admin access is verified and priorities set.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. The affected apps and causes are identified.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A refresh and certificate plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Metadata is refreshed and certificates rolled app by app.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Logins recover and caches expire.

T+06:00 — Validation. Application and partner access are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Logins fail across apps with signature errors

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:00. Dispatch cannot log in and the finance ERP shows 'invalid token signature'. A partner supply chain portal rejects SAML assertions. Entra ID sign-in logs show many successful authentications, so users believe they are signed in.

The identity engineer finds six apps with expired SAML certificates and several apps caching old signing keys. Conditional access blocks some engineers from the portal because their devices are non-compliant. The break-glass account's password was changed last month and has not been tested.

Azure Service Health shows no incident. A vendor's SaaS says metadata must be re-imported by the customer. Someone suggests turning off signature validation on the dispatch app.

Facilitator provides: the sign-in log summary, certificate expiry list, caching behavior notes and the conditional access summary.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Reject disabling signature validation and record it.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Identity & Entra ID Engineering
  • Identify affected apps and error codes.
  • Verify break-glass access.
  • Plan certificate and metadata refresh.
Cloud Platform & Application Engineering
  • Refresh caches and restart components.
  • Update trust settings for dispatch first.
  • Work with vendors.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank apps and find owners.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Contact partners.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Look for abuse.
  • List missing alerts.
Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about rollover.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform operations.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan manual dispatch.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about certificate ownership.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell authentication success from application rejection?
  2. What do the errors show about key rollover versus certificate expiry?
  3. Why is turning off signature validation unacceptable and what do you do instead?
  4. How do you reach the admin portal with conditional access blocking you?
  5. What happens when break-glass credentials are untested?
  6. How do you handle apps with no known owner?
  7. What do partners need?
  8. What should operations hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the untested break-glass until T1 attempts to use it.
  • If players disable validation, ask who approves and how it is recorded.
  • Look for the metadata refresh approach.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

  • Assesses contractual impact.
  • Captures evidence of break-glass approvals.
  • Logs findings on certificate ownership.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Treat signing material as inventoried assets with owners and expiry alerts, preferring automated rollover and metadata-based trust over manual certificate pinning.

Design relying parties to refresh keys: short JWKS cache lifetimes, metadata auto-refresh and overlapping certificates during rollover.

Keep break-glass real: at least two cloud-only accounts, hardware keys in separate locations, excluded from conditional access with alerts, and tested quarterly.

Never trade away validation: have pre-approved safe alternatives and a rule that disabling validation requires formal risk acceptance.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.17, 8.24) and SOC 2 expect certificate inventory, tested emergency access and change control evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on break-glass readiness and refusing insecure shortcuts.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.
