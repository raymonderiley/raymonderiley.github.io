Data Center HVAC Total Failure in High-Ambient Summer Conditions

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines how an organization responds when the cooling plant of its primary data center fails completely during an extreme heat event. With outdoor temperatures above 43 C (110 F), the loss of chilled water and computer room air handlers means room temperature can climb at several degrees per minute, forcing rapid decisions about load shedding, graceful shutdown order and the risk of hardware damage.

The exercise is designed for working-level staff: facilities and critical-environment engineers, server, virtualization, storage and network operators, application owners, the service desk, physical security, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. It tests whether the team understands thermal runway rates, who can order shutdowns, and whether the shutdown and restart sequences are documented and rehearsed.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours. The twelve-inject library supports repeat sessions with different injects. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Prairie State Health Network operates a 600 kW Tier III-style data center with 84 racks, a raised floor with hot-aisle containment, and a chilled water plant with N+1 chillers feeding 10 computer room air handlers (CRAHs). The facility hosts the electronic medical record, imaging archive, claims system and the virtualization cluster for regional clinics. A secondary data center 300 km away holds replicated data but has capacity for only about 60 percent of production workloads.

On a Saturday afternoon at 15:05, with an outdoor temperature of 44 C, a pump controller fault and a tripped breaker take both chilled water loops offline. The remaining chiller cannot start because of a high-pressure lockout, and two CRAH fans trip on overload. The building management system (BMS) shows supply air temperature rising from 18 C to 26 C in six minutes, with hot aisle temperatures above 40 C at some racks. Several storage arrays and the top-of-rack switches begin to log thermal warnings.

What the team does not yet know: the chiller lockout requires an on-site manufacturer reset that has a four-hour vendor response, the emergency cooling contract has not been renewed, the generator is healthy but irrelevant to this fault, thermal sensors on the third row are miscalibrated, and the shutdown order in the runbook is five years old and does not include the new imaging archive.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize and communicate thermal runaway risk. Participants interpret BMS and rack-level sensor data, calculate the time to critical temperature and agree on thresholds that trigger load shedding and shutdowns.
  • Execute controlled load shedding and shutdown. The team determines the order for stopping non-critical workloads, powering down storage and hosts, and protecting data on arrays, using documented dependency and priority lists.
  • Restore or substitute cooling. Facilities identify options such as chiller reset, temporary spot coolers, opening doors and floor tiles, chilled water interconnects, rental chiller connections, and the cost and time of each.
  • Decide on failover to the secondary data center. Participants evaluate capacity at the secondary site, replication currency, and clinical priorities for what can be run there.
  • Protect people and safety. The team addresses heat risk for on-site personnel, safe access to hot aisles and fire-detection false alarms from high temperatures.
  • Control restart and prevent secondary failures. The team sequences restart to avoid power inrush, verifies component health, and plans the hardware damage assessment.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Use real temperatures, rates and run times from your own facility where known. Where the team has no data, record it as a gap.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. BMS alarms, thermal warnings and facility staff reports lead to incident declaration.

T+00:20 — Thermal assessment. Runaway rate is estimated and the time to critical temperature is calculated.

T+00:45 — Load shedding decision. Non-critical workloads are shed and shutdown order is initiated.

T+01:30 — Cooling restoration options. Chiller reset, emergency cooling and vendor responses are evaluated.

T+02:30 — Failover decision. Secondary data center failover for clinical systems is decided.

T+04:00 — Stabilization. Temperatures are controlled, partial cooling restored, access and safety checked.

T+05:30 — Restart planning and validation. Staged restart, hardware health checks and application validation.

T+07:00 — Return to service and close-out. Services are released in priority order and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Both chilled water loops fail on a 44 C afternoon

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 15:09 on a Saturday. The BMS displays a red alarm: chilled water supply loops A and B are offline. Supply air temperature has climbed from 18 C to 26 C in six minutes and hot-aisle sensors in rows 5 to 8 read 38 to 42 C. Two of the ten CRAHs show fan overload trips. The facilities on-call technician reports that Chiller 2 is in a high-pressure lockout and Chiller 1 was already down for planned maintenance.

The monitoring system shows thermal warnings from three storage arrays and a core switch. The SAN array in row 6 shows a 'degraded cooling' state. The virtualization cluster reports CPU throttling on four hosts. The imaging archive (a new system) shows an elevated disk temperature alarm.

Outdoor ambient is 44 C. The generator is online and healthy but not relevant to this fault. The emergency cooling vendor's phone line goes to voicemail. The only on-site facilities technician says the reset requires the manufacturer's service tool.

Facilitator provides: the BMS trend graph, the rack-level sensor table described verbally, the runbook shutdown list dated five years ago, and the vendor contact sheet.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead, safety lead and scribe.
  • Set thermal thresholds for load shedding and shutdown, and the next update time at T+00:20.
  • Decide whether to alert the secondary data center and clinic managers now.
Facilities & Critical Environment Engineering
  • Report what the plant is doing and which resets are possible without the vendor.
  • Estimate the temperature rise per minute and the time to 40 C inlet.
  • Start deploying temporary cooling measures and contact all vendors; state the real response times.
Server & Virtualization Operations
  • Begin non-critical workload shutdown and consolidate the rest away from hot rows.
  • Check CPU throttling and HA behavior so that load does not cascade.
  • Identify hosts to power off first.
Storage & Network Operations
  • Check storage arrays' thermal states and the process for a safe shutdown.
  • Identify the imaging archive's tolerance and its shutdown procedure, which is not in the runbook.
  • Confirm that replication to the secondary site is current.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Application owners rank their systems and state the maximum tolerable downtime for clinical use.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message to clinics.
  • Clinical owners: confirm the downtime workflow.
Security & Physical Access
  • Set limits on personnel entering hot aisles and arrange for water and cooling breaks.
  • Watch for false fire alarms and suppression interactions.
  • Prepare access for the vendor.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Open the BC plan for facility loss and the communications log.
  • Ask for the documented clinical RTO and RPO.
  • Draft the clinic notification.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record and capture the detection time.
  • Ask whether the emergency cooling contract and the chiller maintenance schedule were approved by risk.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How fast will the room reach 40 C inlet, and how did you calculate it? What if the sensors in row 3 are wrong?
  2. What temperature thresholds does your runbook define for load shedding and shutdown, and who has authority to order a shutdown?
  3. In which order do you power down, and what is missing from the five-year-old list?
  4. Which temporary cooling measures are realistic within 60 minutes and which are not?
  5. Should the team shed load or evacuate to the secondary data center first, and what limits the secondary's capacity?
  6. What are the safety considerations for staff in a 42 C hot aisle?
  7. How would you handle the imaging archive that is not in the runbook?
  8. What must clinics be told in the first 20 minutes?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not volunteer that the emergency cooling contract lapsed unless the team calls the vendor.
  • If the team delays shutdown to keep clinical systems up, ask what hardware damage and data loss risk they accept and who signs.
  • Watch for conflict between facilities and IT priorities. Note who has the authority to override.
  • Time-box discussion to about 30 minutes because thermal events are fast.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, owns severity and the decision to order shutdowns and fail over to the secondary data center.
  • Sets decision thresholds, for example: at 35 C inlet begin load shedding; at 40 C inlet shut down storage and hosts in priority order.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence during thermal runaway, then 30 minutes; keeps a decision log.
  • Authorizes emergency spend for rental cooling and vendor dispatch.

Facilities & Critical Environment Engineering

  • Diagnoses the chilled water failure: pump controller fault, breaker trip and chiller high-pressure lockout, and states what can be reset locally versus by the vendor.
  • Estimates temperature rise using BMS trends and supplies a time-to-critical estimate to the incident commander.
  • Deploys temporary measures: portable spot coolers, opening containment doors, removing blanking panels and doors on racks, fan assistance.
  • Calls the emergency cooling contractor and chiller manufacturer and confirms the true response times.

Server & Virtualization Operations

  • Identifies workloads by priority tier and begins controlled shutdown or migration of non-critical virtual machines.
  • Uses DRS or manual vMotion to consolidate and evacuate hot hosts where possible.
  • Powers down hosts in the order agreed, with databases quiesced first.
  • Prepares restart sequencing and power inrush controls.

Storage & Network Operations

  • Powers down or quiesces storage arrays in the documented order, confirming write cache is destaged before power off.
  • Identifies switches and routers in hot rows and plans their shutdown, protecting management and out-of-band paths.
  • Verifies replication status to the secondary data center and its capacity.
  • Prepares network changes and DNS for failover.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank their systems by clinical and business impact and give the maximum tolerable downtime.
  • Clinical systems owners prepare downtime procedures and paper-based workflows for clinics.
  • The service desk issues the incident script and tracks tickets and call volume.
  • Owners prepare validation tests for post-restart verification.

Security & Physical Access

  • Confirms the safety of personnel working in hot aisles and sets limits on time in hot areas.
  • Manages physical access and handles false fire or smoke alarms and any suppression impact.
  • Ensures doors and containment changes do not create security exposure.
  • Supports emergency vendor access and escorts.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates the business continuity and clinical downtime plans and coordinates with clinic managers.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership and clinics with approved wording.
  • Coordinates patient safety communications with the clinical leadership.
  • Tracks the outage against RTO and RPO for each clinical system.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified in the incident process (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 controls 5.24-5.28; 7.5 physical environmental controls; SOC 2 availability criteria).
  • Evaluates regulatory obligations for health data availability and any notification triggers.
  • Captures evidence for emergency spend approvals and decisions to shut down.
  • Logs findings on cooling redundancy, the lapsed emergency contract and the stale shutdown order.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Cooling is a dependency of every system. Treat chilled water, CRAH capacity and power to the cooling plant as part of the service resilience design, with N+1 verified by test, not by drawing.

Know the thermal runaway time for each row. Measure it, publish it and calibrate sensors. The time between loss of cooling and critical temperature drives every other decision and may be only minutes in high-density rooms.

Define thresholds and authority in advance: inlet temperatures that trigger load shedding, storage shutdown and host shutdown, with named people who may order them without a meeting.

Keep the shutdown and restart order current. Include new systems such as the imaging archive, document dependencies and ensure the sequence is tested at least annually.

Maintain real emergency cooling arrangements: current contract, pre-engineered connection points, power for spot coolers and a heat-wave readiness check. Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and SOC 2 will ask for maintenance records, testing and capacity planning.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on the elapsed time between loss of cooling and each decision, and the quality of the thermal estimates.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team produce a time-to-critical-temperature estimate, and how was it derived?
  2. What are your documented inlet temperature thresholds, and who has the authority to act on them?
  3. How current is the shutdown order and which systems were missing?
  4. Which temporary cooling options are pre-engineered, and which were improvised?
  5. What did you learn about the emergency cooling contract and the vendor's response times?
  6. How were storage arrays shut down, and did anyone verify cache destage?
  7. What was the plan for the secondary data center capacity and how was the priority list built?
  8. What safety limits were placed on staff in hot aisles?
  9. How would you have handled false fire alarms and suppression interactions?
  10. Did the sensor data conflict, and how did it affect decisions?
  11. What caused the restart delay and how would you sequence power inrush?
  12. How would you detect and document hardware damage caused by heat?
  13. How accurate were communications to clinics, and what workarounds did they have?
  14. What evidence is needed for insurance and audit that the scribe did not capture?
  15. What would you change before the next heat event?
