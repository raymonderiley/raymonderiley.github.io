Azure App Service Plan Regional Capacity Exhaustion

Azure Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an Azure App Service regional capacity event in which a regional scale-out request fails because the target scale unit has no free capacity, autoscale cannot add instances during a demand spike, and the web applications on the plan slow down and return errors while a failover region is not warmed up.

Participants are working-level: cloud platform and SRE engineers, application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of plan SKUs, autoscale limits, scale units, zone redundancy, Front Door failover and capacity reservation.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Marlowe Ticketing sells event tickets through web and API apps hosted on a Premium v3 App Service plan in a single region, currently at 6 instances with autoscale up to 20. A secondary region has a plan scaled to 1 instance with deployment slots that are not kept in sync.

At 10:00 on Friday, a high-demand concert goes on sale. CPU on the plan hits 90 percent and autoscale tries to add instances. Scale-out operations fail with 'insufficient capacity for the requested SKU in this scale unit'. Response times rise from 300 ms to 9 seconds, HTTP 503 rates reach 25 percent and the queue of waiting buyers grows. Azure Service Health shows an advisory for constrained Premium v3 capacity in the region.

What the team does not yet know: a different SKU (Premium v2) may have capacity, moving the plan to a new scale unit requires a restart and may change outbound IPs that partners allow-list, the secondary region cannot scale quickly because it uses a different SKU family, and database connection limits will be hit if traffic is sent to the secondary region without warming.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a capacity failure. Participants read autoscale logs, activity logs and Service Health to separate load from platform capacity limits.
  • Protect the sale. The team applies throttling, a virtual waiting room and caching to reduce load while capacity is sought.
  • Find capacity safely. Engineers evaluate alternative SKUs, scale units, zones and regions with their side effects such as outbound IP changes.
  • Fail over deliberately. Participants decide how to shift traffic to the secondary region with database and cache readiness.
  • Communicate with buyers and partners. The team provides accurate updates and handles allow-list impacts.
  • Improve capacity resilience. Participants review reserved capacity, warm standby, load testing and zone redundancy.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • State the side effects of every scaling or move action, including IP changes and restarts.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Errors and failed scale-out lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Load is reduced and the capacity cause is confirmed.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Capacity options are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A scale, move or failover plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. The plan is executed with traffic shifting.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Latency and errors stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Applications and partners are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Scale-out fails during the on-sale

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 10:15. The web plan CPU is at 92 percent with 6 instances. Autoscale logs show repeated failures to add instances with an insufficient capacity message. HTTP 503 rate is 25 percent.

The platform engineer finds a Service Health advisory for constrained Premium v3 capacity in the region. The secondary region plan has 1 instance, a different SKU family and out-of-sync slots.

Switching SKU would restart the app and change outbound IPs that three partners allow-list. The database is near its connection limit.

Facilitator provides: autoscale logs, metrics, Service Health text, plan configuration and a partner list.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize load shedding.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering (App Service)
  • Evaluate SKU and zone options.
  • Assess IP change effects.
  • Prepare secondary scale-up.
Application Engineering
  • Enable caching and rate limits.
  • Check database connections.
  • Plan warm-up.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank flows and decide on a waiting room.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Contact partners.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Watch database and cache.
  • Preserve logs.
Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about capacity.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Coordinate partners.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about payment obligations.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you know this is platform capacity and not an app problem?
  2. What are your options for adding capacity and their side effects?
  3. What do you protect first during the on-sale?
  4. Do you fail over, and what must be ready first?
  5. How do outbound IP changes affect partners?
  6. How does the database limit shape your choices?
  7. What do buyers need to hear?
  8. Why was the secondary region not ready?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the IP allow-list issue until T1 evaluates a SKU change.
  • If players fail over without warming, ask about database limits.
  • Look for load shedding first.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes the on-sale and payment completion and sets approval rules for failover.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes load shedding and region shifting.
  • Decides on communications.

Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering (App Service)

  • Reads autoscale and activity logs and checks Service Health.
  • Evaluates alternate SKUs, scale units and zone redundancy.
  • Plans plan migration and outbound IP effects.
  • Prepares the secondary region scale-up.

Application Engineering

  • Enables caching, queueing and rate limits at the app and Front Door.
  • Checks database connections and pooling.
  • Prepares warm-up for the secondary region.
  • Validates purchase flows.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank flows such as checkout over browsing and decide on a waiting room.
  • The service desk logs buyer contacts.
  • Owners coordinate partner allow-lists.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of CPU, instance count, 503 rates and latency.
  • Monitors database and cache saturation.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Adds capacity alerts.

Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on capacity constraints.
  • Asks about SKU and scale unit availability.
  • Requests capacity reservation options.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for ticketing and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to buyers.
  • Coordinates with partners.
  • Tracks impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.6 capacity management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses consumer and payment obligations.
  • Captures evidence of decisions.
  • Logs findings on capacity planning.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Treat platform capacity as a risk, not a guarantee: for events with known peaks, pre-scale, use zone redundancy and ask Microsoft about capacity reservation options.

Keep a warm secondary: matching SKU family, synced slots, replicated data and tested Front Door failover with cache and connection limits sized for real load.

Protect the platform with load shedding patterns such as waiting rooms, caching, rate limits and graceful degradation.

Reduce side effects of scaling and moves by using NAT gateways with stable outbound IPs and documented partner allow-lists.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6) and SOC 2 expect capacity planning, load testing and tested failover evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on warm standby and capacity planning.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How long did it take to see capacity as the cause?
  2. What did you protect first?
  3. How did you evaluate SKU options?
  4. How did outbound IPs affect partners?
  5. How did you decide on failover?
  6. How did the database limit shape actions?
  7. How did you handle payment rate limits?
  8. How was the waiting room decided?
  9. How accurate were updates to buyers?
  10. What did Microsoft support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How was peak load tested?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alerts are missing?
  15. What would you change in capacity design?
