Generative AI Phishing Kit Creating Hyper-Realistic Targeted Extortion Emails in Seconds

AI Failure & Autonomous Agent Disruption

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a generative AI phishing kit producing individualized extortion emails that cite real personal and work details for thousands of staff in minutes, threatening exposure unless they pay or click.

Participants are working-level: email security engineers, collaboration platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations analysts, the AI platform and cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of mass mail triage, staff welfare, data source discovery and safe reporting.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Eastvale Group Insurance has 9,000 employees and a mail gateway with phishing detection. A report button exists but gets limited use. Staff data such as names, titles and some personal details appears in public sources and in earlier data breach dumps.

At 08:30 on Monday, 3,100 staff receive emails that cite their home address, manager's name and a recent project, claiming compromising material will be released unless they pay or open a link. The messages vary in wording. Calls to the service desk surge. Some staff click, and a few report fear for family safety.

What the team does not yet know: how the attackers compiled the details, whether any corporate data is in play, how many clicked or paid, and whether physical safety threats are credible.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize mass targeted extortion. Participants identify individualized messages at scale.
  • Contain mail and clicks. The team purges messages and blocks links.
  • Support staff. Participants provide clear guidance and welfare support.
  • Assess data exposure. The team determines whether corporate data was used.
  • Handle safety concerns. Participants route credible threats to security and authorities.
  • Improve resilience. Participants review reporting, detection and awareness.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Purge the messages and block the links before analyzing, and give staff a simple instruction: do not pay, do not reply, report.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Mass extortion emails lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Messages purged and staff guidance issued.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Data source and exposure are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A support and control plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Fixes and support proceed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Staff confidence stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Controls are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes staff who stay calm and report when extorted by name and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Email Security Engineering

  • Purges messages and blocks links and senders.
  • Reviews click logs.
  • Plans detection tuning.
  • Plans reporting improvements.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Collaboration Platform Engineering

  • Resets credentials for those who entered them.
  • Checks endpoints of clickers.
  • Validates mail rules.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and HR define staff support and escalation.
  • The service desk uses a script and logs calls.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison

  • Asks for takedowns.

  • Activates continuity for insurance security operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 6.3 awareness, 8.7 malware protection, 5.24-5.28 incident management, 6.8 event reporting; SOC 2 security).
  • Assesses staff protection and phishing resilience.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Tell staff early and plainly: do not pay, do not reply, report it.

Use behavioral and language-model-based detection that is not based on templates.

Provide welfare support and a route for credible threats to security and police.

Reduce staff data exposure in directories and public profiles where possible.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (6.3, 8.7, 5.24, 6.8) and SOC 2 expect awareness and reporting evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on staff-first and safe reporting.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. What did you tell staff first?
  2. What did you purge first?
  3. How did you treat safety fears?
  4. How did you find the data source?
  5. How did you support staff?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
