AWS Transit Gateway Route Table Storm and Packet Drop

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a routing instability on an AWS Transit Gateway that connects dozens of VPCs, VPN tunnels and Direct Connect attachments. Route propagation and static route changes loop between attachments, route tables flap and packets are dropped or blackholed, producing intermittent failures that are hard to localize.

Participants are working-level: cloud network engineers, platform and SRE engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests the team's understanding of Transit Gateway route tables, propagation, attachments and quotas, and its discipline in making network changes under pressure.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Redwood Logistics operates a hub-and-spoke network in one AWS region. A Transit Gateway connects 41 VPCs across production, staging and shared services, two Site-to-Site VPN connections to branch offices and one Direct Connect gateway to the data center. Separate route tables segment production from non-production, and route propagation is enabled from the Direct Connect and VPN attachments. Network changes are made by two teams using Terraform and, occasionally, the console.

At 09:15 on Tuesday, a network engineer adds a static route for a new partner network to the shared services route table and enables propagation from a newly attached VPC whose CIDR overlaps a block already advertised from the data center over BGP. Within minutes, the Direct Connect gateway begins advertising a summarized prefix learned back from the Transit Gateway, and route table entries alternate between attachments. Traffic between production VPCs and the data center drops intermittently, VPN tunnels flap as BGP sessions reset, and CloudWatch shows rising PacketDropCountBlackhole and PacketDropCountNoRoute on several attachments.

What the team does not yet know: the overlapping CIDR was introduced by a team that used a default range, the static route has a lower priority than the propagated route in some tables and higher in others, route table association changes in the last hour were made through the console and are not in Terraform, and the Transit Gateway route table route limit is close to being reached.

Exercise Objectives

  • Localize a routing problem on a shared hub. Participants use Transit Gateway flow logs, CloudWatch metrics, route table search and Network Manager to find which attachment and route is responsible.
  • Understand propagation, association and precedence. The team explains route table associations, propagation, static route precedence and why overlapping CIDRs cause instability.
  • Make safe changes under pressure. Engineers remove or isolate the offending attachment and routes with a clear sequence, a peer check and a rollback.
  • Protect critical traffic. Participants identify which business flows rely on the data center path and arrange alternatives.
  • Control the blast radius. The team stops other network changes, limits automation and avoids cascading BGP resets.
  • Improve network governance. Participants examine CIDR planning, change control, drift between console and code and route quota monitoring.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • State which route table, attachment or CIDR you mean in every statement; vague references such as 'the network' are not accepted.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Intermittent drops and BGP flaps lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Network changes are frozen and recent changes are identified.

T+01:15 — Localization. The overlapping CIDR and conflicting routes are found.

T+02:00 — Remediation plan. Attachment isolation and route removal are planned and peer-reviewed.

T+03:00 — Execution. The offending attachment and routes are removed in sequence.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. BGP sessions and route tables are verified as stable.

T+06:00 — Validation. Application flows and cross-VPC traffic are tested.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Freeze is lifted and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Intermittent drops between production and the data center

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:40. The service desk reports that the warehouse management system times out intermittently when reaching the data center database. VPN branch users report dropped sessions. Monitoring shows PacketDropCountBlackhole rising on the production VPC attachment and two Direct Connect BGP sessions in the 'flapping' state.

A network engineer opens the shared services route table and sees two entries for the same prefix with different attachments, and one of them points to the newly attached VPC. The new VPC's CIDR overlaps the data center block 10.40.0.0/16. The route table has 9,800 routes against a limit of 10,000.

The AWS Health Dashboard shows no events. The Terraform pipeline shows a pending apply that would add further static routes. Console changes in the last hour are not reflected in code.

Facilitator provides: the route table extracts, the attachment metric summary, the BGP session log and the change history.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Freeze all network changes and lock the pipeline.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Network Engineering
  • Identify the conflicting routes and the attachment responsible.
  • Explain precedence between static and propagated routes.
  • Describe how to isolate the attachment safely.
Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering
  • Lock the pipeline and check for drift.
  • Test connectivity from representative instances.
  • Identify critical flows needing workarounds.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • List dependent services and symptoms.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Prepare degraded modes.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the drop and BGP dashboard.
  • Check Direct Connect status.
  • List missing alarms.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case with attachment IDs.
  • Ask about the route limit.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform depots and leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask who approved the new attachment.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell blackholed packets from a missing route using metrics and flow logs?
  2. Which entry wins in the route table and why?
  3. What is the safest way to remove the new attachment's effect?
  4. Why did BGP sessions flap and could your fix make it worse?
  5. Which business flows must be protected first?
  6. How close are you to the route limit and what happens at the limit?
  7. Why are console changes not in Terraform and who approved them?
  8. What should depots and leadership be told?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the overlapping CIDR until the network engineer searches the route tables.
  • If players want to delete routes immediately, ask about the order and the effect on BGP.
  • Watch for the pipeline apply being allowed to run.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Inject #2: Isolating the attachment drops the partner link

Time frame T+00:20 · Variant A

Situation

The network engineer disables propagation from the new attachment. Drops decrease, but the partner's connection, which relies on the same attachment, goes down and the partner's operations team calls the service desk.

The partner contract requires 99.9 percent availability.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Weigh the partner outage against stabilizing the core network.
Cloud Network Engineering
  • Determine whether the partner route can be restored through a non-overlapping design.
  • Confirm propagation is disabled and routes cleared.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Notify the partner relationship owner.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Assess contract exposure.

Discussion questions

  1. Is a partner outage acceptable to stabilize the core?
  2. How could the partner be restored without overlap?
  3. Who talks to the partner?
  4. What does the contract require?

Facilitator notes

  • Tests prioritization.
  • Look for a concrete alternative such as NAT or a new range.
  • Connect to CIDR governance.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes data center connectivity for production and sets a change freeze across network teams.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes removal of the new attachment even if its owners object.
  • Decides on escalation to the AWS technical account manager.

Cloud Network Engineering

  • Searches the Transit Gateway route tables for overlapping and conflicting routes, and reads attachment-level metrics.
  • Reviews Transit Gateway flow logs and Network Manager route analyzer results for failing paths.
  • Plans removal of the offending propagation or static route and the sequence of BGP changes.
  • Checks route counts against the quota.

Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering

  • Identifies impacted services and tests connectivity from representative instances.
  • Checks Terraform state for drift and locks the pipeline to prevent further applies.
  • Prepares workarounds such as temporary VPC peering or VPN paths for critical flows.
  • Supports validation after remediation.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners list services depending on the data center path and their failure symptoms.
  • The service desk groups tickets by symptom and issues guidance.
  • Owners prepare retries, queueing or degraded modes.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of packet drops, BGP state and bytes by attachment.
  • Confirms flow logs are enabled and reachable.
  • Checks the AWS Health Dashboard and Direct Connect status.
  • Creates alarms for blackhole drops and route count thresholds.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a high-severity case and shares the Transit Gateway and attachment IDs and timeline.
  • Asks about propagation behavior, route limits and BGP reset impacts.
  • Requests confirmation that no underlying service event exists.
  • Records the guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates the continuity plan for logistics operations and informs depots of degraded system access.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership.
  • Coordinates manual dispatch procedures if needed.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.20 network security, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates contractual implications.
  • Captures evidence of the change that triggered the incident.
  • Logs findings on CIDR governance and console changes.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Treat the Transit Gateway as shared critical infrastructure. Use separate route tables for segmentation, restrict who can change them and require peer review and automated overlap checks before any attachment or propagation change.

Run central IP address management. Overlapping CIDRs should be impossible to create, with pipeline checks that fail when a new VPC or route overlaps an existing prefix.

Instrument the hub. Enable Transit Gateway flow logs, alarm on blackhole and no-route drops, BGP session state and route counts against quotas, and keep reachability analysis tools ready.

Eliminate drift. Route changes should be made through code, with detection of console changes and a documented emergency path that is reconciled afterward.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.20, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect network segmentation evidence, change approval records and monitoring of critical connectivity.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on how quickly the overlap was found and on control of network changes.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How long did it take to find the conflicting route and what led you to it?
  2. Who understood route table precedence and was that knowledge shared?
  3. How did you decide the removal sequence?
  4. How effective was the change freeze and who violated it?
  5. What flow logs and metrics were available and what was missing?
  6. How did you handle the partner link and other business priorities?
  7. What happened with the route quota?
  8. What did the pipeline do and who could stop it?
  9. How did the team deal with BGP flapping?
  10. How accurate were updates to depots and leadership?
  11. What did AWS support add?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. How will overlapping CIDRs be prevented?
  14. What access or permissions were missing?
  15. What would you change in network change control?
