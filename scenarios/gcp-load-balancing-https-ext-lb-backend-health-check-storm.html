GCP Load Balancing HTTPS Ext. LB Backend Health Check Storm

Google Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a global external HTTPS load balancer whose health check configuration marks healthy backends unhealthy after an endpoint change, causing backends to flap, capacity to collapse and a storm of health check traffic to overload instances.

Participants are working-level: network engineers, platform and SRE engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Google Cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of health check intervals, thresholds, firewall rules for probes, failover behavior, connection draining and fail-open.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Willow Retail serves its storefront through a global external HTTPS load balancer with managed instance groups in two regions. Health checks hit /healthz every 5 seconds with a 2-failure threshold. The /healthz endpoint checks the database and a payment dependency.

At 18:00 on Friday, the payment provider has a short slowdown. The /healthz endpoint starts timing out because it waits on the payment dependency. Backends in region A are marked unhealthy, traffic shifts to region B, which becomes overloaded and also fails its health checks. Probe traffic from many Google front ends adds load. The load balancer returns 502 for most requests. Black Friday traffic is three times normal.

What the team does not yet know: the health endpoint is too deep and creates a failure cascade, a firewall rule change earlier reduced probe allowance causing some probes to be dropped, fail-open behavior can send traffic to all backends if all are unhealthy, and connection draining is configured for 5 minutes which delays recovery.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize health check cascade. Participants distinguish backend failure from health check failure.
  • Stabilize traffic. The team changes health checks and thresholds to stop flapping.
  • Protect backends. Engineers relieve load with caching, rate limits and instance scaling.
  • Fix probe design. Participants make health endpoints shallow and separate readiness from dependencies.
  • Handle payment dependency. The team degrades payments gracefully.
  • Improve load balancing resilience. Participants review thresholds, probe firewall rules, capacity headroom and failover testing.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Do not remove health checks entirely; change them in controlled steps and verify.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. 502 errors and flapping backends lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Health checks are stabilized and load relieved.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Probe design and dependency impact are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A stabilization and communication plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Changes are applied in steps.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Traffic and backends stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Payments and storefront are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Situation

It is 18:20. The storefront returns 502 for most requests. Both regions show backends flapping between healthy and unhealthy.

The network engineer finds /healthz checks the payment dependency, which slowed down. Region B overloaded after traffic shifted.

  • Plan fail-open.

  • Contact provider.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell backend failure from health check failure?
  2. Why does a deep health check cause a cascade?
  3. What is the safest first change?

  5. How do you degrade payments gracefully?

  7. What do customers hear during the sale?

  • Look for stepwise changes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes keeping healthy backends in service and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Network Engineering (Load Balancing)

  • Reads backend health, probe logs and load balancer metrics.
  • Relaxes thresholds and intervals in controlled steps.
  • Fixes probe firewall rules.
  • Plans fail-open and draining settings.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Platform & SRE Engineering

  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Keep health checks shallow and fast, and separate liveness from dependency readiness so dependencies degrade features rather than remove backends.

Set thresholds and intervals to tolerate short blips and test probe firewall rules.

Keep regional headroom so a region loss does not cascade.

Load-test failover.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.14) and SOC 2 expect capacity and resilience evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on health check depth and regional headroom.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.
