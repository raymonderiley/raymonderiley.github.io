Enterprise Backup Server Catalog Corruption During Incremental Run

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines the loss of the backup catalog (the index that records what was backed up, where, and when) on the enterprise backup server during a nightly incremental run. The backup data may still exist on disk, tape and cloud tiers, but without the catalog nobody can say which media hold which restore point. The exercise focuses on the silent nature of this failure: nightly jobs appear to succeed until someone needs to restore.

Participants are working-level: backup administrators, storage and infrastructure operators, database and application owners, security analysts, the service desk, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The scenario deliberately overlaps with a request to restore a production database, so the team discovers the problem under pressure.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours. The twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Lakeshore Manufacturing protects 900 servers with a single enterprise backup platform. A primary backup server in the main data center holds the catalog (a database of about 2.4 TB), and writes to a deduplicating disk appliance (96 TB) and a tape library with 600 slots, with weekly tapes vaulted offsite. A secondary backup server exists but receives catalog copies only once a week. The nightly catalog backup is configured to run at the end of the incremental window.

At 01:40, during the incremental run, the catalog database's underlying volume experiences a storage latency spike and an unclean shutdown of the catalog service. On restart, the catalog database fails consistency checks and reports corrupted index pages. The scheduled catalog backup that night completes 'successfully' but backs up the corrupted state. At 07:30 an application team asks for a restore of a manufacturing execution database to the state of three days ago after a bad schema change, and the restore wizard returns no recovery points.

What the team does not yet know: the last good catalog copy is 6 days old, tape media labels exist but the barcode-to-job mapping lived only in the catalog, the deduplication appliance holds the data but needs the catalog to rehydrate it, and a security analyst has just flagged unusual administrative logins to the backup server during the night.

Exercise Objectives

  • Detect and scope backup catalog failure. Participants recognize that catalog corruption can be silent, determine what data can still be restored, and establish which restore points are lost to the index.
  • Recover the catalog. The team evaluates options: restore the catalog from the weekly copy, rebuild it by scanning disk and tape media, use catalog database repair utilities, or engage the vendor, and estimates time and completeness of each.
  • Serve urgent restores during the outage. The team identifies a prioritized restore queue and alternative recovery paths, such as database-native backups, snapshots and replicas.
  • Assess the security angle. Security and backup staff determine whether the corruption is accidental or a deliberate attack on backups, and how that changes recovery and containment.
  • Protect ongoing backup coverage. Participants decide how to run new backups safely while the catalog is unreliable and how to prevent protection gaps.
  • Improve resilience design. The team examines catalog protection frequency, secondary server practices and restore testing.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Use the capabilities of your own backup platform when answering. Where the platform's behavior is unknown, record it as a question for the vendor.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. The failed restore and catalog errors lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Scope and triage. Catalog state, last good copy, media inventory and urgent restores are identified.

T+01:15 — Security assessment. Administrative login anomalies are investigated.

T+02:00 — Options evaluation. Catalog restore, rebuild and vendor repair are compared.

T+03:00 — Recovery path decision. A recovery path is chosen and urgent restores are prioritized.

T+04:30 — Execution. Catalog recovery proceeds and urgent restores are performed.

T+06:00 — Validation. Restore tests, backup runs and catalog consistency checks are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Protection is confirmed, communications issued and incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Restore request finds no recovery points

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 07:42. The MES database owner requests a restore to the state of three days ago after a schema change broke the production line interface. The backup administrator opens the restore console and finds no recovery points listed for the server. The job history shows nightly incremental jobs reporting success.

On the backup server, the catalog service log shows an unclean shutdown at 01:41 and index page checksum errors on restart. The backup platform's health dashboard still shows green because it checks only that the service is running. The catalog backup that ran at 03:00 reported success.

The last good catalog copy on the secondary server is six days old. The tape library shows 311 tapes in slots, but the media list in the console is empty. The deduplication appliance is reachable and reports 71 percent utilization.

Security notes that the backup server logs show interactive administrative logins from a jump host at 01:20 and 01:35, which the backup team says they did not perform.

Facilitator provides: the catalog error log excerpt, the media inventory screenshot, the job history, and the login records.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident, set severity and name leads for catalog recovery, urgent restores and security.
  • Decide whether to treat as a security incident now.
  • Set the next update at T+00:30.
Backup & Recovery Engineering
  • Explain what is lost with catalog corruption and what is still on media.
  • Identify the options and time for each: restore from weekly copy, rebuild, vendor repair.
  • Stop all jobs that write to the catalog until the state is known.
Storage & Infrastructure Operations
  • Check the catalog volume event and appliance health.
  • Confirm the dedupe data is intact.
  • Arrange retrieval of offsite tapes if needed.
Database & Application Owners
  • MES owner: define the required recovery point and the production impact.
  • Check for native backups or storage snapshots as alternatives.
  • Other owners: list their urgent restore needs.
Security Operations
  • Review logins from the jump host and find out who used them.
  • Check for commands that expire images or delete catalog entries.
  • Recommend isolation or continued operation.
Service Desk & Operations Center
  • Log all restore requests and communicate delays.
  • Monitor job status and flag affected jobs.
  • Prepare the holding message.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Identify processes at risk without restore capability.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RPO and RTO of MES.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask whether catalog restore has ever been tested.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What does a corrupted catalog mean for the data on disk and tape, and what can you still do?
  2. Why did the platform show green and the jobs succeed, and what monitoring should detect catalog health?
  3. What are your options for the catalog and what is the time and completeness of each?
  4. How do you serve the MES restore while the catalog is unusable?
  5. Is this an accident or an attack, and what evidence will tell you?
  6. Should new backups run tonight? What is the risk either way?
  7. What is the weekly catalog copy gap and who accepted that risk?
  8. What do you tell the MES team and the plant manager right now?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the jump host logins until the security team asks about administrative access.
  • If the team assumes the dedupe data is lost, ask what evidence supports that. The data is intact; the index is the problem.
  • Observe whether teams think of storage snapshots and database-native backups as alternatives.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, sets priorities between urgent restores and catalog recovery, and approves the chosen path.
  • Runs a 30-minute cadence, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Decides whether to treat the incident as potentially security-related and invoke the security incident process.
  • Approves vendor engagement and emergency support spend.

Backup & Recovery Engineering

  • Assesses catalog database integrity and identifies the last good copy, its age and what gaps it leaves.
  • Evaluates catalog rebuild from media: time to scan disk and tape, and what metadata might be missing.
  • Contacts the vendor for repair tools and escalation, and tests a restore of the catalog on a secondary server.
  • Plans how new backups will run without risking more damage.

Storage & Infrastructure Operations

  • Checks storage latency events at the time of the catalog volume issue and verifies the dedupe appliance health.
  • Confirms that deduplicated data is intact and identifies any appliance-level snapshots or replication that can serve recovery.
  • Prepares extra capacity for catalog restore and temporary work areas.
  • Locates offsite tapes and arranges retrieval.

Database & Application Owners

  • The manufacturing execution database owner states the exact restore point needed and tries alternatives such as database-native backups, replicas and storage snapshots.
  • Other application owners list their top restore needs and current backup coverage.
  • Owners assess which systems have no alternative recovery paths.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Security Operations

  • Reviews administrative logins, command history and changes to the backup server during the night, and checks for deletion commands or expiry changes.
  • Determines whether the catalog corruption can be attributed to an intentional act.
  • Checks credentials and MFA for backup administrators and rotates if needed.
  • Advises whether to isolate the backup server and preserve evidence.

Service Desk & Operations Center

  • The service desk tracks incoming restore requests and communicates the delay to requesters.
  • The operations center monitors backup job status and flags jobs that run against the damaged catalog.
  • Maintains a prioritized restore request list.
  • Escalates urgent restores to the incident commander.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Identifies the business processes at risk without restore capability, especially manufacturing execution and finance.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership and plant managers.
  • Coordinates manual process or manual rework if data loss occurs.
  • Tracks time against RTO and RPO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 controls 5.24-5.28; 8.13 backup; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates contractual and regulatory obligations for data protection.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and vendor interactions.
  • Logs findings on catalog backup frequency, restore testing and monitoring of backup health.

Resilience Architect Guidance

A backup is only as good as its index. Protect the catalog like a tier-zero database: frequent copies (at least every few hours), copies on separate storage and a different server, and regular restore tests of the catalog itself.

Monitor backup health, not just job status. Alert on catalog integrity checks, empty restore point lists, media inventory counts and the age of the last verified restore.

Know your alternative recovery paths: database-native backups, storage snapshots, replicas and immutable copies. Make sure they are documented and tested so that a failure of the backup platform is not a failure of recovery.

Treat the backup system as a target. Use unique, MFA-protected administrative accounts, restrict jump host access, keep immutable copies and assume an attacker will try to destroy backups.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (control 8.13) and SOC 2 expect documented backup policies, restore test evidence and monitoring of backup success; a green dashboard is not evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on how the failure was detected, how long it was invisible, and the quality of alternatives to the catalog.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How long was the catalog corrupted before someone noticed, and what would have detected it sooner?
  2. Why did the dashboard and the catalog backup report success?
  3. What is the age and location of your last good catalog copy, and who accepted that gap?
  4. What options did you compare for recovery and which did you choose?
  5. How did you serve the urgent MES restore and what alternative paths exist?
  6. What did the security review find and how did it affect the plan?
  7. How would you identify tape and dedupe data without the catalog?
  8. How did you protect against further catalog damage during the incident?
  9. What were the criteria to resume backups?
  10. How accurate were communications to the plant and MES team?
  11. How often are restores tested and what do the results show?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. Which accounts have administrative access to backups and how are they protected?
  14. What access or vendor support was missing?
  15. What will change in catalog protection after this exercise?
