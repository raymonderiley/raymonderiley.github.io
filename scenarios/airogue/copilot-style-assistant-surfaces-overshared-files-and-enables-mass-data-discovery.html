Copilot-Style Assistant Surfaces Overshared Files and Enables Mass Data Discovery

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Copilot-style assistant in Microsoft 365 and Entra ID that faithfully surfaces whatever a user can technically access. Years of oversharing mean ordinary questions now reveal confidential files, and a curious employee, or an attacker with a stolen account, can run mass discovery in minutes.

Participants are the working-level incident team: Microsoft 365 and identity administrators, information governance and records staff, security analysts, business data owners, privacy and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests permission hygiene and detection of misuse that uses legitimate access.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Microsoft 365 & Entra ID. The organization rolled out an AI assistant integrated with email, files, chat and calendars. The assistant respects existing permissions. SharePoint sites and OneDrive folders have accumulated 'everyone' and 'company-wide' sharing links, and many sensitive libraries lack labels. Entra ID sign-in logs and Purview audit record assistant interactions at summary level.

Scenario

Overton Group launched the assistant to all staff after a short pilot with a small team. The pilot did not test sensitive libraries. A permissions report from last year flagged 120,000 items shared with everyone, but it was not remediated.

On Tuesday, an employee asks the assistant to 'summarize anything about layoffs next quarter' and receives text from a leadership planning file that was shared by a company-wide link. Word spreads, and employees try prompts like 'show salary bands' and 'list customer contract terms'.

On Thursday, an alert shows a contractor account signing in from an unfamiliar country and issuing 600 assistant prompts in an hour, retrieving summaries from legal, finance and engineering libraries. The assistant's usage dashboard says 'adoption high, satisfaction 4.6 of 5'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Oversharing existed before the assistant.
  • The assistant summarizes content users can technically open.
  • A contractor account is used from an unfamiliar location.
  • Audit logs record prompts and referenced files at limited detail.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which files were surfaced to whom.
  • Whether the contractor account is compromised.
  • How much sensitive content has company-wide links.
  • Whether summaries were copied elsewhere.

Exercise Objectives

  • Limit discovery now. The team restricts assistant access to sensitive libraries, disables the compromised account and tightens sharing links. Evidence of success: Sensitive libraries are excluded from the assistant and the account is secured within 30 minutes.
  • Determine exposure. Security and governance review audit data to identify prompts, referenced files and recipients. Evidence of success: An exposure report by user and library exists with gaps stated.
  • Remediate oversharing at scale. Owners and administrators remove company-wide links and apply labels in priority order. Evidence of success: A remediation plan with counts and owners exists.
  • Handle sensitive disclosures. HR, legal and business owners decide how to handle disclosure of confidential plans. Evidence of success: A decision record with actions exists.
  • Communicate carefully. Communications and the leadership liaison give accurate statements without repeating content. Evidence of success: Messages are consistent and approved.
  • Redesign assistant readiness. Participants set readiness gates, label policies and monitoring. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Prompts reveal sensitive content. Decision: declare and restrict discovery. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Employees quote sensitive plans; Contractor account anomalies; Mass prompts.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: exclude libraries, disable the account and remove broad links. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Links regenerate; Other tenants affected; Cached summaries.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: reconstruct prompts and files and assess account compromise. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Audit detail limited; Account used before; Copies elsewhere.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: prioritize remediation and handle disclosure. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Volume too large; Owners unknown; Disagreement on severity.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: remove links, label content and notify as decided. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Owners resist; Work disrupted; Notice delays.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor prompts and sharing. Escalate to leadership or change course if: New oversharing; Prompt probing; Agent attempts resume.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: test with persona prompts and review labels. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Gaps in labeling; Evidence gaps; New exposure found.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: define assistant exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early re-enablement; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The assistant surfaces overshared files and enables mass data discovery

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:45 on Thursday. A security alert shows a contractor account signing in from an unfamiliar country and issuing 600 assistant prompts in an hour. Prompts asked about legal disputes, salary bands and customer contract terms.

Earlier this week, employees found that the assistant summarized a leadership planning file shared by a company-wide link. A permissions report from last year flagged 120,000 overshared items, still open.

The commander must decide how to limit discovery quickly, how to assess what was surfaced and how to start remediating oversharing at scale.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a Microsoft 365 lead, an identity lead, a governance lead and a security lead.
  • Authorize restricting assistant access to sensitive libraries and disabling the account.
  • Set a 20-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Restrict the assistant's scope and export prompt and reference logs.
  • Identify prompts matching discovery patterns.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Remove company-wide links on top-risk libraries and apply labels.
  • Reset the contractor account.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Investigate the contractor sign-ins and the prompts.
  • Preserve logs.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify the most sensitive libraries and their owners.
  • Plan remediation.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Assess personal data in surfaced files.
  • Prepare notice analysis.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and capture readiness approvals.
  • Review labeling and sharing policy.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare staff and leader messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for market-sensitive duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Open a Microsoft support case.
  • Ask about audit detail for assistant interactions.

Discussion questions

  1. Why restrict discovery before remediating the sharing?
  2. How do you tell curiosity from attack?
  3. How do you prioritize 120,000 overshared items?
  4. How do you handle disclosure of confidential plans?
  5. What do you tell staff?
  6. Why is the assistant exposing a pre-existing problem and not creating one?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the contractor sign-in until INF reviews identity logs.
  • If players blame the assistant only, ask about permissions hygiene.
  • Look for readiness thinking.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the productivity assistant stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows which sources the assistant reads and how prompts are logged.
  • Provides scope and prompt logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the productivity assistant: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Removes links, applies labels and secures accounts.
  • Owns tenant sharing settings.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Investigates the contractor account and prompt patterns.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Prioritizes libraries and owners.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Assesses personal data in surfaced files.
  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.15, 5.12, 8.3, 8.16) and SOC 2 confidentiality).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with Microsoft support.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Fix permissions before launching an assistant

Treat assistant rollout as a data governance project: remediate company-wide links, label sensitive content and test with realistic prompts before widening access.

Exclude the most sensitive sources

Keep board, legal, HR and merger content out of general assistants by label or site policy.

Monitor discovery behavior

Alert on unusual prompt volume, topics such as salaries or legal disputes and sign-ins from new locations.

Limit sharing defaults

Disable company-wide and anyone links by default, and require owner approval for broad sharing.

Keep audit detail

Retain assistant interaction logs with referenced files for investigations, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 access and classification controls and SOC 2 confidentiality criteria.

Run readiness tests

Use red team prompts against pilot data and track what they retrieve.

Own the remediation backlog

Assign owners to overshared libraries and report progress to leaders monthly.

Prepare the people response

Agree how the organization responds when confidential plans are exposed, so staff hear a consistent message.

Sequence the rollout by data readiness

Roll the assistant out in waves tied to measured readiness: percent of sensitive libraries labeled, company-wide links removed and owners assigned. A wave does not start until its readiness numbers are met. Publish the numbers so leaders can see that a faster launch means more exposure, and so teams know remediation is the path to access.

Make usage visible to owners

Give library owners a monthly report of which of their files were referenced by the assistant, by how many users and from which groups. Owners who see their confidential folders being summarized for hundreds of people tend to fix permissions faster than any policy reminder can achieve, and the report doubles as evidence of ongoing governance.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the productivity assistant was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team connect the prompts to oversharing?
  2. How did you limit discovery?
  3. How did you assess what was surfaced?
  4. How did you prioritize remediation?
  5. What did staff hear?
  6. What readiness controls do you now want?
  7. What would a safe pilot have tested?
  8. Which content should assistants never read?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
