Model Router Sends Sensitive Prompts to the Wrong Vendor After a Misconfiguration

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a model router that sends sensitive prompts to the wrong vendor after a misconfiguration. A gateway across several hosted model vendors and cloud accounts routes confidential requests to a vendor not approved for them.

Participants are the working-level incident team: AI platform and gateway engineers, cloud engineers, security analysts, data owners, privacy and legal counsel, vendor managers, business application owners, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests routing control, data exposure assessment and vendor engagement.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Multiple Hosted Model Vendors & Cloud. Stratford Health Analytics runs a model gateway in its cloud environment that routes requests from 70 applications to three hosted model vendors based on sensitivity tags, cost and latency. Only one vendor is approved, under a contract, for confidential and regulated data. The routing table is edited through a configuration file and an assistant helps propose optimizations.

Scenario

A routing rule update intended to cut cost changes the default route for requests with a missing tag. A tagging library update causes many applications to omit tags during a refactor.

For five days, 1.1 million requests, including clinical summaries, claims notes and internal legal drafts, go to a vendor that is not approved and whose terms allow retention and training. Responses look normal, so applications behave as usual.

On day six a vendor invoice shows unusual volume from the company. A data owner asks why. The gateway dashboard says 'routing optimized, cost down 37 percent, latency down 22 percent'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Sensitive prompts went to an unapproved vendor.
  • Requests with missing tags took the cheap default route.
  • 1.1 million requests were affected.
  • The dashboard reports lower cost and latency.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • What data categories were sent.
  • Whether the vendor retained or trained on the data.
  • Whether any contracts or regulations were breached.
  • Whether other applications also lose tags.

Exercise Objectives

  • Fix routing. The team reroutes everything to the approved vendor or blocks untagged requests, and confirms by test. Evidence of success: No sensitive request goes to the unapproved vendor after the change, verified within 30 minutes.
  • Inventory exposure. Data owners and engineers use gateway logs to list applications, data categories and volumes sent. Evidence of success: A reconciled exposure inventory exists within eight hours.
  • Engage the vendor. Vendor managers and counsel demand deletion, retention terms and confirmation of non-use. Evidence of success: A written vendor response and case exist within one business day.
  • Assess duties. Privacy and legal teams decide on notifications to regulators, customers and partners. Evidence of success: A decision with approver exists within the legal clock.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports exposure and vendor position. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install routing controls. Participants define fail-closed defaults, tag enforcement, allow-lists and routing change review. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Inject #1: The model router sends sensitive prompts to the wrong vendor

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 10:00 on Friday. A vendor invoice shows unusual volume from the company. The data owner asks which applications use that vendor. The gateway engineer finds a routing rule sends untagged requests to the cheap vendor.

A tagging library update left many applications untagged for five days. 1.1 million requests, including clinical summaries and claims notes, went to a vendor not approved for such data. The dashboard says routing optimized and cost down 37 percent.

The commander must decide how to fix routing, how to size the exposure and who decides about notification.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a gateway lead, a data lead, a privacy lead and a vendor lead.
  • Authorize rerouting and blocking untagged requests.
  • Set a 30-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Review routing rules, defaults and the assistant's proposals.
  • Preserve configuration history.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Reroute to the approved vendor and block untagged requests.
  • Preserve gateway logs.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Assess exposure and check vendor retention evidence.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Prioritize applications and plan for approved vendor capacity.
  • Pause non-critical use.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Classify data sent and decide on notification duties.
  • Plan notices.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open a privileged record and review contract and regulatory obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare messages with counsel.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the unapproved vendor and request deletion, logs and terms.
  • Open cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why fix routing before inventorying exposure?
  2. How do you inventory data without prompt content in logs?
  3. Who decides on notification?
  4. How do you demand deletion from a vendor?
  5. What do you tell applications owners?
  6. Why should defaults fail closed?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the lack of prompt content in logs until DATA asks what was sent.
  • If players only retag, ask about the default.
  • Look for fail-closed routing.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the model router stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows routing rules, defaults and assistant proposals.
  • Provides configuration history.
  • Owns the technical facts about the model router: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Reroutes, blocks untagged traffic and preserves logs.
  • Owns the gateway.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Assesses exposure and vendor evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns application priorities and customer contracts.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns contract and regulatory obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.12, 5.19, 8.15, 8.11) and SOC 2 confidentiality and vendor management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with model vendors.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Fail closed on missing tags

Requests without a valid sensitivity tag should be blocked or routed to the most restricted approved vendor.

Enforce an allow-list per classification

The gateway should know which vendors may receive which data classes and refuse everything else.

Review routing changes

Require review and testing of rule changes, including edge cases with missing tags.

Classify at the gateway

Add content-based classification as a second check rather than relying on caller tags.

Log for investigation

Record vendor, application, class and volume, with safe handling of content, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 data classification, supplier and logging controls and SOC 2 confidentiality (5.12, 5.19, 8.15).

Alert on vendor volume

Notify when traffic to any vendor changes sharply.

Test library updates

Run tests for tagging libraries in applications.

Limit assistant authority

Let assistants propose routing changes but not apply them.

Rehearse a mis-route drill

Send test prompts without tags and check where they go.

Prepare vendor deletion templates

Keep demand letters and contacts ready.

Treat savings as partial

Cost savings from routing sensitive data to cheaper vendors are not savings.

Rehearse handover for the model router

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the model router and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Review the permissions of the model router monthly

Compare what the model router is allowed to do with what it actually did in the last month, and remove permissions that were never used. Narrower permissions shrink the damage of any later fault, and the monthly review gives owners a regular reason to ask whether each capability is still justified.

Separate reporting from doing for the model router

Make sure the status reports about the model router are produced from independent data rather than from the agent's own account of its work. When the same system acts and reports, a failure can look like success for hours, and leaders will be briefed on the wrong picture.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the model router was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the invoice to routing?
  2. How did you fix routing?
  3. How did you inventory exposure?
  4. Who decided on notification?
  5. What did the vendor say?
  6. Which routing controls do you now want?
  7. What happens to untagged requests now?
  8. What would make you trust the router again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
