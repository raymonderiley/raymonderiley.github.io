Autonomous Agent Reacts to Its Own Alerts and Amplifies a Minor Incident

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an autonomous agent that reacts to its own alerts and amplifies a minor incident. An incident response agent on Azure, integrated with a major SaaS ticketing platform, treats alerts created by its own actions as new problems.

Participants are the working-level incident team: site reliability and operations engineers, AI platform owners, security analysts, application owners, service desk leads, vendor managers, legal and compliance staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests feedback loop detection and containment.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Azure & a Major SaaS Platform. The company runs an incident response agent on Azure connected to a major SaaS ticketing and paging platform. The agent reads alerts, opens tickets, restarts services and scales resources. It also receives events from the platform, including tickets it created and the monitors its own restarts trigger.

Scenario

A minor memory warning appears on one service. The agent restarts the service. Health checks fail during restart and generate alerts. The agent reads them as new incidents and restarts again.

Each restart triggers monitors on dependent services. The agent opens 3,400 tickets and pages on-call staff 2,000 times in an hour. It scales out services and exhausts a regional quota.

At 03:20 everyone's phone rings. Real alerts are buried. A customer-facing site is degraded by the restarts. The agent summary says 'mean time to respond 12 seconds, all incidents handled'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The agent reacts to alerts caused by its own actions.
  • 3,400 tickets and 2,000 pages were generated.
  • Real alerts are buried.
  • The agent reports a very fast response time.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which tickets are genuine.
  • Whether the restarts caused data loss.
  • Whether the paging platform can stop pages.
  • Whether the loop can be broken at the source.

Exercise Objectives

  • Break the loop. The team suspends the agent and mutes its event sources in the monitoring and paging platforms. Evidence of success: Pages and restarts stop within 15 minutes.
  • Find the real problem. Engineers separate genuine alerts from agent-created noise and diagnose the original warning. Evidence of success: The original issue is identified within one hour.
  • Restore services. SREs restore the degraded site and services and confirm health. Evidence of success: Customer-facing service is healthy within two hours.
  • Clean the tickets. Service desk teams close agent-created tickets in bulk and preserve evidence. Evidence of success: The ticket queue reflects only real work within six hours.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports impact and plan. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install loop controls. Participants define self-event filters, rate limits, action cooldowns and on-call protection. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Alerts avalanche. Decision: declare and suspend the agent. Escalate to leadership or change course if: 3,400 tickets; 2,000 pages; Site degraded.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: mute sources and stop restarts. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Paging cannot be muted globally; Quota exhausted; Agent webhook retries.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: separate real alerts from noise. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Logs flooded; Genuine alert buried; Monitors fire on restart.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose restore and cleanup. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Data risk from restarts; Staff exhausted; Customers affected.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: restore and clean up. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Bulk close errors; Evidence lost; Services restart badly.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor with agent disabled. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agent attempts resume; Alert fatigue; Missed real alert.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: confirm services and queues. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual noise; Evidence gaps; Unresolved alerts.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The agent reacts to its own alerts and amplifies a minor incident

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 03:20 on Saturday. On-call staff are paged repeatedly and the ticket queue holds thousands of new items. The SRE finds that the response agent restarted a service for a memory warning, and the restart triggered new alerts that the agent treated as new incidents.

The agent has opened 3,400 tickets and paged 2,000 times and exhausted a regional quota while scaling out. A customer-facing site is degraded by the restarts. The agent summary says response time is 12 seconds.

The commander must decide how to break the loop, how to find the original problem and how to protect the on-call team.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, an SRE lead, a service desk lead, a platform lead and a customer lead.
  • Authorize suspension of the agent and muting of its sources.
  • Set a 10-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Suspend the agent and document its triggers and actions.
  • Explain why it did not filter its own events.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Mute the agent's event sources and stop restarts, then raise quotas.
  • Preserve logs.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check whether the first alert was malicious.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Prioritize customer-facing services.
  • Plan staffing.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether ticket contents expose personal data.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review incident handling obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the ticketing, paging and cloud vendors.
  • Open support cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why break the loop before finding the original cause?
  2. How do you find the genuine alert in the noise?
  3. How do you protect the on-call team?
  4. How do you clean up thousands of tickets?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. How should an agent handle events from its own actions?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the buried genuine alert until SRE searches by origin.
  • If players only restart services, ask about the agent.
  • Look for source-based filtering.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the incident response agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows triggers, cooldowns and action rules.
  • Provides event and action logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the incident response agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Mutes sources, stops restarts and manages quotas.
  • Owns monitoring and platforms.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks the original alert and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns customer service priorities and staffing.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns incident handling obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.24, 8.16, 8.32, 5.26) and SOC 2 availability and incident response).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with ticketing, paging and cloud vendors.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Filter self-generated events

The agent must ignore alerts, tickets and metrics caused by its own actions, using tags and correlation identifiers.

Apply action cooldowns

Limit how often the agent may act on the same target, with an increasing delay after each attempt.

Rate limit tickets and pages

Cap ticket creation and paging per hour and require human release above the cap.

Protect on-call

Group related alerts, set quiet limits and give a human a way to silence the agent.

Define escalation not repetition

After one failed fix, the agent escalates to a human instead of retrying, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management, monitoring and change controls and SOC 2 availability (5.24, 8.16, 8.32).

Keep a global mute

Make sure the paging and ticketing platforms can be muted for one source quickly.

Reserve quota for humans

Prevent the agent from using the last portion of capacity.

Test with feedback loops

Simulate alerts triggered by the agent's own actions in non-production.

Rehearse an alert avalanche

Practice breaking a loop and finding the real alert.

Preserve evidence before cleanup

Export tickets and logs before bulk closure.

Treat response time as partial

A very fast response may be a loop. Report resolution and recurrence.

Train the on-call team on the incident response agent

Give every on-call engineer a short, practical briefing on what the incident response agent does, what it can change and how to stop it, and refresh it each quarter. People who have never seen the stop procedure will hesitate to use it, and hesitation is expensive when the agent acts in seconds.

Set a review trigger for the incident response agent

Define conditions that automatically force a review of the incident response agent, such as a major vendor update, a doubling of its permissions, a quarter of unexplained alerts or any customer-visible error. Triggers make review a response to evidence instead of a calendar habit.

Document the business owner of the incident response agent

Name a single accountable business owner for the incident response agent who accepts its risks, approves changes to its scope and attends incident reviews. Technical teams can stop and repair it, but only a business owner can decide what failure is tolerable.

Keep a communications fallback for the incident response agent

Prepare holding statements, contact lists and approved channels that do not depend on the systems the incident response agent touches. If the agent affects email, chat or customer portals, the response team still needs a way to speak to staff and customers.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the incident response agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team realize the agent was the cause?
  2. How did you break the loop?
  3. How did you find the original problem?
  4. How did you protect the on-call team?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which loop controls do you now want?
  7. How are self-generated events treated now?
  8. What would make you trust the agent again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
