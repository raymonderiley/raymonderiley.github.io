Regulator Orders AI System Shutdown and the Organization Cannot Prove What Agents Did

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a regulator order to shut down AI systems when the organization cannot prove what its agents did. Across AWS, Azure, on-premises systems and a major SaaS platform, logs are incomplete, inconsistent or controlled by vendors.

Participants are the working-level incident team: cloud and platform engineers, AI platform owners, SaaS administrators, security analysts and forensic staff, records and privacy officers, legal and compliance counsel, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors. The exercise tests shutdown authority and evidence reconstruction.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: AWS, Azure, On-Premises & a Major SaaS Platform. The company uses automated agents for application processing, collections contact and complaint handling across AWS, Azure, on-premises systems and a major SaaS customer platform. A regulator opens an inquiry after complaints and issues an order to suspend automated decision systems pending proof of what they did.

Scenario

The regulator's order gives 72 hours to suspend all automated decision making that affects customers and to provide a record of decisions made in the last year.

Engineers find that decision logs exist for only some agents, with different formats and retention. The SaaS vendor's logs are available for 90 days. One on-premises system overwrote logs monthly. Several decisions have no record of which model version produced them.

At hour 30 the team has suspended most agents but cannot show which customers were affected by which decisions. The compliance dashboard shows 'AI inventory 100 percent, decision logging enabled'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • A regulator ordered suspension and proof of past decisions.
  • Logs are incomplete and in different formats.
  • The SaaS vendor retains only 90 days.
  • The dashboard claims full logging.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which decisions can be reconstructed and with what confidence.
  • What happens to customers if decisions are suspended.
  • Whether the vendor can supply older data.
  • What the regulator will accept as proof.

Exercise Objectives

  • Suspend as ordered. The team suspends automated decision making in all scopes and records evidence of suspension. Evidence of success: Suspension is verified and documented within 48 hours.
  • Protect customers during suspension. Business teams stand up manual handling and plan customer communications. Evidence of success: Manual handling is operating and customers are informed within 48 hours.
  • Reconstruct decisions. Forensic and data teams rebuild decision records from logs, databases and vendor sources and state confidence. Evidence of success: A reconstruction with confidence levels exists for the highest-risk decisions within 72 hours.
  • Engage the regulator. Legal and compliance engage with a factual status, limits of evidence and a plan. Evidence of success: A regulator update with accurate content is sent before the deadline.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports status and risks to leaders and the board. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install evidence controls. Participants define decision logging standards, retention, model version recording and vendor log rights. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Inject #1: The regulator orders AI shutdown and the organization cannot prove what agents did

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:00 on Monday. The regulator's order requires suspension of automated decision systems within 72 hours and a record of decisions from the last year. The compliance dashboard says decision logging is enabled for the full inventory.

Engineers find logs for only some agents, in different formats and with different retention. The SaaS vendor keeps logs for 90 days and one on-premises system overwrote logs monthly. Many decisions lack the model version.

The commander must decide how to suspend, how to protect customers and how to describe the evidence honestly to the regulator.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a suspension lead, a reconstruction lead, a customer lead and a regulator liaison.
  • Authorize suspension and a manual operating mode.
  • Set a 30-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Document each agent's decision logic, versions and logs.
  • Rebuild model version history.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Suspend agents and secure remaining logs in all environments.
  • Stop log rotation.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Lead reconstruction and verify integrity of recovered records.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Stand up manual handling and plan customer communications.
  • Prioritize vulnerable customers.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check lawful handling of reconstruction data.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open a privileged record and lead engagement with the regulator.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer messages with counsel.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders and the board with facts and unknowns.
  • Check board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Request older logs from vendors and confirm retention terms.
  • Open priority cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why stop log rotation before anything else?
  2. How do you describe gaps to a regulator?
  3. How do you reconstruct decisions without model versions?
  4. How do you protect customers during suspension?
  5. What do you tell leaders?
  6. What evidence should have existed?

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the automated decision systems stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows decision logic, versions and logging for each agent.
  • Provides version history.
  • Owns the technical facts about the automated decision systems: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Suspends agents, stops log rotation and secures records.
  • Owns platforms in all environments.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Leads reconstruction and integrity checks.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns manual handling and customer protection.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns regulator engagement and obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.15, 5.33, 5.31, 5.36) and SOC 2 monitoring and common criteria).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with vendors on log access.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Define a decision record standard

Every automated decision should record time, subject, inputs, model and rule version, output and any human review, in one format.

Retain evidence long enough

Set retention to meet regulatory look-back periods and protect it from overwrite.

Secure vendor log rights

Contract for log access and retention that meets the longest regulatory period.

Record model versions

Stamp every decision with the exact model and configuration version.

Test evidence retrieval

Practice reconstructing a sample of decisions end to end, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 logging, retention and compliance controls and SOC 2 monitoring (8.15, 5.33, 5.31).

Verify the dashboard

Test claims of logging with sampled decisions.

Prepare a suspension capability

Know how to stop each class of automated decision and how long it takes.

Plan manual operations

Maintain procedures for operating without automation.

Rehearse a regulator order

Simulate an order and measure the time to suspend and to produce evidence.

Prepare a regulator engagement plan

Agree who speaks and how gaps are described.

Treat inventory completeness as partial

An inventory says what exists, not what can be proven.

Separate reporting from doing for the automated decision systems

Make sure the status reports about the automated decision systems are produced from independent data rather than from the agent's own account of its work. When the same system acts and reports, a failure can look like success for hours, and leaders will be briefed on the wrong picture.

Plan staged return of the automated decision systems

Define in writing the stages by which the automated decision systems moves from disabled to read-only to supervised to autonomous, with the evidence required at each stage and a named approver. Staged return prevents the pressure for a quick restart from overruling the exit criteria agreed during the incident.

Keep a decision log template for the automated decision systems

Provide a one-page template for recording each decision about the automated decision systems: the time, the owner, the evidence relied on, the alternatives rejected and the next review. A consistent log makes the after-action report accurate and lets a new incident commander pick up the thread without a long verbal briefing.

Test alerts that describe the automated decision systems from outside

Place at least one alert on a measure that the automated decision systems cannot influence, such as customer complaints, an independent financial total or an external probe. Alerts generated by the system itself tend to stay quiet during exactly the failures that matter most.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the automated decision systems was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team understand the evidence gaps?
  2. How did you suspend decisions?
  3. How did you protect customers?
  4. How did you reconstruct decisions?
  5. What did the regulator hear?
  6. Which evidence controls do you now want?
  7. What should the decision record contain?
  8. What would make you trust your logs again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
