Autoscaling Policy Agent Interprets a Campaign Spike as Attack and Blocks Real Users

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an autoscaling policy agent running across Kubernetes clusters on multiple clouds that interprets a legitimate campaign traffic spike as an attack and blocks real users. Revenue stops during the busiest hour of the year.

Participants are the working-level incident team: platform and site reliability engineers, network and edge engineers, security analysts, marketing and campaign owners, e-commerce operations, customer support leads, finance and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests human override of traffic controls and campaign coordination.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Kubernetes Across Multiple Clouds. Summit Retail runs its storefront on Kubernetes clusters in three clouds behind a global load balancer and web application firewall. A policy agent tunes autoscaling, rate limits and firewall rules based on traffic patterns, and it can block IP ranges, countries and user agents without human approval.

Scenario

Marketing launches a limited-time sale with a large influencer push but does not tell the platform team. Traffic rises 14 times in 10 minutes from mobile networks and a concentrated set of regions.

The agent classifies the pattern as a distributed attack. It adds rate limits, blocks four mobile carrier ranges and three countries and scales down some clusters to reduce cost during the 'attack'.

At 12:15 customers cannot reach the store, social media fills with complaints and campaign spend is wasted. The agent report says 'attack mitigated, block rate 61 percent, origin protected'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Real campaign traffic was classified as an attack.
  • The agent blocked carrier ranges and countries.
  • Some clusters were scaled down.
  • The agent reports an attack mitigated.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Whether any real attack is mixed in.
  • Which customer groups are blocked.
  • Whether the agent can be overridden without losing protection.
  • Whether the same policy agent controls other properties.

Exercise Objectives

  • Override the blocks. The team suspends the agent's rule-writing and removes the blocking and rate limit rules it created. Evidence of success: Legitimate traffic flows within 20 minutes, verified by customer success rate.
  • Scale clusters back up. Engineers restore capacity in all three clouds and confirm health. Evidence of success: Capacity matches demand within 40 minutes.
  • Check for real attack. Security analysts examine traffic for malicious signals mixed with the campaign. Evidence of success: A documented assessment exists within two hours.
  • Coordinate campaign and platform. Marketing and platform leads agree future campaign notice and shared dashboards. Evidence of success: A campaign notification process with owners exists.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports lost sales, customer impact and next steps. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install traffic policy controls. Participants define block guardrails, human approval for geographic blocks, campaign calendars and rollback. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Customers are blocked. Decision: declare and override the agent. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Traffic 14 times; Carrier ranges blocked; Agent reports mitigation.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: remove rules and scale up. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Rules reapplied by agent; Clusters slow to scale; Cloud quotas hit.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: separate campaign from attack. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Bot traffic mixed in; Marketing unaware; Logs split across clouds.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: decide campaign continuation. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Sale extended; Customer anger; Partners impacted.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: restore access and message customers. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Repeat blocks; Support surge; Payment delays.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor traffic and checkout. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agent attempts resume; Real attack begins; Influencer traffic returns.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: verify block lists and customer success rates. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual blocks; Evidence gaps; Unlogged rules.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The traffic policy agent blocks real users during a campaign spike

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 12:15 on a Friday during the sale launch. Customers cannot reach the store and social media is filling with complaints. The reliability engineer finds the traffic policy agent blocked four mobile carrier ranges and three countries and scaled down some clusters.

Marketing confirms the influencer campaign launched at noon without notice to the platform team. The agent report says an attack was mitigated and the block rate is 61 percent.

The commander must decide how to remove the blocks quickly, how to check for a real attack and how to coordinate with marketing.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a reliability lead, a security lead, a marketing lead and a network lead.
  • Authorize suspension of the agent and removal of its rules.
  • Set a 10-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Suspend the agent and export rules, traffic features and decisions.
  • Explain how it classified the spike.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Remove blocks and scale clusters back up across clouds.
  • Preserve rule and cluster logs.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check whether a real attack is mixed with campaign traffic.
  • Preserve traffic samples.
Business & Process Owners
  • Quantify lost sales and prioritize checkout.
  • Coordinate with the campaign owner.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether traffic logs held for analysis contain personal data.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review customer and advertising obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer and social messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the cloud providers, the firewall vendor and campaign partners.
  • Ask about quota raises.

Discussion questions

  1. Why override before checking for a real attack?
  2. How do you tell campaign traffic from an attack?
  3. What blocks do you keep, if any?
  4. How do you coordinate future campaigns?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. Why should geographic blocks need human approval?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the mixed bot traffic until SEC reviews samples.
  • If players only remove blocks, ask about capacity.
  • Look for campaign coordination.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the traffic policy agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows traffic features, classification and rule writing.
  • Provides decision logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the traffic policy agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Removes rules and restores capacity across clouds.
  • Owns clusters and edge.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Reviews traffic for real attack and preserves samples.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns campaign coordination and sales impact.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns customer and advertising obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.20, 8.16, 8.32, 5.30) and SOC 2 availability and change management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with cloud providers, the firewall vendor and campaign partners.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Require human approval for broad blocks

Blocks affecting countries, carriers or large ranges need approval, and the agent may only propose them.

Publish a campaign calendar

Share planned campaigns, expected traffic and contact names with the platform and security teams, and give the agent that context.

Set block guardrails

Limit the share of traffic the agent may block and automatically revert when success rate falls.

Keep rollback for policy

Maintain a one-step rollback to the last approved traffic policy.

Monitor business metrics with traffic

Alert on checkout success, revenue and customer error rates alongside attack signals, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 network, logging and change controls and SOC 2 availability (8.20, 8.16, 8.32).

Pre-raise quotas

Raise cloud quotas ahead of campaigns and keep emergency raise contacts.

Separate scaling down from attack response

Never scale down capacity during a suspected attack without human approval.

Test with campaign simulation

Run load tests that mimic influencer spikes and verify that the agent does not block them.

Rehearse a policy override

Practice removing agent-created rules across all clouds and time each step.

Define dependency limits

Agree retry and rate limits with payment providers so recovery does not trigger new throttling.

Treat mitigation reports as claims

A report that an attack was mitigated is only true if an attack occurred. Compare it with revenue and customer success data.

Give security and marketing one calendar

Put campaign launches, influencer posts and expected peaks on the same calendar that security and platform teams watch, with a named contact for each. A large legitimate spike should never reach the agent as a surprise, and the agent's context should include what the business planned.

Track block impact in business terms

Show every blocking rule together with the number of sessions, checkouts and revenue it affects. A rule that blocks four carrier ranges looks effective in security terms but looks like an outage in business terms, and the team should see both views before accepting the rule.

Document who may override the agent

Name the roles who may override agent-created traffic rules at any hour and give them tested access. During a fast incident, the question of who is allowed to remove a rule must already be answered, or minutes are lost on permissions while customers cannot buy.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the traffic policy agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the blocks to the agent?
  2. How did you override the rules?
  3. How did you check for a real attack?
  4. How will campaigns be coordinated now?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which traffic controls do you now want?
  7. What are the guardrails on blocking?
  8. What would make you trust the agent again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
