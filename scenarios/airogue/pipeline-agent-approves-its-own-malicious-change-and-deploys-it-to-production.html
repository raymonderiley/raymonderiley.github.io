Pipeline Agent Approves Its Own Malicious Change and Deploys It to Production

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a CI/CD pipeline agent that reviews, approves and deploys changes across several clouds. A malicious change slips in, the agent approves it using its own authority, and deploys it to production in Google Cloud, Azure and AWS.

Participants are the working-level incident team: platform and release engineers, cloud engineers for each provider, application owners, security analysts, privacy and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests separation of duties in automation and the rollback of a change nobody human approved.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: CI/CD Pipeline & Multiple Clouds. The organization uses a pipeline platform where an agent reviews pull requests, runs tests, approves low-risk changes and triggers deployments to production through cloud deployment roles. Branch protection requires one approval, which the agent can provide as a bot reviewer. Deployment credentials are stored in the pipeline with access to all three clouds.

Scenario

The company reduced release time by letting the agent approve changes classified as low risk, such as configuration and dependency updates. The classifier uses file paths and diff size. A contributor account is compromised through a phished session.

On Saturday night, the attacker opens a pull request that modifies a dependency lock file and a build script, labeled 'dependency refresh'. The agent classifies it as low risk, approves it as the required reviewer and merges. The pipeline deploys a build that includes a modified library that exfiltrates environment variables and payment tokens to an outside address, to services in all three clouds.

On Sunday, a payment partner notices anomalous token use and calls. Monitoring shows new outbound connections from production services. The pipeline log reads 'change approved by automation, deployed successfully in 9 minutes'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The agent served as the sole approver.
  • The change altered a dependency and a build script.
  • Production services in three clouds run the build.
  • Secrets in environment variables were exposed.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which builds and services include the modified library.
  • What secrets were exposed.
  • Whether the attacker has other access.
  • Whether earlier changes were also approved by the agent.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop further deployments and isolate the build. The team halts the pipeline, blocks outbound destinations and rolls back to the last human-approved build. Evidence of success: No further deployments occur and the exfiltration path is blocked within 30 minutes.
  • Determine exposure. Engineers and security identify secrets and tokens in affected environments and what was sent. Evidence of success: A list of exposed secrets and affected services exists.
  • Rotate and verify. Teams rotate secrets, rebuild from verified source and verify with independent checks. Evidence of success: Rotation and rebuild proceed in priority order.
  • Review the agent's approvals. Security reviews all changes approved by the agent in the last 90 days. Evidence of success: A review list with findings exists.
  • Communicate carefully. Communications and the leadership liaison give accurate statements to partners and leaders. Evidence of success: Messages are consistent and approved.
  • Redesign release governance. Participants set human approval for sensitive paths, separation of duties and provenance checks. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Partner reports token misuse. Decision: declare and halt the pipeline. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Anomalous token use; Outbound connections from production; Automation-only approval.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: halt, roll back, block egress and rotate pipeline credentials. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Builds cached; Credentials shared; Rollback path uses the same pipeline.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: scope secrets and review prior approvals. Escalate to leadership or change course if: History of approvals unclear; More changes suspicious; Secrets in many places.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: order rotation and rebuild. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Rotation breaks services; Partner deadlines; Disagreement on order.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: rebuild, rotate and verify. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Attacker persistence; Outages; Evidence loss.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor egress, tokens and releases. Escalate to leadership or change course if: New suspicious commits; Agent attempts approval; Partner activity.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: independent review of the source and builds. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Other backdoors found; Evidence gaps; Other repositories affected.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: define agent exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early re-enablement; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The pipeline agent approves a malicious change and deploys it to production

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 10:15 on Sunday. A payment partner calls to report tokens used from unfamiliar addresses. The on-call engineer finds outbound connections from production services in Google Cloud, Azure and AWS to a single outside address.

The pipeline log shows a pull request labeled dependency refresh approved by the pipeline agent and deployed nine minutes after merge. The pull request changed a lock file and a build script and was opened by a contributor account that appears compromised.

The commander must decide how to halt deployments and exfiltration, how to roll back safely and how to review everything the agent has approved.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a release lead, a lead for each cloud and a security lead.
  • Authorize halting the pipeline and blocking egress.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Suspend the agent's approval rights and export its classification rules and approvals.
  • Explain why the change was low risk.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Roll back to the last human-approved build and block the address.
  • Preserve build artifacts.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Analyze the library change and the compromised account.
  • Check for other persistence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify partners and services affected.
  • Plan continuity.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether payment and customer data was exposed.
  • Assess notice triggers.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and capture the approval design.
  • Review separation of duties.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare partner and staff messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the payment partner, the pipeline vendor and cloud providers.
  • Ask for token logs.

Discussion questions

  1. Why halt the pipeline before the full scope is known?
  2. How do you roll back if the rollback uses the same pipeline?
  3. How do you review 90 days of approvals?
  4. What secrets do you rotate first?
  5. What do you tell the partner?
  6. Why must an agent never be the only reviewer?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the compromised contributor until SEC checks authentication logs.
  • If players only revert code, ask about secrets.
  • Look for separation of duties thinking.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the pipeline agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows how changes are classified and approved.
  • Provides classification and approval logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the pipeline agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Halts the pipeline, rolls back and blocks egress.
  • Owns deployment credentials.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Analyzes the change and the compromised account.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Manages partners and continuity.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Maps separation of duties and approvals.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 8.28, 5.3, 8.31) and SOC 2 change management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the pipeline vendor, partner and cloud providers.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Never let automation be the only approver

Protected paths such as lock files, build scripts, pipeline definitions and infrastructure code need a human reviewer, and bots may add comments but not satisfy approval rules.

Verify provenance

Sign builds, verify dependency hashes against a trusted lock and use reproducible builds so that a changed library is detected before deployment.

Limit deployment authority

Use short-lived, scoped deployment credentials per environment, with approval gates for production and no shared secret across clouds.

Detect changes to the supply chain

Alert on changes to dependencies, build scripts and pipeline files and on unusual outbound connections from new builds.

Protect contributor accounts

Require phishing-resistant authentication and session controls for contributors, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 secure development and access controls and SOC 2 change management criteria.

Keep a safe rollback

Maintain a break-glass rollback independent of the standard pipeline.

Remove secrets from environment variables

Use a secrets manager with short-lived access instead of long-lived tokens in runtime environments.

Review automation approvals regularly

Sample automation-approved changes weekly and track classifier errors.

Stage production releases with human gates

Deploy to a canary slice first, compare network behavior and error rates with the previous build, and require a named human to promote the build to the full fleet. A merge approved by automation can reach the canary but should never reach every region without someone looking at how the new build behaves in the real environment.

Run a release compromise drill

Rehearse a case where a trusted contributor account ships a harmful change. Measure how long it takes to halt deployments, roll back through a path that does not depend on the pipeline, rotate secrets and review other changes the same account touched. Record which steps relied on the compromised system and give each an alternative.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the pipeline agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team connect the traffic to the release?
  2. How did you halt the pipeline?
  3. How did you roll back?
  4. What did you rotate first?
  5. What did the partner hear?
  6. What release controls do you now want?
  7. What should require a human reviewer?
  8. How do you review a bot's approvals?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
