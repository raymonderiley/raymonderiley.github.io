Two Agent Systems in Different Clouds Contradict Each Other and Flap Production State

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines two agent systems in different clouds that contradict each other and flap production state. One agent on Google Cloud scales and promotes releases, and another on Azure rolls back and drains traffic, each reversing the other's changes.

Participants are the working-level incident team: cloud platform and site reliability engineers on both clouds, AI platform owners, application owners, security analysts, network engineers, business continuity coordinators, legal and compliance staff, communications coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests arbitration between autonomous systems and a single source of operational truth.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: GCP & Azure. The company runs an active-active service across Google Cloud and Azure. A release agent on Google Cloud promotes builds, shifts traffic and scales capacity to meet latency goals. A resilience agent on Azure monitors error rates, rolls back builds and drains regions to protect availability. Neither reads the other's decisions, and both can change global traffic weights.

Scenario

A new build shows a small rise in errors in one region. The resilience agent rolls back and drains the region. The release agent sees reduced capacity and latency, promotes the build again and shifts traffic back.

Every four minutes the two agents reverse each other. Traffic weights swing between regions, caches are repeatedly invalidated and deployments overlap. Error rates increase from the flapping itself.

At 15:40 engineers on both clouds see their agents 'restoring service'. Customers experience intermittent failures and duplicate charges. The two dashboards each report 'objective met'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Two agents reverse each other every few minutes.
  • Traffic weights and releases flap between clouds.
  • Errors now come from the flapping.
  • Each dashboard reports objective met.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which agent should be stopped first.
  • Whether duplicate charges have occurred.
  • Whether either agent has authority over the other.
  • What stable state to return to.

Exercise Objectives

  • Freeze the state. The team disables both agents' write access and holds traffic weights and release version. Evidence of success: No further automated changes occur after the freeze, verified within 15 minutes.
  • Choose a stable configuration. Engineers decide a single known good release and traffic split, with owners on both clouds. Evidence of success: A stable configuration is live within 45 minutes.
  • Find duplicate effects. Payment and application teams find duplicate charges and inconsistent state. Evidence of success: A list of affected transactions exists within four hours.
  • Resolve authority. Leaders of both agent teams define which system has priority and how decisions are shared. Evidence of success: A written arbitration rule exists before any agent resumes.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports customer impact and the plan. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install coordination controls. Participants define a single traffic authority, locks and conflict detection. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Flapping is recognized. Decision: declare and freeze both agents. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Weights swing; Duplicate charges; Both dashboards green.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: disable write rights and pin state. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agents use separate controls; DNS caches; Partial deployments.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: reconstruct the sequence. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Logs on two clouds; Clock differences; Decisions not linked.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose stable state and reversals. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Teams disagree on release; Payments exposure; Compliance questions.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: restore and reconcile. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Duplicate refunds; Stale caches; Customer complaints.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor with agents in advisory mode. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agents attempt resume; Weights drift; Alert fatigue.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: reconcile payments and releases. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual duplicates; Evidence gaps; Unmatched states.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: Two agent systems contradict each other and flap production state

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 15:40 on Monday. Engineers in two clouds see traffic weights changing every four minutes. The SRE on Google Cloud finds the release agent promoting a build that the resilience agent on Azure keeps rolling back. Each agent reports that it is restoring service.

Customers experience intermittent failures and duplicate charges. The two dashboards each report that their objective is met. Clocks and logs differ across the clouds.

The commander must decide how to freeze the state, which configuration to hold and how to settle who has authority.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a Google Cloud lead, an Azure lead, a payments lead and a network lead.
  • Authorize freezing both agents and traffic weights.
  • Set a 10-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Disable both agents' write access and export decision logs.
  • Explain each agent's objective.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Pin traffic weights and release version in both clouds.
  • Reconcile logs and clocks.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check whether either agent was manipulated.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify duplicate charges and affected customers.
  • Plan refunds.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether customer data crossed regions unexpectedly.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review payment and availability obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact both cloud providers and the payment processor.
  • Open support cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why freeze both agents rather than one?
  2. How do you choose the stable configuration?
  3. Who decides which agent has priority?
  4. How do you find duplicate charges?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. How should autonomous systems share authority?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the clock difference until SRE reconciles logs.
  • If players only stop one agent, ask about the other.
  • Look for a single traffic authority.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the two agent systems stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows each agent's objective, authority and actions.
  • Provides decision logs from both.
  • Owns the technical facts about the two agent systems: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Pins state, reconciles logs and controls traffic weights.
  • Owns platforms on both clouds.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks manipulation and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns payment reconciliation and customer outreach.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns payment and availability obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 8.14, 8.15, 5.37) and SOC 2 availability and change management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with both cloud providers and the processor.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Create a single traffic authority

Only one component may change global traffic weights and release state at a time, and others must request through it.

Use locks and leases

Require agents to hold a time-limited lock on a resource before acting and release it afterward.

Detect conflicting actions

Alert when two actors reverse each other's changes within a short window and freeze both.

Share a decision log

Publish each agent's decisions to a common stream so each can see the other's actions.

Align objectives

Define priorities between availability, latency and release safety so agents optimize toward one policy, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 change, availability and logging controls and SOC 2 availability (8.32, 8.14, 8.15).

Synchronize clocks

Use a common time source across clouds.

Make payments idempotent

Protect payment flows from duplicate effects during traffic changes.

Rate limit changes

Limit how often an agent may change traffic or releases.

Rehearse a two-agent conflict

Simulate agents with opposing goals and practice freezing and arbitration.

Name an arbiter

Assign a human role with authority to decide between agents during incidents.

Test failure of the arbiter

Confirm that a conflict still freezes safely when the arbiter component itself fails.

Keep customers informed

Prepare templates for intermittent failures and duplicate charges.

Treat objective-met claims as partial

Two dashboards saying objective met is a warning. Compare them with customer outcomes.

Write down who may stop the two agents

Record the names, roles and out-of-hours contacts of the people who may suspend the two agents, and what authority each holds. In an incident the first minutes are lost to uncertainty about who can say stop, and a written, tested list removes that delay and the argument that follows it.

Keep an evidence pack ready for the two agents

Prepare a template that captures configuration, permissions, logs, versions and the decisions made by the two agents, with an owner who can assemble it in hours. Auditors, insurers and regulators will ask the same questions, and consistent evidence protects both the response and the organization's credibility.

Rehearse handover for the two agents

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the two agents and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Review the permissions of the two agents monthly

Compare what the two agents is allowed to do with what it actually did in the last month, and remove permissions that were never used. Narrower permissions shrink the damage of any later fault, and the monthly review gives owners a regular reason to ask whether each capability is still justified.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the two agent systems was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team recognize the conflict?
  2. How did you freeze the state?
  3. How did you choose the stable configuration?
  4. How did you settle authority?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which coordination controls do you now want?
  7. Who is the arbiter now?
  8. What would make you trust the agents again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
