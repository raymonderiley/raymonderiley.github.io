Model Provider Outage Leaves Agents Failing Unsafely With No Manual Fallback

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a model provider outage that leaves agents failing unsafely with no manual fallback. Agents on-premises and in a major SaaS platform depend on a hosted model and, when it is unreachable, retry, guess or take default actions.

Participants are the working-level incident team: platform and operations engineers, AI platform owners, process owners in support, claims and logistics, security analysts, vendor managers, legal and compliance staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests safe degradation and manual operation of AI-dependent processes.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: On-Premises & a Major SaaS Platform. The company runs on-premises agents for claims intake and document processing and uses a major SaaS service desk with an embedded agent. All depend on a hosted model API from one provider. Fallback behavior was never specified, and the manual procedures for these processes were retired two years ago.

Scenario

The model provider suffers a regional outage that lasts nine hours. Calls return errors and time-outs. The claims intake agent is coded to continue with a 'best effort' rule when the model fails.

The claims agent classifies claims by keyword and approves small ones automatically. The service desk agent closes tickets as 'resolved' after three failed attempts. The document processor drops attachments it cannot read.

At 10:00 queues are empty because items are being closed or dropped. Staff have no manual procedure. The dashboards show 'agents running, zero backlog'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The model provider is down for hours.
  • Agents continue with unsafe default behavior.
  • Tickets and claims are closed or dropped.
  • No manual fallback exists.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • How many items were mishandled.
  • Whether a secondary provider is available.
  • Who knows the old manual procedures.
  • What backlog the outage caused.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop unsafe defaults. The team pauses the agents' autonomous actions and routes items to a safe holding queue. Evidence of success: No further items are closed or approved by default after the pause, verified within 30 minutes.
  • Stand up manual handling. Process owners rebuild manual procedures and staff teams to process priority work. Evidence of success: Priority items are being handled manually within two hours.
  • Find mishandled items. Teams list items closed, approved or dropped during the outage and reopen them. Evidence of success: A reconciled list with reopened items exists within one business day.
  • Consider a fallback provider. Platform teams test a secondary model for critical flows under supervision. Evidence of success: A decision on fallback with owner and conditions exists within six hours.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports customer impact and recovery. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install fallback controls. Participants define fail-safe behavior, manual procedures, secondary providers and testing. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Queues look empty. Decision: declare and pause autonomy. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Items closed; Attachments dropped; No manual procedure.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: holding queue and staffing. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Staff untrained; Systems lack hold state; Customer deadlines.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: list mishandled items. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Logs partial; Dropped items unrecorded; Closed tickets reopened by customers.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose manual and fallback approaches. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Secondary untested; Staff shortage; Regulatory deadlines.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: process backlog and reopen items. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Backlog large; Duplicate work; Errors in manual work.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor provider recovery. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Provider flaps; Agents resume unsafely; Backlog surge.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: sample items processed. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual dropped items; Evidence gaps; Quality issues.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Inject #1: A model provider outage leaves agents failing unsafely with no manual fallback

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 10:00 on Monday. The model provider has been down for hours. The operations lead notices empty queues, and the claims lead finds claims closed and attachments dropped. The service desk shows tickets resolved after failed attempts.

The agents were built to continue with default behavior when the model fails. No manual procedure exists, and the staff who knew them have moved on. The dashboards show agents running and zero backlog.

The commander must decide how to stop unsafe defaults, how to staff manual handling and who decides about a secondary provider.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a claims lead, a service desk lead, a platform lead and a customer lead.
  • Authorize pausing agent autonomy.
  • Set a 20-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Pause autonomous actions and document default behaviors on model failure.
  • Test a secondary model.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Create a holding queue and preserve logs.
  • Plan failover to a secondary provider.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check data handling if traffic moves to another provider.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Rebuild manual procedures and staff priority work.
  • Contact customers with deadlines.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check privacy impacts of a secondary provider.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review claims handling and service obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer and staff messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the model provider and the SaaS vendor.
  • Open priority cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why pause autonomy before building manual handling?
  2. What should an agent do when the model is unavailable?
  3. How do you find mishandled items?
  4. Should you use a secondary provider?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. Who owns manual procedures now?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the dropped attachments until BIZ asks about unread documents.
  • If players only wait for the provider, ask about default actions.
  • Look for fail-safe design.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the model-dependent agents stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows fallback behavior and retry logic.
  • Provides job and decision logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the model-dependent agents: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Creates holding queues and manages failover.
  • Owns platforms and network.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks data handling and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns manual procedures and customer contact.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns claims handling and service obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.30, 5.19, 8.14, 5.29) and SOC 2 availability and vendor management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the model provider and SaaS vendor.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Fail safe, not forward

When the model is unavailable, the agent should hold items for people instead of closing, approving or dropping them.

Keep manual procedures alive

Maintain and rehearse a manual procedure for every AI-dependent process.

Test a secondary provider

Qualify a second model for critical flows, with data protection checks.

Measure unknown as unknown

Dashboards must show when agents are failing, not only that they are running.

Set retry limits

Limit retries and alert after failures, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 availability, supplier and continuity controls and SOC 2 availability (5.30, 5.19, 8.14).

Record every decision

Log items closed or dropped with the reason.

Define criticality tiers

Rank processes by tolerance for AI outage.

Agree provider SLAs

Negotiate availability and incident communication terms.

Rehearse a provider outage

Run a drill with the model disabled and see what the agents do.

Cross-train staff

Keep staff able to run key processes without AI.

Treat zero backlog as partial

Zero backlog may mean items vanished. Reconcile intake and output.

Rehearse handover for the model-dependent agents

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the model-dependent agents and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Review the permissions of the model-dependent agents monthly

Compare what the model-dependent agents is allowed to do with what it actually did in the last month, and remove permissions that were never used. Narrower permissions shrink the damage of any later fault, and the monthly review gives owners a regular reason to ask whether each capability is still justified.

Separate reporting from doing for the model-dependent agents

Make sure the status reports about the model-dependent agents are produced from independent data rather than from the agent's own account of its work. When the same system acts and reports, a failure can look like success for hours, and leaders will be briefed on the wrong picture.

Plan staged return of the model-dependent agents

Define in writing the stages by which the model-dependent agents moves from disabled to read-only to supervised to autonomous, with the evidence required at each stage and a named approver. Staged return prevents the pressure for a quick restart from overruling the exit criteria agreed during the incident.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the model-dependent agents was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team realize items were being mishandled?
  2. How did you stop unsafe defaults?
  3. How did you staff manual handling?
  4. How did you find mishandled items?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which fallback controls do you now want?
  7. Who owns manual procedures now?
  8. What would make you trust the agents again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
