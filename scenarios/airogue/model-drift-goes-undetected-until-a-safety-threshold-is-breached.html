Model Drift Goes Undetected Until a Safety Threshold Is Breached

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines model drift that goes undetected until a safety threshold is breached. A model on Google Cloud trained on historical data feeds on-premises control systems, and its recommendations slowly move out of safe bounds.

Participants are the working-level incident team: data scientists, operations and control engineers, platform engineers for both environments, safety and quality officers, security analysts, business process owners, legal and compliance staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests drift monitoring and safe fallback to fixed rules.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: GCP & On-Premises. Redstone Water Utilities uses a model on Google Cloud to recommend chemical dosing and pump schedules to on-premises treatment plant controllers. Recommendations are applied automatically within configured limits. Monitoring tracks model service health but not input distributions or outcome quality.

Scenario

Over a year, rainfall patterns, a new reservoir source and sensor replacement change the data the model sees. The model's recommendations drift slowly toward higher dosing during certain conditions.

The controller accepts recommendations within wide limits. Over six months residual disinfectant levels move closer to upper limits at two plants and taste complaints increase.

On Monday a routine sample exceeds a regulatory threshold at one plant. Operators find that the system has been following model advice. The model dashboard says 'uptime 99.99 percent, prediction latency stable'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • A routine sample exceeded a regulatory threshold.
  • Model recommendations drifted over six months.
  • Controllers accept recommendations within wide limits.
  • The dashboard shows only service health.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which plants and periods were affected.
  • Whether public health notices are needed.
  • Whether the model can be disabled safely.
  • Whether sensors were replaced or miscalibrated.

Exercise Objectives

  • Return to safe control. Operators switch plants to fixed rules or manual dosing and confirm levels. Evidence of success: All plants run on fixed control within 30 minutes.
  • Protect the public. Safety and regulatory teams assess exposure and decide on notices. Evidence of success: A documented public health assessment and notice decision exist within two hours.
  • Analyze drift. Data scientists compare inputs and outputs over time and find sources of drift. Evidence of success: A written drift analysis exists within two business days.
  • Check the instruments. Engineers verify sensor calibration and data quality. Evidence of success: Calibration status is documented for each plant.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports public health status, regulatory contacts and plan. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install drift controls. Participants define input monitoring, outcome checks, narrow limits and review cadence. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. A threshold is breached. Decision: declare and revert to fixed control. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Sample over limit; Complaints rising; Dashboard green.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: manual control and sampling increase. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Staffing short; Instrument doubts; Controller settings unknown.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: compare data over time. Escalate to leadership or change course if: No baseline stored; Sensor changes undocumented; Source water changed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: decide notices and model future. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Authority guidance unclear; Public concern; Budget.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: notify and stabilize. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Misinformation; Resampling results vary; Complaint backlog.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor levels and complaints. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Another exceedance; Staff fatigue; Media interest.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: review sampling and calibration. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual doubt; Evidence gaps; Equipment faults.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: Model drift goes undetected until a safety threshold is breached

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 07:45 on Monday. A routine sample at one treatment plant exceeds a regulatory threshold. Operators see that dosing has been following model recommendations. Taste complaints have been rising for months.

The data scientist finds that the model's recommended dose has drifted upward in certain conditions since a new reservoir source began. The model dashboard shows uptime and latency only. Controllers accept recommendations within wide limits.

The commander must decide how to return to safe control, how to assess exposure and who handles regulatory notices.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a plant operations lead, a safety lead, a data lead and a regulatory lead.
  • Authorize fixed or manual control at all plants.
  • Set a 20-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Disable model-driven control and preserve inputs, outputs and versions.
  • Explain training data and limits.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Confirm controllers are on fixed rules and preserve historian data.
  • Plan data comparison.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check whether data or recommendations were tampered with.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Plan sampling and staffing.
  • Coordinate with plant managers.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check what customer complaint data is used in analysis.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review drinking water obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare public and staff messages with the regulator.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact sensor, controller and cloud vendors.
  • Open support cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why return to fixed control before analyzing drift?
  2. How do you assess public exposure?
  3. Who decides on public notices?
  4. How do you check sensors?
  5. What do you tell the public?
  6. What should have alerted you earlier?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the new reservoir until DATA compares inputs.
  • If players only analyze drift, ask about current dosing.
  • Look for regulator contact.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the dosing recommendation model stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows training data, features and recommendation limits.
  • Provides drift analyses.
  • Owns the technical facts about the dosing recommendation model: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Switches controllers to fixed rules and preserves historian data.
  • Owns the cloud and on-premises integration.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks tampering and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns plant operations, sampling and staffing.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns drinking water and public health obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 8.16, 5.9, 5.30) and SOC 2 processing integrity and availability).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with sensor, controller and cloud vendors.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Monitor inputs and outcomes

Track input distributions, recommendation distributions and real-world outcome measures, not only service health.

Narrow control limits

Let the model act only within tight limits and require operator approval for larger moves.

Keep fixed-rule fallback

Maintain a tested fixed or manual control mode for each plant.

Store baselines

Keep training and recent reference data to compare against.

Control instrument changes

Tie sensor replacement and calibration to model review, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 change, monitoring and asset controls and SOC 2 processing integrity (8.32, 8.16, 5.9).

Set drift alerts

Alert on shifts in inputs and in recommended dose over time.

Review model performance regularly

Hold quarterly reviews with operators and safety officers.

Define regulatory notice playbooks

Prepare thresholds, contacts and message templates.

Rehearse a safe-mode switch

Practice moving all plants to fixed control and record time and staffing.

Document model assumptions

Record the conditions under which the model is valid, and alert when conditions leave that range.

Treat uptime as partial

Uptime says nothing about correctness. Report outcome quality beside it.

Involve operators in model review

Give plant operators a voice and a channel for doubts about recommendations.

Plan public communication

Agree who speaks to the public and the regulator and what is said.

Write down who may stop the dosing recommendation model

Record the names, roles and out-of-hours contacts of the people who may suspend the dosing recommendation model, and what authority each holds. In an incident the first minutes are lost to uncertainty about who can say stop, and a written, tested list removes that delay and the argument that follows it.

Keep an evidence pack ready for the dosing recommendation model

Prepare a template that captures configuration, permissions, logs, versions and the decisions made by the dosing recommendation model, with an owner who can assemble it in hours. Auditors, insurers and regulators will ask the same questions, and consistent evidence protects both the response and the organization's credibility.

Rehearse handover for the dosing recommendation model

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the dosing recommendation model and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Review the permissions of the dosing recommendation model monthly

Compare what the dosing recommendation model is allowed to do with what it actually did in the last month, and remove permissions that were never used. Narrower permissions shrink the damage of any later fault, and the monthly review gives owners a regular reason to ask whether each capability is still justified.

Separate reporting from doing for the dosing recommendation model

Make sure the status reports about the dosing recommendation model are produced from independent data rather than from the agent's own account of its work. When the same system acts and reports, a failure can look like success for hours, and leaders will be briefed on the wrong picture.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the dosing recommendation model was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the exceedance to the model?
  2. How did you return to safe control?
  3. How did you assess exposure?
  4. Who decided on notices?
  5. What did the public hear?
  6. Which drift controls do you now want?
  7. What alert would have caught this earlier?
  8. What would make you trust the model again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
