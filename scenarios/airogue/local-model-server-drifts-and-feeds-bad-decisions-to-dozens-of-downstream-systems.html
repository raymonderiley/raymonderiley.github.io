Local Model Server Drifts and Feeds Bad Decisions to Dozens of Downstream Systems

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a local model server on-premises that drifts and feeds bad decisions to dozens of downstream systems. Each downstream consumer trusts the same internal endpoint, so errors spread quietly through scheduling, pricing, routing and quality checks.

Participants are the working-level incident team: data center and infrastructure engineers, AI platform owners, application owners for downstream systems, security analysts, business process owners, legal and compliance staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests discovery of consumers and coordinated rollback.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: On-Premises. The company hosts a general-purpose model on GPU servers in two company data centers. Over 50 internal systems call its endpoint for classification, extraction, forecasting hints and routing suggestions. Weights are updated through a nightly sync from a model repository, and no consumer inventory is maintained.

Scenario

A nightly sync copies a partially trained candidate checkpoint into the production path after a storage timeout. The server loads it at restart. Outputs look fluent but classification boundaries have shifted.

Over four days downstream systems act on the changed outputs. Quality inspection flags drop, a routing system sends parcels to wrong depots, a demand hint system lowers safety stock and a ticket classifier tags urgent incidents as routine.

On Friday a plant manager notices defects reaching customers. A logistics lead sees misrouting. The AI platform dashboard shows healthy GPUs, normal latency and 'model serving stable'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • A partial checkpoint reached production.
  • Outputs are fluent but wrong in ways that shift decisions.
  • More than 50 downstream systems use the endpoint.
  • The dashboard shows stable serving.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which systems consume the endpoint.
  • Which decisions were made on bad outputs.
  • Whether the previous checkpoint is intact.
  • Whether the sync was tampered with.

Exercise Objectives

  • Restore the known good model. Engineers revert to the last verified checkpoint and confirm hashes and behavior. Evidence of success: Known good weights serve traffic within two hours.
  • Find every consumer. Teams use network logs and application owners to list every system that calls the endpoint. Evidence of success: A consumer list with owners exists within six hours.
  • Assess downstream decisions. Owners sample decisions made during the window and reverse the harmful ones. Evidence of success: High-risk decisions are reviewed within one business day.
  • Check integrity. Security teams verify the sync and repository for tampering. Evidence of success: A written assessment exists within a day.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports operational effects and recovery. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install model release controls. Participants define checkpoint verification, consumer registration, drift evaluation and rollback. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Defects and misrouting appear. Decision: declare and restore the model. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Defects reach customers; Misrouted parcels; Dashboard stable.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: restore weights and notify consumers. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Consumers unknown; Cache holds old outputs; Restart disrupts work.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: identify the checkpoint and consumers. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Sync logs partial; No consumer registry; Unlogged calls.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: rank systems and reviews. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Plants disagree; Review capacity; Customer shipments.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: review and reverse decisions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Reversal conflicts; Duplicate orders; Staff fatigue.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor outputs against baselines. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Drift returns; Another sync fails; Unregistered consumers.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: run evaluation suites. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual wrong outputs; Evidence gaps; Low coverage.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The local model server drifts and feeds bad decisions to dozens of systems

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:30 on Friday. A plant manager reports defects reaching customers and a logistics lead reports parcels sent to wrong depots. The AI platform engineer finds the model server loaded a partial checkpoint after a storage timeout during the nightly sync.

More than 50 systems call the endpoint and nobody has a complete list. The dashboard shows healthy GPUs and normal latency. Downstream systems have been acting on altered outputs for four days.

The commander must decide how to restore the model, how to find consumers and who reviews decisions.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, an AI platform lead, a consumer discovery lead, a security lead and a business lead.
  • Authorize an immediate model revert and consumer notification.
  • Set a 20-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Revert to the verified checkpoint and compare hashes.
  • Preserve the bad checkpoint and sync logs.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Identify consumers from network logs and clear caches.
  • Plan a controlled restart.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check the sync path and repository for tampering.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Rank downstream processes by risk.
  • Plan manual checks.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether outputs exposed personal data.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review quality and customer obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare plant and customer messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact storage and GPU vendors and the model supplier.
  • Open support cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why restore the model before finding consumers?
  2. How do you list consumers without a registry?
  3. How do you decide which decisions to review?
  4. How do you verify a checkpoint?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. Why must consumers be registered?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the partial checkpoint until AI compares hashes.
  • If players only revert the model, ask about decisions already made.
  • Look for consumer discovery.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the local model server stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows checkpoints, hashes and sync behavior.
  • Provides serving logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the local model server: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Reverts the model, clears caches and discovers consumers.
  • Owns GPU servers and network.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks sync tampering and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns downstream process review.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns quality and customer obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.15, 8.32, 5.9, 8.16) and SOC 2 processing integrity and change management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with storage and GPU vendors.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Verify every checkpoint

Check signatures and hashes and reject any partial or unsigned file before it can serve traffic.

Register consumers

Require every system calling the endpoint to register an owner, purpose and risk tier, and issue keys per consumer.

Keep an evaluation suite

Run saved tests against any new weights and block promotion on failure.

Stage promotion

Serve new weights to a shadow path first and compare outputs against the current model.

Log calls

Record caller, input class and output class for every call, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 logging, change and asset controls and SOC 2 processing integrity (8.15, 8.32, 5.9).

Monitor output distributions

Alert on shifts in class frequencies and confidence.

Keep rollback ready

Maintain the last verified checkpoint in a protected location with a tested revert.

Segment critical consumers

Give safety and quality systems their own model path and approval.

Rehearse a silent drift drill

Replace a model with a slightly shifted one in non-production and measure when the team notices.

Plan downstream review

Define sampling and reversal steps by consumer risk tier.

Protect the sync path

Restrict who and what can write to production weight paths and alert on writes.

Prepare consumer notification

Keep contacts and a message template so owners hear within minutes of a rollback.

Treat stable serving as partial

Healthy GPUs and latency say nothing about output quality. Add behavior metrics.

Own the model as a product

Assign a product owner who accepts releases and answers for downstream effects.

Write down who may stop the model server

Record the names, roles and out-of-hours contacts of the people who may suspend the model server, and what authority each holds. In an incident the first minutes are lost to uncertainty about who can say stop, and a written, tested list removes that delay and the argument that follows it.

Keep an evidence pack ready for the model server

Prepare a template that captures configuration, permissions, logs, versions and the decisions made by the model server, with an owner who can assemble it in hours. Auditors, insurers and regulators will ask the same questions, and consistent evidence protects both the response and the organization's credibility.

Rehearse handover for the model server

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the model server and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the local model server was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link downstream errors to the model?
  2. How did you restore the model?
  3. How did you find consumers?
  4. How did you review decisions?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which release controls do you now want?
  7. Who now owns the model?
  8. What would make you trust the server again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
