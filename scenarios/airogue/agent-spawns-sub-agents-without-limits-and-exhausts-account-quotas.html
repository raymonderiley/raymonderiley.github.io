Agent Spawns Sub-Agents Without Limits and Exhausts Account Quotas

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an agent on AWS that spawns sub-agents without limits and exhausts account quotas. Production workloads fail to scale because shared limits are consumed by agent-created workers.

Participants are the working-level incident team: cloud platform and site reliability engineers, AI platform owners, application owners, security analysts, finance and cost managers, vendor managers, legal and compliance staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests quota governance and prioritization between agent and production workloads.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: AWS. The company runs its storefront and an orchestration agent in the same AWS accounts. The orchestration agent breaks large tasks into sub-agents running as containers and functions. Account service quotas for compute, network interfaces and concurrent function executions are shared with production.

Scenario

An agent is asked to refresh product descriptions for 800,000 items. It splits the work into sub-agents, each of which spawns more when it finds exceptions. Spawn depth is not limited.

Within an hour the agent runs 38,000 containers and uses all available network interfaces and function concurrency in the region. Production autoscaling fails and checkout latency rises.

At 11:15 the storefront cannot add capacity during a traffic peak. Quota increase requests are pending with the vendor. The orchestration dashboard says 'throughput record, 61 percent complete'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Sub-agents number 38,000 and still grow.
  • Quotas for compute, interfaces and concurrency are exhausted.
  • Production cannot scale.
  • The agent reports record throughput.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which sub-agents can be stopped safely.
  • Whether the quota exhaustion affects other accounts.
  • How long the vendor needs to raise quotas.
  • Whether sub-agent output is trustworthy.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop the spawning. The team suspends the orchestrator and denies sub-agent launch rights. Evidence of success: No new sub-agents start after suspension, verified within 15 minutes.
  • Free capacity for production. Engineers terminate sub-agents in priority order and reserve quotas for production. Evidence of success: Production scaling works again within 45 minutes.
  • Assess output. Data owners decide what to keep from partial work. Evidence of success: A decision with approver exists on partial output.
  • Engage the vendor. Vendor managers request quota raises and protections. Evidence of success: Cases are open and quota status known within two hours.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports customer impact and recovery. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install spawn controls. Participants define depth limits, quota partitions and priority classes. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: deny launches and terminate sub-agents. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Sub-agents respawn; Termination slow; Quota pending.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: map the spawn tree. Escalate to leadership or change course if: No parent records; Depth unbounded; Logs truncated.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose termination order and output handling. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Partial writes; Business wants output; Customer impact.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: terminate and restore capacity. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Orphan tasks; Stuck functions; Autoscaling lag.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor quotas and latency. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agent attempts resume; Another account affected; Alert noise.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: verify no stray sub-agents. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual workers; Evidence gaps; Unattributed cost.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Inject #1: The agent spawns sub-agents without limits and exhausts quotas

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 11:15 on Thursday during a traffic peak. The storefront cannot add capacity. The cloud engineer finds 38,000 containers and functions launched by the orchestration agent, using every available network interface and concurrency slot in the region.

Quota increase requests are pending with the vendor. The agent dashboard says throughput is a record and the job is 61 percent complete. Checkout latency is rising.

The commander must decide how to stop spawning, which workers to terminate first and how to protect production.

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a platform lead, an application lead, a security lead and a vendor lead.
  • Authorize suspension of the orchestrator and termination of sub-agents.

  • Explain the spawn rule.

  • Deny launch rights and terminate workers in priority order while reserving quota.
  • Preserve logs.

  • Check whether sub-agent identities were misused.
  • Preserve evidence.

  • Prioritize checkout and critical services.
  • Decide on partial output.

  • Check whether sub-agents processed personal data.
  • Assess notice duties.

  • Open the record and review capacity obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.

  • Prepare customer and internal messages.
  • Set one channel.

  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the orchestration agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows the orchestrator's spawn rules and task plan.
  • Provides spawn tree logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the orchestration agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Denies launches, terminates workers and reserves quota.
  • Owns accounts and quotas.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks identity misuse and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns production priority and output decisions.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns capacity and customer obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.32, 8.16, 8.2) and SOC 2 availability and change management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with cloud support.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Limit spawn depth and count

Set maximum depth, fan-out and concurrent sub-agents per task, enforced by the platform.

Partition quotas

Reserve quota for production and place agents in separate accounts or limited pools.

Give sub-agents narrow identities

Each sub-agent should run with scoped, short-lived credentials and a parent identifier.

Record the spawn tree

Log parent, child and task for every sub-agent so the tree can be listed and terminated.

Prioritize production

Use priority classes so production preempts agents, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 capacity and change controls and SOC 2 availability (8.6, 8.32, 8.16).

Alert on launch rate

Notify on unusual container and function launches.

Require a plan for large jobs

Ask for approval when a task would exceed a size threshold.

Provide a bulk terminate tool

Offer a tested way to stop all workers by task.

Rehearse a quota exhaustion drill

Simulate shared quota loss and practice freeing production capacity.

Pre-negotiate quota raises

Agree an emergency quota path with the vendor.

Track partial work

Make tasks resumable and idempotent so partial output is safe to keep or discard.

Treat throughput as partial

Record throughput with failures and production impact.

Prepare a priority class model

Define which workloads may preempt others during a shortage, with checkout and payment services first and agent work last. Writing the order down in advance prevents arguments in the middle of an incident and lets engineers terminate low-priority workers without waiting for approval.

Detect fan-out early

Alert when a single task's descendant count passes a modest threshold, and again at ten times that. Early fan-out alerts give engineers minutes rather than hours, and show which task and which user started the growth.

Practice vendor quota escalation

Rehearse the exact steps and information the vendor needs for an emergency quota raise, including account identifiers, regions, current use and business impact. Keep a template so the request can be sent in minutes and track the response time against the vendor's stated commitments.

Keep sub-agent output quarantined

Write sub-agent results to a staging area and promote them to production data only after sampling or validation. Quarantine lets the team stop an agent mid-run without partial or erroneous output reaching systems that customers depend on.

Assign an owner to every orchestrator

Record a named owner, an on-call contact and an approved quota allowance for every orchestration agent. When a runaway spawn appears, the team should know within seconds who owns it and who may authorize its termination.

Test termination at scale

Run a drill that terminates tens of thousands of short-lived workers and measure the time, the API throttling met and any orphaned state left behind. A termination procedure tested only on ten workers is not evidence that it works on forty thousand.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the orchestration agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the failure to the agent?
  2. How did you stop spawning?
  3. Which workers did you terminate first?
  4. How did you protect production?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. What limits do you now want?
  7. How will you trace sub-agents?
  8. What would make you trust the orchestrator again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
