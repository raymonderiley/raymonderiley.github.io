Malicious Container Image Pulled by a Deployment Agent Spreads Across Clusters

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a deployment agent that pulls a malicious container image into Kubernetes clusters across several clouds. The image spreads, reaching clusters in Google Cloud, Azure and AWS before the first alert is understood.

Participants are the working-level incident team: platform and Kubernetes engineers, site reliability engineers, security analysts, application owners, privacy and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests supply chain response, cluster isolation and credential recovery at scale.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Kubernetes Across Multiple Clouds. The organization runs about 35 clusters on GKE, AKS and EKS. A deployment agent watches registries for new image versions, updates manifests and rolls them out, including a set of base images used by many teams. Images are pulled by tag rather than digest. Clusters share a few registry credentials and some nodes hold broad cloud roles.

Scenario

Pinecrest Digital Services let its deployment agent 'keep base images current for security'. The agent updates the tag used by manifests when a newer one appears in the registry it watches, and it pulls from two registries including a public one used for sidecar images.

A popular sidecar image in the public registry is replaced by an attacker with a version that contains a credential harvester and a tunnel to an outside server. The agent updates 90 workloads across 22 clusters within hours. The harvester reads service account tokens and cloud metadata, and uses them to launch pods in other namespaces and clusters.

At 14:00 on Wednesday, the cloud security team sees metadata service requests from pods that do not normally use them and traffic from nodes to an unknown address. The agent's report says 'security base images current across 22 clusters'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Images are pulled by tag, not digest.
  • A credential harvester runs in sidecars.
  • Tokens and node roles allow lateral movement.
  • The agent continues to update clusters.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which clusters and namespaces are affected.
  • Which credentials were taken.
  • Whether attacker pods exist in other clusters.
  • Whether data was reached through node roles.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop spread. The team halts the agent, blocks the image and the outside address and applies network policies to stop lateral movement. Evidence of success: No new pulls of the image occur and outbound tunnel traffic is blocked, verified within 30 minutes.
  • Find affected workloads and attacker footholds. Engineers locate all pods using the image and any attacker-created workloads or roles. Evidence of success: An inventory by cluster and namespace exists.
  • Rotate and rebuild. Teams rotate tokens, node roles and registry credentials and redeploy from verified digests. Evidence of success: Rotation and redeployment proceed in priority order.
  • Assess data and cloud impact. Security and owners check what the attacker accessed using stolen tokens. Evidence of success: A timeline and an access list exist.
  • Communicate carefully. Communications and the leadership liaison give accurate statements. Evidence of success: Messages are consistent and approved.
  • Redesign image and deployment controls. Participants set digest pinning, registry allow lists, admission control and least privilege. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Odd metadata requests are noticed. Decision: declare and stop the agent. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Metadata queries from unusual pods; Outbound tunnel from nodes; Unreviewed image update.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: halt rollout, block image and address, isolate namespaces. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Image cached on nodes; Attacker pods in other clusters; Shared credentials.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: inventory pods and trace token use. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Logs short; Cloud roles used; Multiple clusters.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose rotation and redeploy order. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Rotation breaks workloads; Dependencies unclear; Disagreement on order.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: redeploy from digests, rotate and remove footholds. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Attacker persistence; Outages; Evidence loss.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor admission, metadata and egress. Escalate to leadership or change course if: New pulls; Beaconing; Agent attempts resume.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: scan clusters and verify digests. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual images; Evidence gaps; Other registries affected.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: define agent exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early re-enablement; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The deployment agent pulls a malicious image that spreads across clusters

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 14:00 on Wednesday. The cloud security analyst sees metadata service requests from sidecar pods and traffic from nodes in three clouds to an unknown address. Cluster audit logs show the deployment agent updated an image tag across 22 clusters this morning.

The sidecar image was replaced in a public registry by an attacker. Pods in several namespaces run the image. Some nodes hold broad cloud roles. The agent's report says security base images current across 22 clusters.

The commander must decide how to stop the spread, how to find all affected workloads and how to rotate credentials without causing outages.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a Kubernetes lead for each cloud and a security lead.
  • Authorize halting the agent and blocking the image.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Suspend the agent and export registry sources, tags, digests and rollout history.
  • Explain how it selected the update.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Apply network policies and registry blocks, cordon affected nodes and preserve images.
  • Plan redeployment by digest.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Analyze the image and token use.
  • Check for attacker-created pods and roles.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify critical workloads among affected ones.
  • Plan continuity.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check what data the stolen tokens could reach.
  • Assess notice triggers.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and capture registry and tag policy.
  • Review change control.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare a holding statement.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Report to the registry and cloud providers.
  • Ask for registry audit logs.

Discussion questions

  1. Why stop the agent before the full inventory?
  2. How do you find every pod running the image?
  3. What do you rotate first?
  4. How do you deal with attacker-created workloads?
  5. What do you tell leaders about the scale?
  6. Why must images be pulled by digest?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the node role reach until SEC reviews cloud logs.
  • If players only delete pods, ask about tokens.
  • Look for lateral movement thinking.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the deployment agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows registries watched, tags used and how rollouts are triggered.
  • Provides rollout history.
  • Owns the technical facts about the deployment agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Applies network policies, blocks images and redeploys by digest.
  • Owns clusters.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Analyzes the image and token use and hunts footholds.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Prioritizes workloads and continuity.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Maps registry policy and change control.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.21, 8.28, 8.20, 8.16) and SOC 2 change management and security).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the registry and cloud providers.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Pull by digest from approved registries

Pin every image by digest, allow pulls only from reviewed internal registries and block public pulls in production clusters.

Enforce admission control

Use policy engines to require signed images and block privileged or unknown ones.

Reduce token and node role power

Use workload identity with narrow roles and block pod access to node metadata.

Control agent update authority

Image update agents should open reviewed changes instead of rolling out directly, with canaries before broad deployment.

Monitor runtime behavior

Alert on unexpected metadata calls, new outbound destinations and privileged pods, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 supplier and monitoring controls and SOC 2 security criteria.

Segment clusters

Do not share credentials across clusters or clouds, and keep registry credentials separate per environment.

Maintain an image inventory

Know every image and base layer in production and where it came from.

Rehearse cluster compromise

Practice isolating a namespace and rebuilding a cluster from verified sources.

Treat the registry as a trust boundary

Mirror every external image into an internal registry only after scanning, signing and a named owner approves it, and give clusters pull rights to the mirror alone. When an upstream image changes unexpectedly, the mirror holds the old digest and raises a ticket instead of passing the change into production clusters automatically.

Practice cluster rebuilds

Rehearse rebuilding a cluster from infrastructure code and verified digests, including restoring stateful data and rotating every credential it held. Time the rebuild, note which steps needed tribal knowledge and fix them. A team that has rebuilt a cluster once under controlled conditions behaves very differently when it must do it during an incident.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the deployment agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the metadata requests to the image?
  2. How did you stop the spread?
  3. How did you find all affected pods?
  4. What did you rotate first?
  5. What did leaders hear about scale?
  6. What image controls do you now want?
  7. What would have blocked the update?
  8. How do you rebuild with confidence?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
