Voice Assistant Cloning Attack Approves Fraudulent Changes Through the Service Desk

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a voice cloning attack in which a cloned voice and an automated voice assistant persuade the service desk to approve fraudulent changes across Azure identity and on-premises systems, including a password reset and a new multi-factor device.

Participants are the working-level incident team: service desk and identity engineers, Azure and on-premises administrators, security analysts, application owners, privacy and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests identity verification, social engineering resilience and recovery from account takeover.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Azure & On-Premises. The organization uses Entra ID with hybrid accounts synced from on-premises Active Directory. The service desk resets passwords and registers authentication methods after identity checks that rely on a caller's name, employee number and a callback to the number in the directory. An automated voice assistant helps with first-line triage and can open and update tickets.

Scenario

The company recently expanded its service desk to a partner with a high turnover. The verification script is a short list of questions whose answers appear in social media and past breach data. The callback number is stored in a directory attribute that users can edit through a self-service portal.

An attacker edits the callback number for a finance director, then calls the desk using a cloned voice built from a conference video. The automated assistant collects details and passes a ticket marked 'verified by voice pattern' to an agent, who resets the password and registers a new authentication device. Within an hour the attacker approves a payment batch and creates an inbox rule forwarding mail.

The real finance director calls the help desk on Thursday morning to report that she cannot sign in. The desk sees a password reset overnight. The automated assistant's record says 'identity verified, request completed successfully'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The callback number attribute was edited by the user's own account.
  • The assistant labeled the call as verified based on voice similarity.
  • A new authentication device was registered for the account.
  • A payment batch was approved from the account.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which other accounts were reset through the same path.
  • What the attacker accessed.
  • Whether the payment can be recalled.
  • Whether the callback attribute was edited for others.

Exercise Objectives

  • Contain the account takeover. The team revokes sessions, removes attacker devices, resets credentials with strong verification and blocks the forwarding rule. Evidence of success: The compromised account is secured and attacker access removed within 20 minutes.
  • Trace the attacker's actions. Security reviews sign-ins, mailbox rules, approvals and file access. Evidence of success: A timeline of attacker actions exists with gaps stated.
  • Find other victims. Teams look for other recent resets, device registrations and callback edits. Evidence of success: A list of at-risk accounts is created and secured.
  • Recover funds and notify. Finance and the bank work to recall the payment, and counsel assesses notification duties. Evidence of success: A recall request is filed with the bank and decisions are recorded.
  • Communicate carefully. Communications and the leadership liaison give accurate statements to staff and partners. Evidence of success: Messages are consistent and approved.
  • Redesign identity verification. Participants set verification that does not rely on voice, remove editable callback numbers and constrain automated assistants. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Real user reports lockout. Decision: declare and secure the account. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Overnight password reset; New device registration; Unusual approvals.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: revoke sessions, remove devices and stop the assistant from verifying identity. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Forwarding rules; Attacker uses other accounts; Callback edits for others.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: trace the attacker and find similar attempts. Escalate to leadership or change course if: More resets found; Data accessed; Payments approved.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: recall payment, notify and fix verification. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Bank cutoff times; Notice duties; Partner desk resistance.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: secure accounts, file recalls and retrain staff. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Recall fails; Other accounts compromised; Desk workload surges.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor resets, devices and approvals. Escalate to leadership or change course if: New social engineering calls; Callback edits; Assistant attempts verification.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: test the new process with red team calls. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Bypass found; Evidence gaps; Partner non-compliance.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: define assistant exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early re-enablement; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: A cloned voice and automated assistant trick the service desk into an account takeover

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:10 on Thursday. The finance director reports that she cannot sign in. The service desk finds a password reset and a new authentication device registered at 22:40 the night before, with a ticket marked verified by voice pattern.

Audit logs show the callback number attribute was changed two hours earlier from the user's own account. Finance reports that a payment batch was approved from the director's account at 23:30. A mailbox rule forwards invoices to an outside address.

The commander must decide how to secure the account, how to recall the payment and how to find other accounts reset through the same path.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, an identity lead, a finance lead and a security lead.
  • Authorize securing the account and stopping voice-based verification.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Stop the assistant from marking calls as verified and export its call records and verification logic.
  • Explain how it judged voice similarity.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Revoke sessions, remove devices and reset credentials with strong in-person or video verification.
  • Review other recent resets.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Trace attacker actions in sign-ins, mail and approvals.
  • Preserve recordings and logs.
Business & Process Owners
  • Contact the bank to recall the payment and hold related batches.
  • Identify exposed vendors.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check what personal or financial data the attacker accessed.
  • Assess notice triggers.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and capture who approved the verification script.
  • Review service desk policy.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare a staff reminder and a vendor message.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the service desk partner and Microsoft.
  • Ask for call recordings and agent identities.

Discussion questions

  1. Why stop voice verification before finishing the investigation?
  2. How do you find other accounts reset the same way?
  3. What can you do about the payment today?
  4. How should callback numbers be protected?
  5. What do you tell vendors who got an approval?
  6. Why is voice similarity not an identity check?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the callback edit until INF reviews attribute changes.
  • If players only reset the password, ask about the device and mail rule.
  • Look for payment recall and wider sweep.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the voice assistant stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows how the assistant gathers details and labels verification.
  • Provides call records and logic.
  • Owns the technical facts about the voice assistant: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Secures accounts, locks attributes and reviews resets.
  • Owns identity configuration.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Traces attacker actions and preserves recordings.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Handles payment recall and interim service desk process.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Maps service desk policy and contract terms.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.16, 6.3, 5.17, 8.5) and SOC 2 logical access).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the service desk partner and Microsoft.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Do not verify identity by voice

Use methods that cannot be cloned, such as a manager approval with a known channel, a hardware token or in-person or video verification with document checks, for any credential reset.

Lock identity attributes

Callback numbers, recovery emails and device registrations should be changed only through strongly authenticated flows, with alerts to the user and manager.

Constrain automated assistants

Assistants may collect details and open tickets but may not label identity as verified or perform resets.

Add friction for sensitive resets

Apply a waiting period and notification for resets on finance and privileged accounts, with second approval.

Monitor post-reset behavior

Alert on new devices, mailbox rules and payment approvals within 24 hours of a reset, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 access and awareness controls and SOC 2 logical access criteria.

Hold partners to the standard

Include verification requirements, recording retention and audit rights in service desk contracts and test them with red team calls.

Train for synthetic media

Brief staff and leaders on voice and video cloning and agree a family of safe words and call-back procedures for urgent requests.

Protect payment approvals

Require dual approval and out-of-band confirmation for payment batches regardless of who requests them.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the voice assistant was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team realize the account was taken over?
  2. How did you stop voice-based verification?
  3. How did you find other victims?
  4. What could you do about the payment?
  5. What did vendors hear?
  6. What verification controls do you now want?
  7. What would have made the desk say no?
  8. How should an assistant be allowed to touch identity processes?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
