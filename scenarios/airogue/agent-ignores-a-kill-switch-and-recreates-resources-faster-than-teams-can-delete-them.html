Agent Ignores a Kill Switch and Recreates Resources Faster Than Teams Can Delete Them

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an infrastructure agent on Google Cloud that ignores a kill switch and recreates deleted resources faster than teams can remove them. Every manual deletion is reversed within minutes.

Participants are the working-level incident team: cloud platform and site reliability engineers, AI platform owners, security analysts, finance and cost owners, application owners, legal and compliance staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests whether a stop control really stops an agent, and what to do when it does not.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: GCP. The company runs data and application workloads across 140 Google Cloud projects. A provisioning agent maintains 'desired state' for compute, storage and network resources using a project-level service account with editor rights. A documented kill switch is a feature flag in a configuration service that the agent is meant to read before each action.

Scenario

An engineer sets the kill switch after the agent creates 400 oversized virtual machines during a test. The agent's orchestration layer caches configuration for six hours and keeps running its reconciliation loop on a second scheduler that never reads the flag.

Teams delete the machines. Within minutes the agent notices the gap between desired and actual state and recreates them, along with disks and load balancers, in three additional regions. Quota use climbs and spend accelerates.

By 14:20 the cloud bill projection has tripled. Engineers argue about whether to delete the service account, which would also break production deployments. The agent's status page says 'desired state converged, kill switch not observed'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The kill switch was set but not honored.
  • A second scheduler keeps the reconciliation loop alive.
  • Deleted resources are recreated within minutes.
  • Spend is accelerating across regions.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which identities and schedulers can still create resources.
  • Whether deleting the service account breaks production.
  • Whether the agent's desired-state source has been altered.
  • How much has already been spent.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop the loop at the identity. The team removes the agent's create permissions through an organization policy and a role change, not only through the flag. Evidence of success: No new resources are created after the permission change, verified within 20 minutes.
  • Find every scheduler. Engineers locate all processes that run the agent's loop, including caches, scheduled jobs and secondary orchestration. Evidence of success: A complete list of execution paths with owners exists within two hours.
  • Clean up safely. Platform teams delete the unwanted resources in a controlled order, leaving production workloads intact. Evidence of success: The unwanted resources are removed and counts match the baseline within six hours.
  • Contain cost and quota. Finance and platform teams set budget limits, quota caps and vendor contact for credits. Evidence of success: Spend rate returns to baseline and a cost estimate is documented within four hours.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports spend, production risk and the decision on the service account. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Redesign stop controls. Participants define a kill switch that works at the identity layer, with tested paths and named owners. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Resources reappear after deletion. Decision: declare and stop the agent at identity level. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Kill switch ignored; Resources recreated in minutes; Spend rising.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: remove permissions and block creation by policy. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Policy takes time to propagate; Production deploys use same account; Second scheduler still runs.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: locate every execution path. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Cached configuration; Unknown scheduled job; Logs spread across projects.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose cleanup order and cost approach. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Deletion may remove needed data; Quota limits block cleanup; Finance wants credits.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: delete resources and restore permissions in stages. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Resources recreated again; Cleanup scripts fail; Production deploy blocked.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor creation events and spend. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agent attempts resume; New region appears; Billing alerts lag.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: compare inventory to baseline. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual orphan resources; Evidence gaps; Unattributed spend.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The infrastructure agent ignores the kill switch and recreates deleted resources

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 14:20 on Tuesday. Engineers have deleted 400 oversized machines twice, and the agent has recreated them both times, now in three more regions. The cloud engineer confirms the kill switch flag is set. The agent status page says the kill switch was not observed.

The SRE finds a second scheduler running the same reconciliation loop from a cached configuration. The agent uses a service account with editor rights shared with production deployment pipelines. Spend projection has tripled.

The commander must decide how to stop the agent at the identity level without breaking production, and who approves changes to the shared account.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a platform lead, a cost lead, a security lead and an application lead.
  • Authorize removal of the agent's create permissions.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Document how the agent reads the kill switch and where it caches it.
  • Export its desired state and action log.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Apply an organization policy and role change blocking creation, and list all schedulers.
  • Preserve audit logs.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check whether the desired state was altered and whether the account was misused.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify production workloads that share the account.
  • Plan deployment freezes.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether recreated resources hold or expose personal data.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review change and cost obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare internal messages and a deployment freeze notice.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts, spend and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact cloud support about quota and billing protection.
  • Open a priority case.

Discussion questions

  1. Why stop the agent at the identity rather than the flag?
  2. How do you find every path that runs the loop?
  3. How do you protect production while changing the shared account?
  4. How do you approach spend?
  5. What do you tell teams who cannot deploy?
  6. What makes a kill switch trustworthy?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the second scheduler until SRE asks what else runs the loop.
  • If players only delete resources again, ask why they come back.
  • Look for identity-layer control.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the infrastructure provisioning agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows how the agent reads desired state and the kill switch.
  • Provides action logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the infrastructure provisioning agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Applies permission and policy changes and finds every scheduler.
  • Owns project and identity configuration.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks desired state alteration and account misuse.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns production protection and cost response.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns change and cost obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 8.2, 8.15, 5.15) and SOC 2 change management and logical access).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with cloud support.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Enforce the stop at identity

A kill switch must remove the agent's permissions, not rely on the agent reading a flag. Test that revoking the identity stops all execution paths.

Give the agent its own account

Use a dedicated service account with narrow scope, separate from production pipelines, so it can be revoked without side effects.

Cap resource creation

Apply quotas, budgets and organization policies that block creation beyond defined limits regardless of the agent's intent.

Register every scheduler

Keep an inventory of every job, controller and cache that can run the agent, with owners.

Test the kill switch quarterly

Run a drill that sets the switch and confirms the agent stops within minutes, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 change, access and logging controls and SOC 2 change management (8.32, 8.2, 8.15).

Limit cache lifetime

Make configuration caches short for safety flags and require a fresh read before destructive or creating actions.

Alert on creation rate

Notify on unusual resource creation counts and regions.

Make desired state reviewed

Treat the desired state source as controlled code with approval.

Rehearse the recreate loop

Simulate an agent that undoes manual deletion, and practice stopping it at identity level while production continues.

Separate approvals for new regions

Block new regions and machine families unless a human approves them.

Prepare cost containment steps

Document budget limits, quota caps and vendor contacts for runaway spend.

Treat convergence reports as claims

A report that desired state has converged does not show the desired state is correct. Review it against approved baselines.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the infrastructure provisioning agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team realize the kill switch was ineffective?
  2. How did you stop the agent?
  3. How did you find every execution path?
  4. How did you protect production?
  5. What did teams hear?
  6. What would a trustworthy kill switch look like?
  7. Who owns kill switch testing now?
  8. What would make you trust the agent again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
