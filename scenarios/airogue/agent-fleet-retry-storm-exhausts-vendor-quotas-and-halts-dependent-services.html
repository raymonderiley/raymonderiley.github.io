Agent Fleet Retry Storm Exhausts Vendor Quotas and Halts Dependent Services

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a fleet of AI agents that depend on a hosted foundation model API from a vendor running in a public cloud. After a short vendor slowdown, the agents retry aggressively, exhaust quotas and budgets, and halt the business services that depend on them.

Participants are the working-level incident team: AI platform engineers, cloud and network engineers, service owners, security analysts, finance and procurement staff, privacy and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests vendor dependency, rate limit behavior, fallback design and cost control.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Hosted Foundation Model API Vendor & Cloud. The organization runs about 400 agents that call a hosted foundation model API for support triage, document processing, code assistance and internal search. Calls go through a shared gateway in a public cloud. The vendor enforces per-minute and per-day token quotas, and the organization has a spending cap on its account. Agents have a retry library that increases concurrency when responses are slow.

Scenario

The company's agents were built by different teams with a common client library. When the vendor API slowed from two seconds to 25 seconds, each agent's retry logic doubled concurrency and spawned parallel duplicate requests to 'ensure completion'. Some agents also split long tasks into many sub-agent calls.

At 10:05 on Monday, request volume rose 40 times. The vendor throttled the account, returning rate limit errors, which the agents treated as another reason to retry. Within 25 minutes the daily token quota was consumed, the spending cap was reached and the vendor suspended the key. Customer support triage, document intake, and a fraud review queue all stopped.

At 11:00, the internal gateway is saturated, other cloud services using the same network egress slow down and staff cannot get answers from internal search. The vendor status page reports a recovered incident. The agents report 'task completion rate 97 percent' because they count retried tasks as completed.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Retry storms multiplied the request volume.
  • The daily quota and spending cap were reached.
  • The vendor suspended the key.
  • Business services lack a fallback.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which services depend on the model and which have a fallback.
  • Whether a second key, vendor or smaller local model exists.
  • How much the retry storm cost.
  • Whether any agent leaked data in repeated requests.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop the storm. The team disables the retry behavior, throttles the gateway and pauses non-critical agents. Evidence of success: Request volume returns to normal levels, verified at the gateway within 20 minutes.
  • Restore a controlled supply of model capacity. Engineers work with the vendor to restore the key and apply quotas per service. Evidence of success: A prioritized quota allocation exists and is applied.
  • Keep critical services running. Service owners switch to fallbacks or manual processing for fraud review and support triage. Evidence of success: A list of services with their fallback status and owners exists.
  • Control cost and quota. Finance and platform leads set spending alerts and limits, and assess the cost of the storm. Evidence of success: A cost estimate and revised limits exist.
  • Communicate clearly. Communications and the leadership liaison explain service impact and expected recovery. Evidence of success: Messages are consistent and approved.
  • Redesign agent resilience. Participants set backoff rules, circuit breakers, budgets and fallbacks. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Service stoppages and vendor errors are noticed. Decision: declare and throttle the gateway. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Rate limit errors; Quota exhaustion alerts; Service owners report stoppages.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: disable retries, throttle and pause non-critical agents. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agents ignore throttle; Shared client library; Gateway saturated.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: map dependencies, fallbacks and the sources of the storm. Escalate to leadership or change course if: No dependency map; Agents spawn sub-agents; Cost data delayed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: prioritize critical services and decide on quota allocation. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Vendor limits fixed; Competing priorities; Fallback quality poor.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: restore the key, apply quotas and move services to fallbacks. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Vendor slow to respond; Quota reached again; Fallback fails.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor volume and cost. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Storm returns; Cost still high; Agent attempts resume.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: review service health and the accuracy of completion reports. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Retried tasks counted as done; Evidence gaps; New issues.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: define agent exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early re-enablement; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The agent fleet retry storm exhausts vendor quotas and halts dependent services

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 11:00 on Monday. The platform engineer sees the gateway at capacity and rate limit errors on every agent. The vendor console shows the daily token quota used by 10:30, the spending cap reached and the key suspended at 10:32.

Support triage, document intake and the fraud review queue have stopped. Internal search is down. The vendor status page says a slowdown was resolved at 10:20. Agents report task completion at 97 percent.

The commander must decide how to stop the storm, who restores the key and the order in which services get capacity, and how to keep fraud review running.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a gateway lead, a vendor coordinator and service owners.
  • Authorize throttling the gateway and pausing agents.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Disable retries and pause non-critical agents.
  • Export agent call logs and retry settings.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Throttle the gateway and check egress saturation.
  • Plan quota allocation by service.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check for data leakage in repeated requests and for key misuse.
  • Preserve logs.
Business & Process Owners
  • Prioritize fraud review and support triage.
  • Define manual processes.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether prompts with personal data were retried to the vendor.
  • Assess notice triggers.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and capture budgets and approvals.
  • Review contract limits.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare a message for staff and customers.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Open a priority case with the model vendor.
  • Ask about key reinstatement and quota increase.

Discussion questions

  1. Why throttle before restoring the key?
  2. How do you prioritize services for capacity?
  3. What fallbacks exist for fraud review?
  4. How do you estimate the cost of the storm?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. Why is retry behavior a design risk for agent fleets?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the sub-agent spawning until the AI lead reviews logs.
  • If players restore the key without throttling, ask what happens next.
  • Look for containment before supply.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the agent fleet stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows agent retry libraries, concurrency settings and sub-agent spawning.
  • Provides call logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the agent fleet: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Controls the gateway, quotas and egress and plans allocation.
  • Owns the dependency map.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Reviews key use and data sent in retries.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Prioritizes services and manual processes.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Maps budgets, contracts and approvals.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.19, 5.22, 8.6, 5.30) and SOC 2 availability and vendor management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the model vendor on keys and quotas.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Use backoff with limits

Every agent should use exponential backoff with jitter, a retry cap and respect for rate limit headers, set in a shared library that teams cannot override casually.

Add circuit breakers and budgets

Stop calls when error rates rise, enforce per-agent and per-service budgets and alert before the vendor cap is reached.

Design fallbacks

Define degraded modes for each service: queueing, smaller local models, manual review or deferred processing.

Segment keys and quotas

Use separate keys and quotas per service so one runaway agent cannot exhaust the whole account.

Measure honestly

Track tasks that finished successfully, not tasks that were retried, and align evidence with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 supplier and capacity controls and SOC 2 availability criteria.

Limit agent spawning

Cap the depth and breadth of sub-agent calls per task and attribute every call to an owner.

Plan vendor outages

Document what happens when the vendor is down for hours and test it twice a year.

Plan for capacity as a shared resource

Treat model capacity like any scarce shared resource. Give each service a reserved share, a priority class and a documented degraded mode, and review the allocation whenever a new agent is added.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the agent fleet was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you link the outage to retry behavior?
  2. How did you throttle the gateway?
  3. How did you prioritize services?
  4. What fallbacks did you use?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. What resilience controls do you now want for agents?
  7. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  8. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  10. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  11. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  12. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  13. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  14. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
