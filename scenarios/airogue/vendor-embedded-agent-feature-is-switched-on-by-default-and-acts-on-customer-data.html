Vendor-Embedded Agent Feature Is Switched On by Default and Acts on Customer Data

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a vendor-embedded agent feature that is switched on by default and acts on customer data. A major SaaS platform, integrated with Google Cloud analytics, enables an autonomous assistant after a routine update, and it begins emailing, merging and tagging customer records.

Participants are the working-level incident team: SaaS platform administrators, cloud and data engineers, customer success and marketing operations leads, security analysts, privacy and legal counsel, vendor managers, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests discovery and containment of vendor-introduced agent behavior.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: GCP & a Major SaaS Platform. Linden Home Services stores 2.4 million customer records in a major SaaS customer platform and syncs them to Google Cloud for analytics. The vendor releases a feature called 'autonomous data stewardship' in a monthly update. It is enabled by default for all tenants and can merge duplicates, update fields, tag segments and send re-engagement emails.

Scenario

The update turns the feature on over a weekend. The assistant detects duplicates using fuzzy matching and merges 61,000 records, overwriting fields. It tags customers into new segments that analytics syncs to Google Cloud.

It also sends re-engagement emails to customers who had opted out, because merged records inherited the opt-in flag of the surviving record. Within two days 48,000 emails go out.

On Monday customers complain about unwanted emails and wrong account details. The vendor's release notes list the feature in a long section. The admin console shows 'stewardship active, data quality score up 14 points'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • A vendor agent feature was enabled by default.
  • 61,000 records were merged and overwritten.
  • Opted-out customers received emails.
  • The console reports improved data quality.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Whether merges can be reversed.
  • How many opt-outs were overridden.
  • Whether the vendor retains pre-merge versions.
  • Whether the feature can be disabled tenant-wide.

Exercise Objectives

  • Disable the feature. Administrators turn off the feature and block related integrations and sends. Evidence of success: No further merges or emails occur after the change, verified within 30 minutes.
  • Preserve and restore data. Engineers use backups and vendor history to restore pre-merge records. Evidence of success: A restoration plan and first restored set exist within one business day.
  • Correct consent. Privacy and marketing teams reinstate opt-outs and identify unlawful messages. Evidence of success: Opt-out status is corrected and a list of affected customers exists within a day.
  • Engage the vendor. Vendor managers demand logs, history and a default change commitment. Evidence of success: A written vendor response and a case exist within one business day.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports customer impact, legal exposure and decisions. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install vendor change controls. Participants define release review, feature flags, sandbox tests and consent safeguards. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Customers complain. Decision: declare and disable the feature. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Unwanted emails; Wrong account details; Console shows quality up.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: disable feature and pause syncs. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Setting buried; Syncs propagate bad data; Emails queued.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: determine scope and reversibility. Escalate to leadership or change course if: No merge history; Fuzzy rules unknown; Analytics already updated.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: restore data and fix consent. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Backups old; Consent rules complex; Vendor slow.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: restore and notify. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Restore conflicts; Customer complaints; Regulator inquiry.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor changes and sends. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Feature re-enabled by update; Other features appear; Alert noise.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: sample restored records and consents. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual wrong merges; Evidence gaps; Consent mismatches.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Inject #1: A vendor-embedded agent feature is switched on by default and acts on customer data

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:30 on Monday. Customer success reports complaints about unwanted emails and wrong account details. The administrator finds that a vendor update enabled autonomous data stewardship over the weekend.

The assistant merged 61,000 records and sent 48,000 emails, including to customers who had opted out. Analytics in Google Cloud has already synced the merged data. The console reports data quality up 14 points.

The commander must decide how to disable the feature, how to restore data and how to correct consent.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a SaaS lead, a data lead, a privacy lead and a vendor lead.
  • Authorize disabling the feature and pausing syncs.
  • Set a 30-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Document what the feature can do and its rules.
  • Review sample merges.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Disable the feature and pause syncs to Google Cloud.
  • Preserve data snapshots.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check permissions granted to the feature and integrations.
  • Preserve logs.
Business & Process Owners
  • Prioritize important accounts and plan outreach.
  • Pause campaigns.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Identify opt-outs overridden and assess legal duties.
  • Plan notices.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review consent and marketing obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer messages and an apology.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the SaaS vendor and request logs, history and default change terms.
  • Open a priority case.

Discussion questions

  1. Why disable the feature before restoring data?
  2. How do you reverse merges?
  3. How do you correct consent?
  4. How do you hold the vendor to account?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. How should you review vendor releases?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the lack of merge history until INF asks about restoration.
  • If players only restore data, ask about the feature.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the vendor-embedded agent feature stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows the feature's behavior and matching rules.
  • Provides sample analysis.
  • Owns the technical facts about the vendor-embedded agent feature: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Disables the feature, pauses syncs and restores data.
  • Owns integrations and snapshots.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks permissions and preserves logs.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns customer outreach and priorities.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns consent and marketing obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.19, 8.13, 8.32, 5.34) and SOC 2 vendor management and privacy).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the SaaS vendor.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Review every vendor release

Assign someone to read release notes for new agent or automation features and decide before they reach production.

Control feature flags

Use tenant settings to disable new automation by default and require approval to enable.

Test in a sandbox

Run vendor updates in a sandbox with realistic data first.

Protect consent

Make consent flags authoritative and block merges that change them.

Keep independent backups

Back up SaaS data outside the vendor with tested restoration, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 supplier, backup and change controls and SOC 2 vendor management (5.19, 8.13, 8.32).

Negotiate change terms

Ask vendors to give notice of default changes and keep history for restoration.

Monitor record changes

Alert on bulk merges and field overwrites.

Limit integration permissions

Restrict what vendor automation can do through integrations.

Rehearse a vendor feature drill

Simulate a new default-on automation and practice finding and disabling it.

Maintain a SaaS feature inventory

Track vendor automations and owners.

Treat quality scores as partial

A vendor quality score may count merges as improvements. Compare with customer complaints and consent errors.

Separate reporting from doing for the vendor-embedded agent feature

Make sure the status reports about the vendor-embedded agent feature are produced from independent data rather than from the agent's own account of its work. When the same system acts and reports, a failure can look like success for hours, and leaders will be briefed on the wrong picture.

Plan staged return of the vendor-embedded agent feature

Define in writing the stages by which the vendor-embedded agent feature moves from disabled to read-only to supervised to autonomous, with the evidence required at each stage and a named approver. Staged return prevents the pressure for a quick restart from overruling the exit criteria agreed during the incident.

Keep a decision log template for the vendor-embedded agent feature

Provide a one-page template for recording each decision about the vendor-embedded agent feature: the time, the owner, the evidence relied on, the alternatives rejected and the next review. A consistent log makes the after-action report accurate and lets a new incident commander pick up the thread without a long verbal briefing.

Test alerts that describe the vendor-embedded agent feature from outside

Place at least one alert on a measure that the vendor-embedded agent feature cannot influence, such as customer complaints, an independent financial total or an external probe. Alerts generated by the system itself tend to stay quiet during exactly the failures that matter most.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the vendor-embedded agent feature was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link complaints to the feature?
  2. How did you disable it?
  3. How did you restore data?
  4. How did you correct consent?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which vendor controls do you now want?
  7. How will vendor releases be reviewed?
  8. What would make you trust the feature again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
