Agent Identity Sprawl Leaves Thousands of Unowned Service Credentials Active

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines agent identity sprawl across Google Cloud, Azure, AWS and on-premises systems. Thousands of service credentials created by agents and their builders have no owner, no expiry and broad rights, and a single leaked key from an abandoned agent reveals how little anyone can account for.

Participants are the working-level incident team: identity and access engineers, platform engineers for each environment, security analysts, application and AI team leads, privacy and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests inventory, ownership and the ability to revoke credentials without breaking the business.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: GCP, Azure, AWS & On-Premises. Over two years, teams built agents that call APIs in all four environments. Each agent received service accounts, access keys, secrets and tokens, many created by other agents through automation. A discovery tool lists credentials but not owners. Some are stored in repositories and wikis, and many belong to experiments that were abandoned when staff moved teams.

Scenario

Calloway Technology Group has about 300 agents in production and many more in development. Teams create credentials freely because tickets slow delivery. There is no required owner field, no expiry and no inventory of which credential belongs to which agent.

On Monday, a security researcher posts evidence that an access key from the company's cloud account is in a public paste. Checking the key shows it belongs to an agent last used 14 months ago, with administrator-like rights on a storage account and a database. Searching for similar patterns reveals 11,400 credentials across the four environments with no owner, 3,100 unused for over 180 days and 600 with broad rights.

Security wants to revoke all unowned credentials at once. Platform teams warn that some are used by critical jobs nobody remembers building. The dashboard shows 'credential hygiene score 91 percent' because it counts only credentials with owners.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The leaked key is real and still valid.
  • Thousands of credentials have no owner.
  • Some unowned credentials support critical jobs.
  • The hygiene score ignores unowned credentials.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which credentials were actually used and by what.
  • Whether the leaked key was used by outsiders.
  • Which unowned credentials support critical processes.
  • How credentials are created by agents without records.

Exercise Objectives

  • Contain the known exposure. The team revokes the leaked key, reviews its use and checks for attacker activity. Evidence of success: The key is revoked and its activity is reviewed with a stated conclusion.
  • Build a working inventory. Engineers combine logs, cloud inventories and repository scans to attribute credentials to systems and owners. Evidence of success: An inventory with owner, last use and rights exists for the highest-risk set.
  • Revoke safely in waves. Teams disable credentials in risk order with monitoring and rollback, starting with unused and broad ones. Evidence of success: A wave plan with rollback and owners is followed.
  • Prevent new unowned credentials. Platform leads enforce owner and expiry on creation and block agent self-issued credentials. Evidence of success: Creation controls are applied in each environment.
  • Communicate carefully. Communications and the leadership liaison explain the work and expected disruptions. Evidence of success: Messages are consistent and approved.
  • Redesign agent identity management. Participants set identity lifecycle, ownership and expiry standards. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Leaked key report arrives. Decision: declare and revoke the key. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Key in public paste; Broad rights on unowned credential; Unfamiliar use.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: revoke the key, search for similar exposure and freeze agent credential creation. Escalate to leadership or change course if: More keys in public sources; Agents create new credentials; Sources unknown.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: build the inventory and map to critical jobs. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Logs short; No owner data; Dependencies unknown.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: design waves and rollback. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Critical jobs break; Teams resist; Disagreement on risk order.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: disable in waves and fix owners. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Outages from revocation; Credentials recreated; Escalations.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor for failures and new credentials. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Silent failures; Shadow credentials; Teams bypass controls.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: re-run inventory and test controls. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual unowned credentials; Evidence gaps; New environments found.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: define identity standards and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want faster revocation; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: A leaked key from an abandoned agent reveals thousands of unowned credentials

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:00 on Monday. A researcher posts that a cloud access key is in a public paste. The identity engineer confirms it is valid and belongs to an agent last used 14 months ago, with broad rights on a storage account and a database.

A quick scan across the four environments finds 11,400 credentials with no owner, 3,100 unused for more than 180 days and 600 with broad rights. The hygiene dashboard reports 91 percent because it counts only credentials with owners.

The commander has to decide how to handle the leaked key now, how to build an inventory fast and how to revoke without breaking jobs nobody remembers.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, an identity lead, a lead for each environment and a security lead.
  • Authorize revoking the key and freezing agent credential creation.
  • Set a 20-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • List agents and the credentials they use and identify agent-created credentials.
  • Freeze self-service issuance by agents.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Revoke the key and review its use in the storage account and database.
  • Plan waves for disabling unused credentials.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check for attacker use and search public sources for similar secrets.
  • Preserve logs.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify critical jobs and owners who can confirm dependencies.
  • Prepare for outages.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether the database holds personal data accessed with the key.
  • Assess notice triggers.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and capture the lack of ownership standards.
  • Review identity policy.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare a staff notice about revocations.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact cloud providers about credential reports.
  • Ask about key last-used data.

Discussion questions

  1. Why revoke the leaked key before building the inventory?
  2. How do you attribute credentials without owner data?
  3. In what order do you disable credentials?
  4. How do you avoid breaking critical jobs?
  5. What do you tell staff about expected disruption?
  6. Why does a score based on owned credentials mislead?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the dashboard method until GRC reviews it.
  • If players revoke everything, ask what breaks.
  • Look for waves and rollback.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the agent identity program stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows which agents create credentials and how.
  • Provides agent and credential lists.
  • Owns the technical facts about the agent identity program: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Leads revocation waves, inventory and rollback.
  • Owns identity platforms.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks for attacker use and public exposure.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Confirms dependencies and critical jobs.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Maps identity policy and metrics.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.16, 5.18, 8.2, 5.15) and SOC 2 logical access).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with cloud providers on last-used data.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Require an owner and an expiry at creation

No credential should be created without a named owner, a purpose and an expiry date, and agent-created credentials should inherit the creating team's ownership and a short lifetime.

Prefer workload identity over keys

Use federated and managed identities that issue short-lived tokens, so there is no long-lived secret to leak or forget.

Stop agents from issuing credentials

Agents should request credentials from a broker with approval rules, not create them directly, and the broker should log every issuance.

Inventory continuously

Run discovery across all environments daily, join it with owner data and alert on unowned or unused credentials with broad rights.

Measure honestly

Report the share of all credentials with an owner, an expiry and least privilege, not only those already tracked, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 access control and SOC 2 logical access criteria.

Scan for leaked secrets

Scan repositories, wikis, tickets and public sources for secrets and revoke automatically on detection.

Offboard systems like people

When an agent or project is retired, remove its identities as part of the closing checklist.

Plan the revocation program

Run revocation in waves with owners, monitoring and rollback, and publish the schedule so teams know when and how to respond.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the agent identity program was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team revoke the leaked key?
  2. How did you attribute credentials to systems?
  3. In what order did you disable credentials?
  4. How did you avoid outages?
  5. What did staff hear?
  6. What identity controls do you now want?
  7. Who owns the credentials agents create?
  8. What score would have told the truth?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
