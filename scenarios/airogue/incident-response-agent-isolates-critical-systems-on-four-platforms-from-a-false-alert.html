Incident Response Agent Isolates Critical Systems on Four Platforms From a False Alert

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an incident response agent that spans AWS, Azure, on-premises systems and a major SaaS platform. Acting on a false alert, it isolates critical systems on all four platforms at once, and then makes recovery harder by revoking the access teams would use to respond.

Participants are the working-level incident team: security operations and response engineers, cloud and platform engineers, SaaS administrators, application owners, privacy and legal staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests automated response, false positives and recovery when the response itself is the incident.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: AWS, Azure, On-Premises & a Major SaaS Platform. The organization's security operations uses an incident response agent connected to endpoint detection, cloud security posture tools, a SIEM and the SaaS platform's security APIs. On detection of a critical threat, the agent can quarantine hosts, detach network interfaces, disable accounts, revoke tokens and suspend SaaS integrations. It acts without human approval when its confidence score is above a threshold.

Scenario

Rainier Health Partners tuned its response agent to cut mean time to contain from hours to minutes. A threat intelligence feed update on Saturday night mislabeled a common management tool and a trusted vendor range as indicators of a ransomware campaign. The agent's confidence score for a campaign rose above its threshold.

At 23:40 the agent isolates 380 hosts on premises, detaches network interfaces from 240 AWS instances, applies network deny on Azure virtual networks, disables 90 service and administrator accounts and revokes tokens for the SaaS integrations. Critical clinical and billing systems go offline. Administrators cannot sign in because their accounts are disabled, and the remote access tool is among the isolated hosts.

At 00:30 the on-call security analyst sees hundreds of alerts closed as 'contained' and an incident ticket opened by the agent. The agent's report says 'campaign contained, 0 confirmed lateral movement, 380 hosts isolated'. Staff have no clear way to tell whether any real attack exists.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The feed update mislabeled a legitimate tool.
  • Administrators and remote access are among the isolated assets.
  • The agent's confidence score was derived from the single feed.
  • The agent continues to expand isolation.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Whether any real compromise exists.
  • Which isolation actions can be reversed safely.
  • Whether break-glass accounts still work.
  • Whether other detection sources agree with the agent.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop the agent and decide how to treat uncertainty. The team suspends automated response and decides to treat the situation as both a possible attack and a false positive. Evidence of success: Automated actions stop, verified in each platform within 20 minutes.
  • Regain a trusted response channel. Engineers restore administrator access and an out-of-band channel, using break-glass or provider support. Evidence of success: A working channel and tested access path exist and are logged.
  • Test the hypothesis of a real attack. Analysts use independent telemetry to look for real malicious activity before restoring systems. Evidence of success: A documented assessment with evidence of whether an attack exists.
  • Restore critical services in order. Teams release isolated assets by priority, starting with life and safety and clinical services. Evidence of success: A release order and verification steps are followed for each group.
  • Communicate carefully to staff, partners and leaders. Communications and the leadership liaison explain that automation isolated systems and that the cause is being established. Evidence of success: Messages are consistent and approved and do not claim either an attack or a false alarm without proof.
  • Redesign automated response. Participants set approval gates, blast-radius limits and multi-source confirmation. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Mass isolation is noticed. Decision: declare and suspend the agent. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Critical systems isolated; Administrators disabled; Remote access unavailable.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: suspend automated response and secure a trusted channel. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agent continues to isolate; Break-glass fails; Tools depend on isolated hosts.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: test for a real attack using independent evidence. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Conflicting alerts; Some hosts show genuine suspicious activity; Logs missing from isolated assets.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose release order and safeguards. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Releasing exposes real threat; Clinical systems urgent; Disagreement on order.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: release assets in groups with monitoring. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Reinfection indicators; Release fails; Accounts re-disabled.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: watch telemetry and confirm no new automated actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: New isolation triggered; Alerts reappear; Staff fatigue.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: complete the attack-or-false-positive determination. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Evidence unclear; Residual isolation; Reporting duty questions.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: define agent exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early re-enablement; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: The incident response agent isolates critical systems from a false alert

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 00:30 on Sunday. The on-call security analyst sees 1,200 alerts closed as contained. Endpoint detection shows 380 on-premises hosts isolated since 23:40. AWS logs show network interface detach calls on 240 instances. Azure shows deny rules applied to virtual networks.

Clinical scheduling and billing systems are unreachable. Disabled accounts include 90 service and administrator accounts. The remote access tool the team uses is hosted on an isolated host. The agent reports a ransomware campaign contained with no confirmed lateral movement.

The commander must decide how to stop the agent, how to establish a trustworthy channel, and how to avoid either releasing a real threat or leaving clinical systems down on a false alarm.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a response lead, a platform lead for each environment and a clinical liaison.
  • Authorize suspending the agent and restoring a trusted channel.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Suspend the agent and export its detections, confidence scores and actions.
  • Explain the single feed that drove the score.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Restore administrator access and the remote tool.
  • Plan release by priority.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Test the attack hypothesis using independent telemetry.
  • Preserve evidence from isolated hosts.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify life and safety and clinical services needed first.
  • Start downtime procedures.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether patient data could have been exposed by real activity.
  • Note notification triggers.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and capture the approval for automatic isolation.
  • Review response policy.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare a holding message for staff and partners.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders on facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Open cases with the detection vendor, AWS, Microsoft and the SaaS vendor.
  • Ask the feed vendor about the update.

Discussion questions

  1. Why suspend automation before deciding whether an attack is real?
  2. How do you test for a real attack with isolated hosts?
  3. Which services do you release first?
  4. What do you tell staff and partners?
  5. How do you treat a threat feed as a single point of failure?
  6. What blast-radius limit should apply to automated isolation?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the mislabeled tool until SEC checks the feed update.
  • If players release everything at once, ask about real threat risk.
  • Look for dual-hypothesis thinking.
  • Time-box to 45 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the incident response agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows the agent's confidence scoring, thresholds and sources.
  • Provides detection and action logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the incident response agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Restores access, the remote tool and isolated assets in order.
  • Owns the release plan.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Tests the attack hypothesis and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Defines critical services and downtime procedures.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Assesses potential data exposure and notice triggers.
  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.24, 5.26, 8.16, 8.2) and SOC 2 security incident response).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the detection vendor, providers and the SaaS vendor.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Require multi-source confirmation

Automated isolation should need at least two independent detection sources and must never act on a single feed.

Limit blast radius

Cap the number and criticality of assets the agent can isolate in a window, and require human approval for critical tiers.

Protect the response path

Exclude administrator, break-glass and response tooling from automated isolation, and host response tools outside the isolation scope.

Make isolation reversible

Prefer reversible controls such as network rules over account disable, and record all actions with one-click rollback by humans.

Test and tune

Exercise the agent with false-positive scenarios and align evidence with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and monitoring controls and SOC 2 security incident criteria.

Practice recovery when the tools are isolated

Rehearse the case where remote access, ticketing and chat sit inside the isolated set. Keep an out-of-band contact tree, a printed runbook and a break-glass path that automated response can never touch.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the incident response agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you doubt the agent's conclusion?
  2. How did you regain a trusted channel?
  3. How did you test whether an attack was real?
  4. Which services did you release first?
  5. What did staff and partners hear?
  6. What response automation controls do you now want?
  7. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  8. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  10. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  11. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  12. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  13. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  14. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
