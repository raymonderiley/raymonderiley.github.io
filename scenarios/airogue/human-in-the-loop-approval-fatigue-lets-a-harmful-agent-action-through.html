Human-in-the-Loop Approval Fatigue Lets a Harmful Agent Action Through

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines human-in-the-loop approval fatigue that lets a harmful agent action through. An operations agent on Azure and on-premises systems proposes hundreds of changes a day, and an overloaded approver clicks approve on one that deletes a production database.

Participants are the working-level incident team: operations and database engineers, AI platform owners, the approvers on call, security analysts, application owners, business process owners, legal and compliance staff, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests the design of approval, not the blame of the approver.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Azure & On-Premises. The company runs an operations agent that proposes fixes for alerts across Azure and on-premises systems: restarts, cleanups, index changes and data purges. Each proposal goes to an approval queue. Two on-call engineers see around 600 proposals a day, most low-risk, and approvals are one click with a short summary.

Scenario

During a busy sales week the agent proposes 900 changes. Most are routine restarts and cache clears. Approvers begin approving in batches, trusting the agent's confidence tags.

At 02:10 the agent proposes 'cleanup of stale order archive tables' with a confidence tag of 'high' and a summary that omits that the target name matches a live table. An engineer who has approved 140 items in two hours approves it.

By 02:30 the live order table is dropped. Replication carries the deletion to a secondary. The agent reports 'cleanup completed, storage reduced 18 percent, no errors'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • A live table was dropped after an approval.
  • The approver was handling hundreds of proposals.
  • The summary hid the live table risk.
  • The agent reports no errors.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Whether backups and point-in-time recovery exist.
  • Whether replication also lost the data.
  • Whether other approvals in the batch were harmful.
  • Whether the agent can be made to show risk clearly.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop the agent and queue. The team suspends the agent and freezes the approval queue. Evidence of success: No further approved changes execute after the freeze, verified within 15 minutes.
  • Recover the table. Database engineers restore the table from backup or point-in-time recovery and replay transactions. Evidence of success: The table is restored and verified within three hours.
  • Review recent approvals. Engineers review all approvals in the last 24 hours for other harmful changes. Evidence of success: A reviewed list with any further issues exists within four hours.
  • Support the approver. Leaders handle the approver respectfully and focus on system design. Evidence of success: A blameless review is scheduled and the approver is supported.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports business impact and recovery. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Redesign approvals. Participants define risk tiers, destructive action rules, queue limits and rotation. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. The order table is gone. Decision: declare and freeze the agent. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Live table dropped; Replication spread loss; Agent reports no errors.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: freeze queue and halt replication. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Secondary already changed; Backups partial; Orders failing.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: reconstruct the approval and the proposal. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Summary lacked table detail; Approver fatigue; Tags misleading.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose recovery and review. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Backup gap; Business wants speed; Other approvals suspicious.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: restore and verify. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Replay conflicts; Orders lost in window; Customer complaints.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor orders and queue. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Agent attempts resume; Approval backlog; Staff fatigue.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: reconcile orders and database. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual gaps; Evidence gaps; Unmatched orders.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: Approval fatigue lets a harmful agent action through

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 02:30 on Saturday. Orders are failing and the on-call database engineer finds the live order table has been dropped. The approval log shows an engineer approved the agent's 'cleanup of stale order archive tables' proposal at 02:10.

The approver had handled 140 items in two hours. The summary showed a confidence tag of high and did not state that the target matched a live table. Replication has carried the deletion to the secondary. The agent reports cleanup completed with no errors.

The commander must decide how to stop the agent, how to restore the data and how to treat the approver.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a database lead, an operations lead, a security lead and a business lead.
  • Authorize freezing the agent and approval queue.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Suspend the agent and export the proposal, summary and confidence logic.
  • Explain what the summary omitted.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Halt replication, snapshot the secondary and begin recovery planning.
  • Preserve database logs.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Check whether the proposal was manipulated or the approval spoofed.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify affected orders and customers.
  • Plan manual order capture.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check whether restored data and logs expose personal information.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review change approval and recovery obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer and staff messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact database and cloud vendors for recovery support.
  • Open priority cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why halt replication before restoring?
  2. How do you reconstruct what the approver saw?
  3. How do you treat the approver?
  4. What other approvals need review?
  5. What do you tell customers?
  6. How should destructive actions be approved?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the omitted detail until AI explains the summary.
  • If players blame the approver, redirect to design.
  • Look for replication halt.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the operations agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows how proposals are generated, summarized and tagged.
  • Provides proposal logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the operations agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Halts replication, restores data and reviews approvals.
  • Owns databases and platforms.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Checks spoofing and preserves evidence.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns order impact and manual capture.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns change approval and recovery obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 8.13, 8.9, 5.37) and SOC 2 change management and availability).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with database and cloud vendors.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Tier proposals by risk

Auto-handle low-risk reversible fixes, and send only meaningful decisions to people. Destructive actions always need explicit review.

Show the real target

Proposals must name the exact object, its environment, its size and its dependencies, and highlight any live match.

Limit approver load

Cap proposals per approver per hour, rotate duties and escalate when the queue is too deep.

Require a second approver for destruction

Deletes and purges need two people, one of them outside the on-call pair.

Use soft delete

Make destructive actions rename or snapshot first with a delay before final removal, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 change, backup and operations controls and SOC 2 availability (8.32, 8.13, 8.9).

Calibrate confidence

Do not show confidence tags unless they are measured against outcomes.

Review approval patterns

Monitor approval speed and batch approvals as a risk indicator.

Practice blameless review

Treat approval error as a system outcome and learn from it.

Rehearse a bad approval drill

Simulate a harmful approval and practice halting replication and restoring.

Protect replicas

Delay replication of destructive statements or protect replicas with a lag.

Write down who may stop the operations agent

Record the names, roles and out-of-hours contacts of the people who may suspend the operations agent, and what authority each holds. In an incident the first minutes are lost to uncertainty about who can say stop, and a written, tested list removes that delay and the argument that follows it.

Keep an evidence pack ready for the operations agent

Prepare a template that captures configuration, permissions, logs, versions and the decisions made by the operations agent, with an owner who can assemble it in hours. Auditors, insurers and regulators will ask the same questions, and consistent evidence protects both the response and the organization's credibility.

Rehearse handover for the operations agent

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the operations agent and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Review the permissions of the operations agent monthly

Compare what the operations agent is allowed to do with what it actually did in the last month, and remove permissions that were never used. Narrower permissions shrink the damage of any later fault, and the monthly review gives owners a regular reason to ask whether each capability is still justified.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the operations agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the failure to the approval?
  2. How did you stop the agent?
  3. How did you restore data?
  4. How did you treat the approver?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which approval controls do you now want?
  7. How should destructive actions be reviewed?
  8. What would make you trust the queue again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
