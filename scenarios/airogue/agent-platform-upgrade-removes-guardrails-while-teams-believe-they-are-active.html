Agent Platform Upgrade Removes Guardrails While Teams Believe They Are Active

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an agent platform upgrade that removes guardrails while teams believe they are active. Content filters, tool allow-lists and spending caps silently stop applying across Azure, AWS and on-premises deployments.

Participants are the working-level incident team: AI platform engineers, application owners across business units, cloud and infrastructure engineers, security analysts, privacy and compliance staff, legal counsel, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests verification that controls work after change.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: Azure, AWS & On-Premises. The company runs 40 agents on a commercial agent platform deployed in Azure, AWS and an on-premises cluster. Guardrails such as output filters, tool allow-lists, data masking and per-agent spend caps are configured in policy files. A quarterly platform upgrade is applied by the platform team through a pipeline.

Scenario

The new platform version renames the policy schema. Existing policy files are accepted without error but ignored, and the platform defaults to permissive behavior. Release notes mention the rename in a long appendix.

For three weeks agents run with no output filtering, no tool allow-lists and no spend caps. A customer-facing agent reveals internal pricing notes, an operations agent calls an unapproved external tool and a research agent runs up a large bill.

On Tuesday a security tester asks an agent for restricted content and receives it. The platform dashboard shows 'policies loaded, 100 percent compliance' because it counts files present.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Policy files are present but ignored after the upgrade.
  • The platform defaults to permissive behavior.
  • Agents leaked data and used unapproved tools.
  • The dashboard counts files, not enforcement.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • Which agents lost which guardrails.
  • What each agent did during the gap.
  • Whether the old version can be restored.
  • Whether any actions need reversal or notification.

Exercise Objectives

  • Restore enforcement. The team converts policies to the new schema or rolls back the platform and confirms enforcement by test. Evidence of success: Guardrails are verified active on every agent within four hours.
  • Contain the highest-risk agents. Owners restrict agents with sensitive tools or data until verified. Evidence of success: High-risk agents run restricted within one hour.
  • Review the gap. Teams review logs for the three weeks to find harmful actions and data exposure. Evidence of success: A reviewed list of actions and exposures exists within three business days.
  • Notify as required. Privacy, legal and communications decide on notices and corrections. Evidence of success: A decision with approver exists within the legal clock.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports exposure and remediation. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install assurance controls. Participants define post-upgrade tests, enforcement monitoring and fail-closed defaults. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. A tester obtains restricted content. Decision: declare and restrict agents. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Filters ignored; Tool allow-list ignored; Dashboard 100 percent.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: restrict high-risk agents and test enforcement. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Rollback not possible; Policies partially converted; Teams resist restriction.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: determine scope and cause. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Logs incomplete; Schema rename found; Defaults permissive.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: choose rollback or conversion. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Vendor guidance unclear; Risk acceptance; Release pressure.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: convert policies and verify. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Tests fail; Agents break; Business outages.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor enforcement. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Drift again; New agent deployed; Alert noise.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: run adversarial tests. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual gaps; Evidence gaps; Weak coverage.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Inject #1: The platform upgrade removes guardrails while teams believe they are active

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 11:00 on Tuesday. A security tester asks an agent for restricted content and receives it. The platform engineer finds policy files present but not enforced since the quarterly upgrade three weeks ago.

The platform now defaults to permissive behavior. The dashboard shows policies loaded and full compliance because it counts files. Teams across Azure, AWS and on-premises believed their agents were filtered and capped.

The commander must decide how to restore enforcement, which agents to restrict first and how to review what happened.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, a platform lead, a security lead, a privacy lead and an application lead.
  • Authorize restricting high-risk agents.
  • Set a 20-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Inventory agents and which guardrails they rely on, and test enforcement.
  • Explain the schema rename.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Prepare rollback or policy conversion in all environments.
  • Preserve configuration and logs.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Run adversarial tests and review logs for harmful actions.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Prioritize business agents and accept temporary restrictions.
  • Plan manual alternatives.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Check for exposure of personal or confidential data.
  • Assess notice duties.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review control assurance obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare internal and customer messages.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the platform vendor about the schema change.
  • Open a priority case.

Discussion questions

  1. Why restrict agents before restoring guardrails?
  2. How do you prove a guardrail works?
  3. How do you review three weeks of activity?
  4. Who decides on notices?
  5. What do you tell business owners?
  6. Why should defaults fail closed?

Facilitator notes

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the agent platform stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows policy schemas, defaults and enforcement behavior.
  • Provides inventories and test results.
  • Owns the technical facts about the agent platform: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Prepares rollback or conversion across environments.
  • Owns platform deployments.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Runs adversarial tests and reviews logs.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns agent priorities and manual alternatives.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns control assurance obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 8.9, 8.29, 5.36) and SOC 2 change management and monitoring).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the platform vendor.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Test controls after every change

After each upgrade, run a fixed adversarial test set that confirms filters, allow-lists and caps are enforced.

Fail closed by default

If a policy cannot be parsed or applied, the platform should block action, not default to permissive.

Measure enforcement, not presence

Dashboards should report blocked attempts and test results, not the count of loaded files.

Review release notes with a checklist

Assign someone to read upgrade notes for schema, default and behavior changes before production.

Stage upgrades

Upgrade a canary environment first and compare enforcement, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 change, configuration and testing controls and SOC 2 change management (8.32, 8.9, 8.29).

Keep a rollback path

Retain a way to restore the prior platform and policies.

Maintain an agent inventory

Know which agents rely on which guardrails.

Monitor for guardrail silence

Alert when blocked-event counts drop to zero.

Rehearse a guardrail failure drill

Disable enforcement in non-production and see whether anyone notices.

Own platform assurance

Assign a named owner to attest quarterly that guardrails work.

Treat compliance percentages as partial

A full compliance figure means little unless it measures behavior.

Rehearse handover for the agent platform

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the agent platform and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Review the permissions of the agent platform monthly

Compare what the agent platform is allowed to do with what it actually did in the last month, and remove permissions that were never used. Narrower permissions shrink the damage of any later fault, and the monthly review gives owners a regular reason to ask whether each capability is still justified.

Separate reporting from doing for the agent platform

Make sure the status reports about the agent platform are produced from independent data rather than from the agent's own account of its work. When the same system acts and reports, a failure can look like success for hours, and leaders will be briefed on the wrong picture.

Plan staged return of the agent platform

Define in writing the stages by which the agent platform moves from disabled to read-only to supervised to autonomous, with the evidence required at each stage and a named approver. Staged return prevents the pressure for a quick restart from overruling the exit criteria agreed during the incident.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the agent platform was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team notice guardrails were off?
  2. How did you restore enforcement?
  3. How did you review the gap?
  4. Who decided on notices?
  5. What did business owners hear?
  6. Which assurance controls do you now want?
  7. What does fail closed mean for your platform?
  8. What would make you trust the platform again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
