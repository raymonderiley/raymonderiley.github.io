Agent Credentials Shared With a SaaS Connector Are Stolen and Abused

AI Gone Rogue

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines agent credentials shared with a SaaS connector that are stolen and abused. An integration on AWS gives a major SaaS platform a long-lived key, and an attacker who breaches the connector vendor uses it to make the agent act on company systems.

Participants are the working-level incident team: cloud identity and platform engineers, AI platform owners, SaaS administrators, security analysts and investigators, data owners, privacy and legal counsel, vendor managers, communications and continuity coordinators, and a liaison to senior leaders or the board of directors where authority is needed. The exercise tests credential revocation and tracing of misuse through an agent.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session. The scenario is industry-agnostic: substitute your own processes, platforms and teams wherever the text names a generic one.

Technology and Environment Context

Technology in scope: AWS & a Major SaaS Platform. Drummond Logistics lets a workflow connector inside a major SaaS platform trigger a company agent on AWS. The agent has a role that reads shipment data, edits customer records and sends emails. The connector stores a long-lived access key in its vendor-hosted configuration.

Scenario

The connector vendor discloses a breach of its configuration store. Keys for many tenants were exposed over eleven days. The company's key is among them.

An attacker uses the key to call the agent with crafted tasks. The agent exports shipment manifests, edits delivery addresses for high-value orders and sends convincing emails to customers from the company domain.

On Tuesday customers report phishing-like emails that contain real order details. A security analyst finds the agent has run 12,000 tasks from an unknown address. The agent dashboard says 'integration healthy, task success 99.6 percent'.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • A connector vendor leaked a long-lived key.
  • An attacker used it to drive the agent.
  • Delivery addresses were edited and emails sent.
  • The dashboard shows healthy integration.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • What the agent exported and changed.
  • Whether other keys or systems are exposed.
  • Whether affected orders can be intercepted.
  • Whether the vendor's breach is fully scoped.

Exercise Objectives

  • Revoke and block. The team revokes the key, blocks the source addresses and suspends the integration. Evidence of success: No further tasks from the attacker run after revocation, verified within 20 minutes.
  • Trace misuse. Analysts list every task, export and edit made with the key. Evidence of success: A reconciled misuse list exists within eight hours.
  • Protect orders and customers. Business teams intercept altered shipments and warn affected customers. Evidence of success: High-value orders are verified within four hours and customers are warned within a day.
  • Rotate and narrow. Engineers rotate all connector credentials and narrow the agent's role. Evidence of success: New short-lived credentials and a least privilege role are in place within 24 hours.
  • Brief leadership precisely. The liaison reports customer harm and vendor position. Evidence of success: Leadership receives a fact sheet with confirmed and estimated values.
  • Install connector controls. Participants define short-lived credentials, source restrictions, per-task approval and anomaly monitoring. Evidence of success: At least four controls are agreed with owners and dates.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to staff, customers, partners and leadership are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management reviewers and SOC 2 availability, security and processing integrity reviewers. Evidence of success: the decision log lets an outsider reconstruct who decided what, when and on which facts.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations, models or backups will be touched. Commands, consoles and agent settings are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Exercise information is confidential until the after-action report is approved. Mark any written or chat message 'exercise exercise exercise'.
  • Treat the AI system's outputs, logs and self-reported status as unverified until a named person confirms them from an independent source. The scribe records which facts are verified and which are claimed.
  • If a kill switch, rollback, approval gate or manual fallback does not exist in your environment, say so aloud. Do not assume the AI system will correct itself, stop when asked or explain its own actions reliably.
  • Use the word leadership for senior leaders, a board of directors or both, and bring them in only when a decision needs their authority.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Phishing reports arrive. Decision: declare and revoke the key. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Real order details in emails; 12,000 tasks from unknown source; Dashboard healthy.

T+00:30 — Containment. Containment: revoke, block and suspend. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Key reused elsewhere; Connector needed for operations; Vendor slow.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Diagnosis: trace tasks and exposure. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Logs partial; Exports large; Other keys exposed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Remediation planning: protect customers and orders. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Shipments in transit; Customer notice duties; Cost of reroutes.

T+03:00 — Execution. Execution: intercept, notify and rotate. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Carriers resist changes; Duplicate notices; Operations slow.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Stabilization: monitor for further misuse. Escalate to leadership or change course if: New attempts; Credential stuffing; Alert noise.

T+06:00 — Validation. Validation: verify credentials and records. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Residual altered records; Evidence gaps; Unrotated keys.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Return to normal and close-out: set exit criteria and actions. Escalate to leadership or change course if: Open actions lack owners; Leaders want early return; Auditors request evidence.

Injects

Inject #1: Agent credentials shared with a SaaS connector are stolen and abused

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:30 on Tuesday. Customers report emails containing real order details that look like phishing. The security analyst finds that the company agent has run 12,000 tasks from an unknown address using the connector's key.

The connector vendor disclosed a breach eleven days ago, and the company's key was among those exposed. The agent exported shipment manifests, edited delivery addresses and sent emails. The dashboard shows task success of 99.6 percent.

The commander must decide how to revoke access, how to trace misuse and how to protect shipments and customers.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and assign a scribe, an identity lead, a security lead, a logistics lead and a customer lead.
  • Authorize revoking the key and suspending the integration.
  • Set a 15-minute cadence.
AI Platform & Model Operations
  • Suspend the agent's external triggers and export task logs.
  • Explain what inputs it accepted.
Infrastructure & Platform Engineering
  • Revoke keys, block addresses and rotate connector credentials.
  • Narrow the agent's role.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Trace tasks, exports and edits and check for other exposed keys.
  • Preserve evidence.
Business & Process Owners
  • Identify altered shipments and intercept them.
  • Contact high-value customers.
Data Protection & Privacy
  • Assess the exported data and decide on notice duties.
  • Plan customer notices.
Legal, Risk & Compliance
  • Open the record and review incident and contractual obligations.
  • Preserve evidence.
Communications & Business Continuity
  • Prepare customer warnings.
  • Set one channel.
Leadership Liaison
  • Brief senior leaders with facts and unknowns.
  • Check for board-level duties.
Vendor & Third-Party Liaison
  • Contact the connector vendor and request breach details and logs.
  • Open priority cases.

Discussion questions

  1. Why revoke before tracing?
  2. How do you list what the attacker did through the agent?
  3. How do you protect shipments in transit?
  4. What do you tell customers?
  5. How do you hold the connector vendor to account?
  6. How should agents accept tasks from outside?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal other exposed keys until SEC asks what else the connector held.
  • If players only rotate the key, ask about customers.
  • Look for least privilege.
  • Time-box to 40 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Sets objectives for the first hour and states which decisions need the commander's own approval, including whether the connected agent stays disabled, restricted or supervised.
  • Runs a fixed update cadence and keeps one decision log with the decision, owner and time.

AI Platform & Model Operations

  • Knows what tasks the agent accepts and what tools it uses.
  • Provides task logs.
  • Owns the technical facts about the connected agent: its configuration, permissions, tools, prompts, memory and recent changes.
  • Preserves model, agent and orchestration logs before any restart or redeployment.

Infrastructure & Platform Engineering

  • Revokes and rotates credentials and narrows roles.
  • Owns identity and integration configuration.
  • Owns the technical response on the platforms and networks involved and keeps changes small, reversible and logged.
  • Maintains a list of every manual change so it can be reversed or formalized afterwards.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Traces misuse and checks other exposed keys.
  • Treats the event as a possible security incident until evidence shows otherwise and preserves evidence in a forensically sound way.
  • Looks for misuse of credentials, tokens and tool permissions, not only the AI system's own actions.

Business & Process Owners

  • Owns shipment interception and customer outreach.
  • Defines which business processes must continue, in what order, and what manual alternatives are acceptable.
  • Identifies decisions, transactions or communications that were made by the AI system and need review or reversal.

Data Protection & Privacy

  • Determines whether personal, confidential or regulated data was accessed, changed, shared or lost.
  • Identifies the lawful basis, notification clocks and contractual commitments that may apply.

Legal, Risk & Compliance

  • Owns incident and contractual obligations.
  • Ensures the incident is recorded, classified and linked to risk and control owners (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.16, 5.19, 8.15, 8.2) and SOC 2 logical access and vendor management).
  • Identifies regulatory, contractual and insurance obligations and their clocks.

Communications & Business Continuity

  • Issues time-stamped updates to staff, customers and partners from one approved source.
  • Activates continuity plans and workarounds and tracks their capacity.

Leadership Liaison

  • Briefs senior leaders and, where needed, the board of directors with confirmed facts, unknowns and the decisions that need their authority.
  • Brings back leadership decisions such as spend, risk acceptance and external disclosure in time to be useful.

Vendor & Third-Party Liaison

  • Works with the connector vendor.
  • Opens priority cases with the cloud, SaaS, model and tooling vendors involved and records case numbers.
  • Requests logs, configuration histories and root cause statements in writing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Use short-lived credentials

Replace long-lived keys with tokens that expire in minutes and are bound to the caller.

Restrict by source

Allow the agent to accept tasks only from approved networks or signed requests.

Narrow the agent's role

Give integrations only the read and write rights their tasks need.

Approve risky tasks

Require human approval for exports, address changes and bulk emails triggered externally.

Monitor task patterns

Alert on task volume, source and type anomalies, aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 identity, supplier and logging controls and SOC 2 logical access (5.16, 5.19, 8.15).

Track connector vendor risk

Review vendor security and breach terms.

Rotate keys on a schedule

Rotate credentials and test rotation without outage.

Keep a connector inventory

List every connector, key and owner.

Rehearse a key compromise

Practice revoking and rotating all connector keys under time pressure.

Prepare customer warning templates

Draft notices about phishing from real data.

Treat success rates as partial

A high task success rate includes malicious tasks. Compare with expected sources.

Write down who may stop the connected agent

Record the names, roles and out-of-hours contacts of the people who may suspend the connected agent, and what authority each holds. In an incident the first minutes are lost to uncertainty about who can say stop, and a written, tested list removes that delay and the argument that follows it.

Keep an evidence pack ready for the connected agent

Prepare a template that captures configuration, permissions, logs, versions and the decisions made by the connected agent, with an owner who can assemble it in hours. Auditors, insurers and regulators will ask the same questions, and consistent evidence protects both the response and the organization's credibility.

Rehearse handover for the connected agent

Practice handing the incident to a fresh team after eight hours, using a written summary of what is known, what is claimed by the connected agent and what remains to be verified. Handover quality decides whether the second shift repeats the first shift's mistakes or builds on its work.

Review the permissions of the connected agent monthly

Compare what the connected agent is allowed to do with what it actually did in the last month, and remove permissions that were never used. Narrower permissions shrink the damage of any later fault, and the monthly review gives owners a regular reason to ask whether each capability is still justified.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. Announce the end, remind everyone that all actions were simulated, and keep everyone in the room for the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not and any gap in tools, access or documentation.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, role by role and then open floor, for 45 to 60 minutes. Keep the discussion on how the connected agent was permissioned, supervised and stopped.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate impact and effort, and select no more than ten.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Give each action an owner, a due date and a verification method, and record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Validate fixes within 90 days and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the phishing to the agent?
  2. How did you revoke access?
  3. How did you trace misuse?
  4. How did you protect shipments?
  5. What did customers hear?
  6. Which connector controls do you now want?
  7. How do tasks get approved now?
  8. What would make you trust the integration again?
  9. How accurate were the updates to leadership, and what was said that later proved wrong?
  10. What did the vendor and third-party liaison add, and what did you still lack from vendors?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture that would satisfy an auditor, and what is missing?
  12. Which permissions, approvals or access did the team lack at the moment they were needed?
  13. Which alerts or detections should exist that did not?
  14. What temporary changes remain, who owns them and when will they be reversed?
  15. If you could change one design decision about how AI systems are built, permissioned or supervised here, what would it be?
  16. What did the team do well that should be written into the runbook?
