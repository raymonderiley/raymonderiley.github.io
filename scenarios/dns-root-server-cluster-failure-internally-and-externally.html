DNS Root Server Cluster Failure Internally and Externally

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines the failure of the organization's authoritative and recursive DNS infrastructure, affecting both internal name resolution (applications, directory, internal services) and external resolution (the public website, mail, partner connections). The organization hosts its own DNS cluster for internal and external zones, so a failure removes names on both sides of the firewall at once.

The exercise is for working-level participants: DNS and network services engineers, network and security operators, server and directory staff, application owners, the service desk, the registrar and external provider liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. It highlights how many systems silently depend on DNS and how hard recovery is when the tools needed to fix DNS themselves use DNS.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Aurora Manufacturing runs its DNS on a four-node cluster of BIND servers with a hidden primary: two nodes in the primary data center serve internal recursion and authoritative internal zones; two nodes in a DMZ serve the public authoritative zones for aurora-mfg.example and several partner-facing subdomains. Internal zones are also integrated with Active Directory DNS for domain controllers. DNS configuration is managed through a version-controlled repository and deployed by an automation tool that itself resolves hostnames.

At 08:55 on Monday, a routine zone update pushes a malformed record set (a CNAME at the zone apex combined with an oversized TXT record). The primary fails to load the zone, logs 'zone not loaded due to errors', and begins to serve SERVFAIL for the zone. Secondary servers keep serving for the SOA refresh interval, but the zone expire timer is short (4 hours) and a faulty serial number rollback forces zone transfer failures. At 09:10, internal recursion resolvers hit a resource exhaustion condition from retry storms and crash. By 09:25, internal resolution is down and by 10:10 external resolution fails as secondaries expire the zone.

What the team does not yet know: TTLs on key records are 300 seconds, so the effect is fast. The automation tool and the version control server rely on DNS names, the out-of-band management network uses names that resolve via the failed cluster, the registrar's lock and the glue records require authorized contacts with MFA tokens held by a person on leave, and the previous version of the zone file was overwritten without a backup copy.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize the failure pattern. Participants distinguish authoritative failure from recursive failure, interpret SERVFAIL, EXPIRE and zone transfer logs, and decide what is the current blast radius.
  • Restore critical name resolution quickly. The team uses temporary measures: hosts file entries for critical systems, alternate resolvers, pre-staged zone copies and forwarders, to bring essential services back while the root cause is repaired.
  • Break the circular dependencies. Participants identify tools that depend on DNS (automation, version control, monitoring, vault, remote access) and use IP-based or out-of-band alternatives.
  • Repair the zone safely. The DNS team restores a known-good zone, validates syntax and records, and avoids reintroducing the malformed data.
  • Manage external visibility. The team engages the registrar or external DNS provider for emergency delegation or a secondary service, deals with TTL and cache issues and assesses mail and partner impact.
  • Prevent recurrence. The team examines pre-deployment validation, change windows, serial number practices and DNS redundancy design.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Use actual hostnames, tools and procedures from your environment where known; if teams do not know how to reach a system without DNS, record it as a gap.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Resolution failures and monitoring alerts lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Scoping and temporary measures. Internal and external impact is scoped; critical names are stabilized.

T+01:15 — Root cause and dependency map. The zone error and circular dependencies are identified.

T+02:00 — Zone repair plan. A known-good zone is prepared and validated.

T+03:00 — External resolution decision. Registrar or provider assistance is decided.

T+04:30 — Execution. Zones are restored, resolvers restarted and caches managed.

T+06:00 — Validation. Internal and external resolution, mail and partner connections are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Services released and incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Internal names stop resolving and external resolution is failing

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:30. The service desk reports 220 tickets in 30 minutes. Users cannot reach internal applications by name, logins to the directory fail intermittently and the ERP system is unreachable. The monitoring system is blind on most checks because its checks use hostnames.

On the DNS cluster, the primary's log shows 'zone aurora-mfg.example: loading from master file failed: CNAME and other data' and 'not loaded due to errors'. The secondaries are serving stale data but their zone refresh failed. The internal recursive resolvers crashed at 09:10 from retry storms.

The automation tool, version control server and the privileged access vault are reached by name. The out-of-band management network also uses names via the same DNS. The registrar's account owner is on leave and the MFA token is with him. The previous zone file was overwritten at 08:52 and the repository history shows the commit with the error.

Facilitator provides: the BIND log extract, the zone diff, the dependency list of tools and the monitoring screenshot.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name leads for internal DNS, external DNS and communications.
  • Decide whether to freeze all DNS changes and automation.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
DNS & Network Services Engineering
  • Interpret the logs and identify the error that stopped the zone.
  • Describe how to restore the previous zone and what is needed.
  • Plan restart of recursive resolvers with rate limits.
Network & Security Operations
  • Confirm the network is healthy and rule out connectivity.
  • Provide IP access to management tools.
  • Apply rate limiting to protect resolvers.
Server & Directory Operations
  • Check directory locator records and domain controller resolution.
  • List critical servers for hosts file entries.
  • Verify authentication behavior.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • List services broken and rank them.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message and IP-based workaround.
  • Owners: identify IP-based access.
Registrar & External Provider Liaison
  • Contact the registrar and verify authorized contacts.
  • Check what can be done without the token holder.
  • Prepare emergency delegation if needed.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Activate the continuity plan.
  • Prepare the first update using channels not dependent on DNS.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask whether the zone change had validation and review.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell an authoritative failure from a recursive failure from what you see?
  2. Which tools do you need to fix DNS that depend on DNS, and what are the alternatives?
  3. What is the quickest path to restoring critical names while the zone is repaired?
  4. How do you restore the zone and prove it is valid before loading it?
  5. What do you do about the registrar token holder on leave?
  6. Why did the zone expire timer matter and how do you handle caches after repair?
  7. How does the team communicate if email depends on DNS?
  8. What do partners and plants need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the registrar MFA problem until the liaison attempts access.
  • If players start by changing the zone live, ask how they validate it and who reviews.
  • Look for use of IP-based out-of-band access.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident and sets priorities for names that matter most: directory, authentication, ERP, mail and partner EDI.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes at the registrar and with external DNS providers.
  • Decides when to restart resolvers and re-enable automation.

DNS & Network Services Engineering

  • Reads BIND logs, serial numbers and zone transfer status and identifies the malformed record set.
  • Restores the last known-good zone from repository history or from any surviving secondary, and validates it with named-checkzone.
  • Plans controlled restart of resolvers and rate limiting to avoid retry storms.
  • Prepares temporary records and forwarder settings.

Network & Security Operations

  • Verifies network health to separate DNS failure from connectivity failure.
  • Applies rate limiting and firewall rules to protect resolvers during recovery.
  • Provides IP-based access to management networks and security tooling.
  • Checks for unusual traffic that could indicate attack.

Server & Directory Operations

  • Verifies domain controller locator records (SRV records) and Active Directory DNS replication.
  • Applies hosts file entries for critical servers where needed.
  • Checks whether authentication is failing and Kerberos clients cache failures.
  • Prepares to flush caches after repair.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners list services broken: ERP, EDI with partners, mail, web store and plant control interfaces.
  • The service desk issues guidance and tracks ticket volume.
  • Owners identify manual fallback or IP-based access.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Registrar & External Provider Liaison

  • Contacts the registrar and any external DNS provider, verifies authorized contacts and MFA tokens and prepares emergency delegation.
  • Checks glue records and registrar locks.
  • Evaluates whether to temporarily delegate to a secondary provider.
  • Keeps a record of all registrar communications.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates the continuity plan for production, shipping and partner communications.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership, plants and partners.
  • Coordinates alternate communication channels as email may be down.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.20 network security; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates contractual impact with partners and customers.
  • Captures evidence of emergency changes.
  • Logs findings on change validation, DNS redundancy and circular dependencies.

Resilience Architect Guidance

DNS is the foundation of nearly everything. Treat internal and external DNS as separate failure domains, with independent providers or secondaries for external zones and tested failover.

Validate before deploying: lint zones, run named-checkzone in the pipeline, stage changes to a canary and require review. Make serial number practices safe and monitor transfer errors.

Break circular dependencies: keep IP-addressed out-of-band access, a documented hosts file for critical systems, offline copies of zone files and credentials for the registrar not tied to one person.

Plan for resolver overload: rate limiting, caching tiers and a rehearsed restart procedure; tune zone expire and TTL values deliberately.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and SOC 2 expect evidence of change control, tested redundancy, supplier arrangements (registrar) and recovery testing.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on circular dependencies and how quickly critical names were stabilized.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did the team confirm which layer of DNS had failed and how long did it take?
  2. Which tools did you need that depended on DNS, and how did you work around them?
  3. How were critical names restored while the zone was being fixed?
  4. Where was the last known-good zone and was it recoverable?
  5. How was the malformed record validated before deployment, and what pipeline checks exist?
  6. How did you handle the registrar account and MFA token problem?
  7. How did the team address retry storms and resolver crashes?
  8. How did you communicate when email was down?
  9. How did partners learn of the outage and workarounds?
  10. What did you learn about TTL and zone expire timers?
  11. How did you prevent automation from re-pushing the error?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. Which decisions were slowed by missing access?
  14. What access or credentials were missing?
  15. What redundancy would change the outcome?
