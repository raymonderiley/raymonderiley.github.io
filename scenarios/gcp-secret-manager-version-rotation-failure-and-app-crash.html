GCP Secret Manager Version Rotation Failure and App Crash

Google Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Secret Manager rotation that creates a new secret version with a bad value and automatically disables the old version, so dozens of applications that read the latest version crash on startup or lose database connections.

Participants are working-level: security engineers, application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Google Cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of secret versions, aliases, rotation schedules, access logging and safe rollback of credentials.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Kingfisher Telecom stores database passwords and API keys in Secret Manager. A rotation function runs on schedule, writes a new version, disables the previous one after 10 minutes and notifies owners. About 70 services read the 'latest' alias at startup or on reconnect.

At 02:30 on Thursday, the rotation function for the billing database credential writes a new version but the database password change step fails midway. The secret now holds a value that does not match the database. The old version is disabled by the cleanup timer. Services restart during a routine scale-down and fail to connect. By 03:00, 35 services are down or degraded and billing runs are failing.

What the team does not yet know: the database still accepts the old password, re-enabling the old version is possible, some services cached the new bad value, rotation also ran for two other secrets with partial failures, and access logs show a separate unusual access from an unknown service account.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a credential rotation failure. Participants correlate secret version changes, application errors and database logs.
  • Restore service safely. The team re-enables the correct version and controls restarts to avoid connection storms.
  • Fix the rotation process. Engineers pause rotation, validate other secrets and repair the workflow.
  • Check for compromise. Participants review access logs for unusual reads.
  • Coordinate across owners. The team tracks which services consume which secrets.
  • Improve secret management. Participants review dual-version rollout, validation before disable and monitoring.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Never print or share secret values in chat or tickets during recovery.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Application crashes and failed billing lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Rotation is paused and the correct version is identified.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Secrets and dependent services are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A restore and restart plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. The correct version is restored and services restart in waves.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Services stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Other secrets and access logs are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Services crash after a failed rotation

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 03:10. Thirty-five services fail to connect to the billing database. Billing runs fail. Secret Manager shows a new version at 02:30 and the previous version disabled at 02:40.

The security engineer finds the rotation function failed after writing the secret but before changing the database password.

The database still accepts the old password. Two other rotations show partial failures. Access logs show an unfamiliar service account reading a secret.

Facilitator provides: version history, rotation logs, error samples and the service list.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize re-enabling the old version.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Security Engineering (Secret Manager)
  • Re-enable the correct version.
  • Pause rotation.
  • Validate other secrets.
Application Engineering
  • Restart in waves.
  • Clear caches.
  • Validate billing.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • List services.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Coordinate restarts.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Review access logs.
  • Monitor database.
  • Preserve logs.
Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about versions.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan customer notice.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about billing obligations.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you confirm the rotation is the cause?
  2. What is the safest fix and who approves?
  3. How do you restart 35 services without a connection storm?
  4. Why did the cleanup disable the old version?
  5. How do you check other secrets?
  6. What does the unusual access mean?
  7. How do you avoid exposing values during recovery?
  8. What do customers hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the unknown account until OPS reviews access logs.
  • If players paste secrets in chat, stop and discuss.
  • Look for controlled restarts.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes restoring services without exposing secrets and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Security Engineering (Secret Manager)

  • Reads secret version history and rotation logs.
  • Re-enables the correct version and pauses rotation.
  • Validates other rotated secrets.
  • Plans a repaired rotation.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Engineering

  • Restarts services in waves and manages connection limits.
  • Clears cached values.
  • Validates billing runs.
  • Tracks affected services.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners list their services and critical paths.
  • The service desk logs reports.
  • Owners coordinate restarts.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Reviews access logs for unknown readers.
  • Monitors database connections.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Adds alerts for rotation anomalies.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on secret version behavior.
  • Asks about version states and access audit.
  • Requests guidance on rotation integration.
  • Escalates through the TAM.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for billing operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 5.17 authentication information, 8.24 cryptography; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses billing continuity and credential protection.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Validate new credentials against the target before disabling old ones and keep two versions live during rollout.

Make clients refresh secrets gracefully and fall back on connection failure.

Alert on rotation failures and on unusual secret reads.

Never place secret values in chat or tickets.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.24, 5.17) and SOC 2 expect key and credential management and change evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on dual-version rotation and secret hygiene.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you link the failure to rotation?
  2. How did you restore the right version?
  3. How did you restart safely?
  4. How did you check other secrets?
  5. How did you assess the unknown reader?
  6. How did you protect secret values?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
