Legacy PBX Telephony Switch Cascade Failure During Crisis

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines the failure of a legacy on-premises PBX telephony switch at the moment the organization depends on voice the most: during another crisis. A cascade of failures (a processor card fault, a corrupted translation database and failed survivable gateways) removes inbound, outbound and internal calling, including emergency-notification and contact center routing.

It is built for working-level participants: telephony engineers, contact center supervisors, network and carrier staff, facilities, the service desk, application owners, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. It highlights how many processes silently rely on voice and how little support remains for aging telephony platforms.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Ridgeway County Utilities runs a ten-year-old PBX serving 2,200 extensions, a 90-seat contact center, a 24-hour outage hotline, and a mass notification dialer that calls on-call staff. The PBX has duplicated common control processors, a configuration database backed up to a local drive, 12 PRI trunks to the carrier, and three branch survivable gateways. The vendor ended full support two years ago and the organization buys time-and-materials service.

During an active storm response, with 6,400 customers without power and the outage hotline at peak volume, the active processor card reports parity errors at 14:20 and the standby fails to take over because of a mismatched software release. By 14:35 the PBX is in a reduced operating mode with no new call setup. Attempts to reload restore only partially because the translation database restored from last night's backup contains a corrupted dial plan segment. The branch gateways cannot register because they depend on the central controller.

What the team does not yet know: the standby card was never upgraded to the same release after a patch two years ago, the last offsite configuration backup is five months old, the carrier can reroute DID numbers but requires a documented request process with a four-hour lead time, and the mass notification system uses the PBX for outbound calling.

Exercise Objectives

  • Diagnose telephony platform failure. Participants separate switch, trunk, carrier and network issues using alarm logs, trunk status and test calls, and identify the failed processor and database issue.
  • Maintain essential communications during the outage. The team identifies alternative channels for the outage hotline and on-call notification, including carrier rerouting, mobile phones, softphones and radios.
  • Recover the platform safely. Telephony engineers decide between card replacement, release alignment, database restore and rebuild, and plan the testing before cutover.
  • Use the carrier effectively. The team executes carrier call forwarding and DID rerouting, understands the lead times and approvals, and manages trunk capacity.
  • Protect contact center and customer communications. Contact center managers use alternate IVR, web messaging and recorded messages to deal with high call volumes.
  • Expose hidden dependencies on telephony. Participants list systems that rely on voice: alarm dialers, elevator phones, fire panel dialers, mass notification, and two-factor voice calls.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Name the specific processes and phone numbers that matter to your operation. Where nobody knows the number or owner, record it.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Alarms, call failures and contact center escalations prompt declaration.

T+00:30 — Triage and essential communications. The scope of the outage is established and alternate channels are activated.

T+01:15 — Root cause and carrier engagement. Processor and database faults are identified; carrier reroute begins.

T+02:00 — Dependency discovery. Hidden telephony dependencies are listed and mitigated.

T+03:00 — Recovery path decision. Repair, rebuild or interim cloud/soft-switch fallback is decided.

T+04:30 — Execution. Recovery steps, testing and phased cutover.

T+06:00 — Validation. Call flow, contact center, emergency lines and notifications are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Services are released and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: PBX stops completing calls during storm response

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 14:38. The PBX alarm panel shows a major alarm on the active common control processor and a minor alarm on the standby that failed to take over. Callers get silence or a fast busy. The contact center supervisor reports that all 90 seats show 'logged out' and the outage hotline queue has 140 waiting callers. The mass notification dialer fails to call on-call crews.

The telephony engineer reloaded the active processor at 14:30, and the system came back for eight minutes before halting again with a translation database error. The restored database from last night's backup has a corrupted dial plan segment for outbound routing. Branch gateways in two locations show 'unregistered'.

The carrier's portal shows all twelve PRI trunks are up and idle, which indicates the fault is inside the switch. Six thousand four hundred customers are without power due to the storm and the volume of calls is five times normal.

Facilitator provides: the PBX alarm log extract, the trunk status screen, the contact center queue statistics and the phone number list for the outage hotline.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and set severity. Name the telephony lead, communications lead and scribe.
  • Decide how crews will be dispatched without the PBX dialer.
  • Set the next update at T+00:30.
Telephony & Voice Engineering
  • Interpret the alarms and say whether the processor, database or both are at fault.
  • List repair options: replace the card, align releases, restore a different database, load a minimal dial plan.
  • Tell the team how long each option takes.
Contact Center Operations
  • Activate the recorded message and web or SMS intake for the outage hotline.
  • Move supervisors to mobile phones for critical callers.
  • Estimate abandoned call volumes.
Network & SIP/PSTN Operations
  • Confirm carrier trunk health and make test calls.
  • Request emergency call forwarding of the hotline number to mobile phones or an alternate site and ask for the lead time.
  • Check the network capacity for softphones.
Facilities & Power
  • Rule out power and environmental causes in the PBX room.
  • Arrange access for vendor and spares.
  • Distribute radios and mobile phones to crews.
Service Desk & Application Owners
  • The service desk: issue the notice to staff on alternate channels.
  • Application owners: list systems depending on voice, including MFA calls and alarm dialers.
  • Mass notification owner: arrange SMS fallback.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Contact the emergency operations center and confirm the contingency plan.
  • Prepare the first public and internal message.
  • Ask for the documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record and capture the detection time.
  • Ask why the standby was on a different release and whether there was a risk acceptance.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. Which communications are essential right now, and what alternate channel exists for each?
  2. The carrier shows trunks idle. What does that tell you and what test would confirm it?
  3. How long does it take to reroute the outage hotline at the carrier, and who is authorized to request it?
  4. What are the risks of restoring a database with a known corrupted segment compared with loading a minimal dial plan?
  5. Which processes use voice that nobody listed in the BIA?
  6. How do crews learn of dispatch orders without the dialer?
  7. What would you tell customers about how to report an outage right now?
  8. What is the plan if the vendor cannot attend for six hours?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the release mismatch until the team asks why the standby did not take over.
  • If players wait for the vendor before activating alternatives, ask what essential communications they have lost in the meantime.
  • Check whether the carrier reroute process is documented, including the authorized contacts and codes.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident and prioritizes essential communications: outage hotline, on-call notification, emergency and safety lines.
  • Decides between repairing the legacy PBX and moving to an interim fallback.
  • Runs a 30-minute cadence, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Approves vendor and carrier emergency charges.

Telephony & Voice Engineering

  • Reads the PBX alarm log and diagnoses the processor parity error and failed standby handover, including release mismatch.
  • Evaluates the database restore options and the age of each backup, including the five-month-old offsite copy.
  • Engages the vendor under time-and-materials terms and gets the spare card or loaner.
  • Prepares a minimal dial plan for essential extensions and the order for restoring services.

Contact Center Operations

  • Activates contact center contingency: recorded messages, web and SMS intake, and use of mobile phones for supervisors.
  • Prioritizes outage hotline callers by type: safety hazards, life support customers, general outages.
  • Tracks abandoned calls and queue volumes using carrier data.
  • Plans agent redeployment and extended hours after recovery.

Network & SIP/PSTN Operations

  • Verifies carrier circuits and PRIs are up and sends test calls to isolate the problem to the switch.
  • Opens an emergency request with the carrier for call forwarding and DID reroute, noting the lead time.
  • Checks SIP and data network capacity if softphones or cloud fallback are used.
  • Prepares trunk capacity changes.

Facilities & Power

  • Confirms power to the PBX room, UPS state and cooling, to rule out environmental causes.
  • Prepares for vendor and spare equipment access.
  • Supports distribution of radios and mobile phones.
  • Verifies fire panel and elevator emergency phone lines.

Service Desk & Application Owners

  • The service desk issues guidance for staff to use mobile phones and chat.
  • Application owners list systems using voice: mass notification, alarm dialers, MFA phone calls, security dispatch.
  • Owners identify fallback for each, such as SMS or email.
  • Prepare validation tests for post-recovery.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates the communications continuity plan and coordinates with the emergency operations center.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership, regulators and customers with approved wording.
  • Coordinates public notices with media relations about how to report outages.
  • Tracks outage against RTO for the outage hotline.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is classified and recorded (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 controls 5.24-5.28; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates regulatory notification obligations related to emergency communications availability.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and emergency vendor approvals.
  • Logs findings: end-of-support platform, standby mismatch, backup age and hidden dependencies.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Voice is a shared dependency that is rarely listed in dependency maps. Build an inventory of everything that uses the PBX or the trunks: alarm dialers, elevator lines, fire panels, MFA call-backs and mass notification.

Never run duplicated processors on different software releases. A standby that cannot take over is not a standby. Test failover at least annually and verify release alignment after every patch.

Keep offsite, current and tested configuration backups, with a minimal dial plan that restores essential extensions first. A five-month-old offsite copy is a gap.

Pre-arrange carrier rerouting: authorized contacts, PINs, destination numbers and lead times, and test them. Maintain alternate channels for the outage hotline, including SMS and web intake.

Plan for platform end of support: spares, loaner agreements and a funded migration to a resilient modern service. Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and SOC 2 look for asset lifecycle management and tested continuity for critical communication.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on how quickly essential communications were restored by alternate means rather than waiting for the PBX.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. Which essential communications did you lose first, and how long until an alternate was in place?
  2. What did the trunk status tell you and how quickly did the team conclude the fault was inside the switch?
  3. Why did the standby not take over, and how would you have found out earlier?
  4. How current are your offsite configuration backups and have you ever restored one?
  5. Who can authorize carrier call forwarding, how long does it take and when was the contact list last verified?
  6. Which hidden systems depended on voice that nobody listed?
  7. How did crews receive dispatch orders without the dialer?
  8. What did the contact center tell callers and was it accurate?
  9. What life-safety lines depend on the PBX and who owns them?
  10. How would you respond if the vendor could not provide a spare for 36 hours?
  11. What controls apply when adopting an interim hosted service during an incident?
  12. How did MFA dependency on voice affect staff, and what is the alternative?
  13. What evidence did the scribe capture that an auditor needs, and what is missing?
  14. What access or credentials were missing during the exercise?
  15. What would you change to ensure the next crisis does not coincide with a communications outage?
