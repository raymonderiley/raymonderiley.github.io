AWS CloudFront Origin Shield DDoS Flood and Cache Poisoning

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a layer-7 flood against a CloudFront distribution that bypasses cache with randomized query strings and, in parallel, a cache poisoning attempt that causes the edge to serve a malicious redirect to legitimate users. The origin load rises even with Origin Shield, and customers see errors and wrong content.

Participants are working-level: edge and CDN engineers, cloud security engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of cache keys, Origin Shield, WAF rate rules, Shield Advanced and purge behavior.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Tidewell Media streams news and runs a subscription site behind CloudFront with Origin Shield enabled in us-east-1. The cache policy forwards all query strings and the 'Host' and 'X-Forwarded-Host' headers to the origin. AWS WAF is attached with the AWS managed rules but no custom rate rule. The company has AWS Shield Standard only.

At 19:30 on a Sunday during breaking news, request volume rises from 40,000 to 900,000 requests per second. Most requests use random query strings that miss the cache. Origin request rate through Origin Shield triples and the application servers saturate. In parallel, an attacker sends requests with a crafted 'X-Forwarded-Host' header that the origin reflects into a redirect. CloudFront caches the poisoned response for the home page for the default TTL, so legitimate visitors are redirected to a malicious site.

What the team does not yet know: the cache key includes unnecessary headers and all query strings, the origin reflects unvalidated headers, a purge (invalidation) of the home page is possible but the attacker can immediately re-poison, and the attack comes from 30,000 distinct IPs rotating user agents.

Exercise Objectives

  • Separate flood from poisoning. Participants recognize two simultaneous issues: volumetric or request flood and integrity compromise of cached content.
  • Stop serving poisoned content. The team invalidates affected objects, fixes the cache key and origin behavior, and prevents re-poisoning.
  • Absorb and filter the flood. Engineers add WAF rate-based rules, geo or header rules, adjust cache keys and consider Shield Advanced engagement.
  • Protect the origin. Participants scale or shield the origin, use Origin Shield and caching to reduce load and keep paid subscriber paths alive.
  • Protect users and brand. The team handles customers redirected to a malicious site and communicates clearly.
  • Improve edge resilience. Participants review cache policy design, WAF baselines, Shield subscription and incident contacts.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Specify the CloudFront behavior, cache policy or WAF rule you would change; avoid generic 'block the attack' statements.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Traffic surge and redirect reports lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Poisoned objects are invalidated and a first rate rule is applied.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. The cache key and header reflection are identified.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A WAF and cache policy plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Rules, cache policy and origin fixes are deployed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Traffic normalizes and cache hit ratio recovers.

T+06:00 — Validation. Content integrity is verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Traffic surge and customers redirected to a malicious site

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 19:55. The newsroom reports the site is slow and readers say the home page redirects to an unfamiliar domain asking for a 'subscription renewal'. CloudFront shows requests at 900,000 per second and cache hit ratio dropped from 94 to 18 percent. Origin CPU is at 100 percent.

The edge engineer sees logs with random query strings like '?x=8h3k2' on most requests. A response for the home page with a 302 redirect has been cached. The security engineer finds that the origin reflects 'X-Forwarded-Host' into the Location header.

The distribution forwards all query strings and headers to the origin. WAF has managed rules but no rate rule. The company has no Shield Advanced subscription. The AWS Health Dashboard shows no events.

Facilitator provides: the CloudFront metrics, log samples, the poisoned response, the cache policy and WAF configuration.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize invalidation and a temporary static home page.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Edge & CDN Engineering
  • Invalidate the poisoned objects and explain re-poisoning risk.
  • Plan a cache policy that ignores random query strings and headers.
  • Explain propagation time.
Cloud Security Engineering
  • Design WAF rate and header rules.
  • Explain the origin reflection fix.
  • Consider engaging AWS.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Fix header reflection or block the header.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Prepare static fallback.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Analyze logs for attack signatures.
  • Preserve evidence.
  • Set alerts.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about Shield Response Team engagement.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform the newsroom.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan a public warning.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about readers harmed by the redirect.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell a flood from a cache poisoning incident, and can both be true?
  2. How do you stop serving the poisoned response and prevent re-poisoning?
  3. Why is the cache hit ratio so low and how do you fix it?
  4. Which WAF rules help and what is the false positive risk?
  5. What does Origin Shield do and not do here?
  6. Should you engage AWS for Shield Advanced now?
  7. How do you warn readers who were redirected?
  8. What should leadership and the public be told?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the header reflection until the security engineer inspects the origin response.
  • If players only invalidate, ask what stops the attacker repeating.
  • Look for cache key hygiene as a core fix.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes stopping malicious redirects and keeping subscriber access.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes aggressive WAF rules and any temporary blocking of regions.
  • Decides on public statements.

Edge & CDN Engineering

  • Reads CloudFront metrics, cache hit ratio, origin latency and Origin Shield statistics.
  • Plans invalidations, a tightened cache policy and removal of query string and header forwarding.
  • Enables standard logging and real-time logs for analysis.
  • Plans a safe rollout of distribution changes given propagation time.

Cloud Security Engineering

  • Designs WAF rate-based rules, header validation and bot control.
  • Investigates the header reflection and recommends origin fixes.
  • Engages AWS Shield Response Team if subscribed.
  • Reviews whether the attacker accessed anything else.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners confirm origin capacity and fix header reflection.
  • The service desk handles customer reports of redirects.
  • Owners prepare a static fallback for the home page.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Analyzes CloudFront logs for patterns, IPs, user agents and headers.
  • Monitors for further poisoning attempts.
  • Preserves evidence of the attack.
  • Creates alerts for abnormal query string diversity.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a case and engages the Shield Response Team if available.
  • Asks about invalidation propagation and attack mitigation.
  • Requests review of the distribution configuration.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for the news and subscription services and informs the newsroom.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates public statements and social channels.
  • Tracks the outage.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.20 network security, 8.23 web filtering; SOC 2 availability and security).
  • Assesses customer harm from redirects.
  • Captures evidence of decisions.
  • Logs findings on cache policy and WAF gaps.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Design cache keys deliberately. Forward only the headers and query strings the origin needs, normalize or ignore random parameters and validate headers at the origin so unkeyed input can never shape a cached response.

Layer defenses: WAF managed rules plus rate-based and bot rules, Shield Advanced for critical properties where justified and Origin Shield and caching to protect the origin.

Plan for integrity incidents, not just availability: know how to invalidate, how to serve a static fallback and how to warn users quickly.

Prepare contacts and playbooks with AWS, including the Shield Response Team, before an event.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.20, 8.23) and SOC 2 expect documented edge configuration, tested rules and incident response evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on separating flood from poisoning and on cache key design.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you recognize two issues at once?
  2. How did you stop serving poisoned content?
  3. What stopped re-poisoning?
  4. How did cache policy contribute?
  5. How effective and risky were the WAF rules?
  6. How did you protect the origin?
  7. What was the Shield Advanced decision process?
  8. How did you handle the fallback and paywall?
  9. How were readers warned and helped?
  10. How accurate were statements?
  11. What did AWS support add?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. How is origin header handling tested?
  15. What would you change in edge security?
