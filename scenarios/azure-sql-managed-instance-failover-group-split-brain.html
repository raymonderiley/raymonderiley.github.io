Azure SQL Managed Instance Failover Group Split-Brain

Azure Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an Azure SQL Managed Instance failover group in which a forced failover to the secondary region leaves applications writing to both instances. The old primary comes back online and keeps accepting writes from clients that still resolve the old listener, so the data diverges even though the failover group defines only one primary.

Participants are working-level: database reliability engineers, cloud platform and network engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of failover group modes, read-write listener behavior, data loss under forced failover and reconciliation.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Northgate Insurance runs its claims platform on a Business Critical Azure SQL Managed Instance in East US 2 with a geo-secondary in Central US in a failover group. Applications use the failover group read-write listener endpoint. Failover policy is manual, with a documented RPO of about 5 seconds and RTO of 30 minutes. A batch interface and a legacy reporting tool use the instance's own host name rather than the listener.

At 10:05 on Tuesday, Azure reports a regional networking degradation in East US 2. Replication lag to the secondary rises to 4 minutes. At 10:25 the on-call engineer runs a forced failover (with potential data loss) to Central US without confirming lag or isolating the old primary. At 10:50, the East US 2 network recovers and the old primary becomes reachable. The batch interface and several app servers with cached connections to the old instance keep writing to it.

What the team does not yet know: the secondary was about 3 minutes behind at failover, so those transactions exist only on the old primary, the old primary has accepted new claims since recovery, identity columns on both instances are issuing overlapping values, and the old primary must be re-seeded as a secondary, which will discard its unique data unless it is exported first.

Exercise Objectives

  • Interpret replication lag and forced failover consequences. Participants read replication metrics and explain what a forced failover can lose.
  • Detect dual-writer conditions. The team identifies which clients still write to the old primary and confirms with connection and write metrics.
  • Fence and stabilize. Engineers block the old primary at network or firewall level and refresh client connections to the listener.
  • Reconcile divergent data. Participants decide how to compare, merge and resolve conflicts, including identity collisions.
  • Decide who accepts data loss. The team records approvals and defines the authoritative source.
  • Improve failover governance. Participants review approval gates, listener-only connectivity, monitoring and tested runbooks.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Use real Azure terms and the actions available to you; when the runbook is silent, record the gap.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Dual-write symptoms and missing claims lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Writes to the old primary are stopped.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. The divergence window and affected tables are scoped.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Reconciliation rules and the authoritative source are agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Reconciliation is executed and the old instance is re-seeded.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Data checks and business reconciliations are verified.

T+06:00 — Validation. Services are validated and regulators assessed.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Claims written to both instances after a forced failover

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 11:05. Adjusters report missing claims entered in the last 40 minutes, and others see duplicate claim numbers. The failover group shows Central US as primary since 10:25. The old East US 2 instance is online again.

The database engineer finds active write connections on the old primary from the batch interface and eleven application servers. Replication lag at failover time was about 3 minutes. Identity values on a claims table overlap between instances.

The failover was run by one engineer without a second approver. Azure Service Health shows the networking event as mitigated. Claims intake is at peak because of a storm event.

Facilitator provides: failover group status, connection counts per instance, the lag graph and the identity range report.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and set data integrity as priority.
  • Decide to stop writes to the old primary.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Database Reliability Engineering (Azure SQL MI)
  • Confirm which instance receives writes and quantify the window.
  • Warn about identity collisions.
  • Plan export of old-primary data.
Cloud Platform & Network Engineering
  • Fence the old primary.
  • Check listener resolution.
  • Verify network recovery.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Stop the batch interface and legacy reporting.
  • Refresh connections to the listener.
  • Identify high-risk transactions.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Confirm alerts.
  • List missing alarms.
Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about re-seeding.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Hold claims intake or log manually.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for RTO and RPO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask who approved the forced failover.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you know both instances are accepting writes and how fast can you stop one?
  2. What did forced failover mean for data loss?
  3. Why were clients still writing to the old primary?
  4. Which instance is authoritative and why?
  5. How do you merge claims and resolve identity collisions?
  6. What is lost if you re-seed the old primary immediately?
  7. What should adjusters and policyholders hear?
  8. What approval should a forced failover need?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal identity collisions until T1 checks identity ranges.
  • If players re-seed first, ask what happens to unique data.
  • Look for integrity over availability.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes data integrity over availability and sets freeze rules.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes the authoritative source and accepted data loss.
  • Approves a claims intake hold.

Database Reliability Engineering (Azure SQL MI)

  • Confirms which instance is primary and measures the divergence window.
  • Compares claims, payments and audit tables across instances.
  • Plans export of unique data from the old primary before re-seeding.
  • Handles identity collisions and reseed.

Cloud Platform & Network Engineering

  • Fences the old primary with network security groups, firewall rules or private endpoint changes.
  • Checks DNS and listener resolution for clients.
  • Verifies regional network recovery.
  • Plans staged traffic restoration.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners stop the batch interface and legacy reporting and refresh connections.
  • Identify claims, payments and notices at risk.
  • The service desk logs adjuster reports.
  • Prepare reconciliation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of connections and writes per instance and replication lag.
  • Confirms alerts and audit logs.
  • Checks Azure Service Health.
  • Adds dual-writer detection.

Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case with failover group and instance details.
  • Asks about the old primary's state and safe re-seeding.
  • Requests root cause for regional degradation.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for claims intake and informs operations.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual claims logging.
  • Tracks the outage.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.14 redundancy; SOC 2 availability and processing integrity).
  • Assesses regulatory implications for claims records.
  • Captures evidence of failover approvals.
  • Logs findings on failover governance.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Separate lag from failure. Alarm on replication lag against the stated RPO and require evidence and a second approver before forced failover; prefer planned failover when both regions are healthy.

Fence before you promote. Block the old primary at the network layer before any forced failover, because cached connections and batch jobs will not follow DNS promptly.

Enforce listener-only connectivity. No application, batch job or reporting tool should connect by instance host name, and connection pools should refresh on failover events.

Plan reconciliation: globally unique keys, change tracking for comparison and business-owned conflict rules.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.14) and SOC 2 expect tested failover with integrity checks and approval records.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on failover approval and fencing.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

  2. What approvals does forced failover need?
  3. How did you find the dual writers?
  4. How fast was the old primary fenced?
  5. How was the authoritative source chosen?
  6. How did you handle identity collisions?
  7. Why were clients on the old host name?
  8. What happened to payments?
  9. How accurate were messages to adjusters?
  10. What did Microsoft support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. What alarms for dual writers should exist?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What will change in the runbook?
  15. How will failover be tested?
