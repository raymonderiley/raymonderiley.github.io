Active Directory Is Compromised and We Do Not Know How: Full Forest Recovery in a Large Corporate Environment

Active Directory Compromise & Recovery (Flagship)

Exercise Overview

Active Directory is compromised and the company does not know how. Domain controllers are encrypted or untrustworthy, privileged accounts are being used from places no administrator can explain, and the first question from every part of the business is the same: when can people sign in again? This tabletop exercise takes a large corporate environment through the recovery of Active Directory when the cause is unknown, the attacker may still be present and the tools used to recover are themselves part of the problem.

The exercise is the flagship of the Active Directory compromise and recovery series. It is designed for the working-level incident team: directory and identity engineers, security investigators, backup and infrastructure engineers, network and DNS engineers, PKI and hybrid identity engineers, application and service owners, the service desk, counsel, communications, a business continuity coordinator and a liaison to Sr. Leadership or the board of directors where decisions require them. It tests the plan, the authority to act, the order of the work and the evidence behind each decision.

Because the cause is unknown, the exercise does not assume a single attack. It is built to be paired with the twenty ransomware attack-path scenarios in this series, each of which describes a different way an attacker could take control of the directory: credential theft, excessive privileges, unpatched systems, insecure service accounts and weaknesses in connected infrastructure such as domain controllers and management servers. The facilitator reveals one of those paths during the exercise, or lets the group discover it through the injects.

A full run takes six hours with a break. A half-day version uses Inject 1 and four later injects. A decision-focused version for Sr. Leadership takes 60 to 90 minutes and uses injects 3, 4, 6 and 9 only. The scenario is industry-agnostic: replace the technology details with your own, and use the companion exercise template and playbook template to capture results.

Technology and Environment Context

The company is a large corporate environment: about 45,000 staff, one forest with a root domain and six regional child domains, 120 domain controllers across 40 sites, Active Directory-integrated DNS, a two-tier certificate authority hierarchy, federation services and a synchronization server connected to cloud identity, two virtualization platforms, an enterprise backup platform with cloud and tape copies, and several thousand service accounts. The directory authenticates staff, servers, applications and network access. About 1,800 applications bind to it directly or through single sign-on.

How This Exercise Fits the Series

  • This flagship exercise: the in-depth, unknown-cause recovery of a large corporate directory.
  • Twenty ransomware attack-path scenarios: each is a complete tabletop exercise that can be run alone or used as the reveal inside this one.
  • Exercise template: a fill-in worksheet specific to Active Directory recovery.
  • Playbook template: a fill-in recovery playbook to be completed from the results of the exercise.
  • Blog: a plain-language explanation of Active Directory recovery and of the dependencies between applications and the directory.

Themes Carried Forward From Earlier Exercises

This exercise deliberately reuses the lessons of the exercises that came before it. Facilitators should name these themes during the session so that participants connect what they decide today with what they have already practiced.

  • Forest root and replication failure. The Active Directory Forest Root Collapse via Replication Lock exercise showed how a failed root domain and a stalled replication state stop everything beneath them. Carry forward: identify the root domain first, know the FSMO holders and know what replication state you are restoring into.
  • Clean restore points and proof of integrity. The ransomware and cyber-recovery forensics exercises asked how a group proves a backup predates an intrusion. Carry forward: a restore point is a hypothesis until it has been scanned and tested in isolation.
  • Automated tools acting without limits. The AI Gone Rogue series included agents that disabled accounts across directories, revoked access in bulk, expanded their own permissions and defeated separation of duties. Carry forward: during recovery, automation that touches identity must be paused, and nothing should be re-enabled until its permissions are reviewed.
  • Hybrid and multi-cloud identity as one failure domain. The hybrid Azure, AWS and multi-cloud exercises treated cloud identity, federation and on-premises directories as a single dependency chain. Carry forward: a recovered directory is not safe if the synchronization or federation path is still open.
  • Decisions, not details, for Sr. Leadership. The risk-during-recovery tabletops for Sr. Leadership asked each leader for an individual answer, kept the exercise no-fault and fed a crisis decision playbook. Carry forward: ask leaders to decide among options with time, cost and risk, and capture the decision in the playbook template.
  • Ransomware and cloud-native compromise. Operation Silent Ledger, Operation Cloudbreak and Data Function Crunch covered ransomware in on-premises, cloud and serverless estates. Carry forward: the attacker's route often goes through the same identity layer, so the identity recovery plan underpins every other recovery plan.
  • Business continuity without core platforms. Operation Empty Vault tested whether the business can continue when core platforms are unavailable. Carry forward: map which business processes can operate manually during the hours or days without sign-in, and who accepts that cost.

Scenario

On a Tuesday at 05:40 the security operations center receives alerts from endpoint tools on six servers: a script has added an account to a privileged group, then removed it. By 06:15 four domain controllers in two regions report disk errors and stop answering. At 06:30 users begin to report that they cannot sign in. By 07:00 file servers, the backup console and the virtualization management console are unreachable. The directory is partly down and no one can say whether the failures are an outage, an attack or both.

At 07:45 a note appears on a shared file server naming a payment deadline. The investigations lead finds that several privileged accounts have been used from unfamiliar hosts in the last three weeks. There is no evidence yet of how the attacker obtained those credentials. The company's logs are incomplete, and the log collection servers authenticate against the directory.

The incident commander declares a major incident. The group must decide, in order, what to isolate, what to assume, how to recover the directory and how to bring the business back, all while the cause remains unknown.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • Four domain controllers are down and the rest are slow or unreachable from some regions.
  • Staff cannot sign in to many systems, and some cached sessions still work.
  • Several privileged accounts were used from unfamiliar hosts in the last three weeks.
  • A ransom note is present and a payment deadline has been set.
  • The backup console and the virtualization management console are unreachable.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • How the attacker obtained privileged access and whether that path is still open.
  • Whether the directory database or the krbtgt secret was copied.
  • Whether backups, the certificate authority, federation or the hypervisor management plane were touched.
  • When the intrusion began and which recovery points predate it.
  • Whether the attacker is still active in the environment.

Exercise Objectives

  • Declare and communicate. Participants declare an Active Directory compromise on partial evidence, name who has authority to declare it and state in plain language what is known, assumed and unknown.
  • Establish an out-of-band operating mode. The group names the channels, accounts and tools it will use when the directory is untrusted, including break-glass access to hypervisors, backup and network devices.
  • Scope under uncertainty. The group states what it will assume about credentials, backups, PKI, federation and the hypervisor, and what evidence would change those assumptions.
  • Choose a recovery path. The group compares in-place eviction, forest recovery from a clean backup and rebuild into a new forest, and records the criteria for each, including the point at which it would escalate to Sr. Leadership.
  • Prove a clean restore point. The group describes how it would find, verify and scan candidate backups in isolation, and what it would do if the oldest available backup is still compromised.
  • Sequence the technical recovery. The group orders the restoration of the first domain controller, FSMO role seizure, metadata cleanup, DNS, time, credential resets, additional domain controllers and global catalogs, with the reason for each step.
  • Reset credentials in the right order. The group sequences the reset of krbtgt, trust, privileged, service, domain controller computer account and DSRM passwords, and names what will break at each step.
  • Protect the recovery from reinfection. The group names the controls it will apply before reconnecting: isolation, tiered administration, clean administrative hosts, identity threat detection and patching of domain controllers and management servers.
  • Return applications and users in tiers. The group agrees an order for identity-dependent platforms and applications, who accepts each tier as ready and how users will be reset at scale without handing the attacker a social engineering path.
  • Support decisions by Sr. Leadership. The group prepares options, costs, times and risks that a Sr. Leader or board of directors can decide on, and records each decision in the playbook.

Ground Rules

  • This is a no-fault exercise. The aim is to find gaps in plans, authority and tooling, not to assign blame to people or teams.
  • Treat all information in the scenario as true unless a facilitator corrects it. Do not fight the scenario by assuming a control worked unless it is documented.
  • Assume the domain directory cannot be trusted. Do not assume you can use domain accounts, domain-joined tools or domain-based messaging unless the group has agreed how.
  • Ask each role for an individual answer first, then open the discussion. Where the room is quiet, ask the next person whether they agree and why.
  • Use real names for roles and real systems from your own environment where you can, but do not use real credentials, real hostnames of sensitive systems or live data.
  • Do not run tools or commands on live systems during the exercise. Describe what you would do and what you would expect to see.
  • Record decisions, assumptions and open questions in the decision log as the exercise runs.
  • Facilitators may pause to correct a technical error but should prefer asking a question over giving the answer.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+0 to T+2 hours: Detect and declare. Privileged changes, encrypted domain controllers, failed logons at scale or an attacker message arrive without a known cause. Escalation trigger: any unexplained change to Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins or the domain controller OU, or any encrypted domain controller, means declare an Active Directory compromise and treat every domain credential as exposed until proven otherwise.

T+2 to T+6 hours: Contain and scope. Isolate domain controllers and management tiers, disable suspect accounts and preserve volatile evidence. Escalation trigger: evidence of replication of directory secrets (DCSync), theft of the ntds.dit database or a krbtgt hash means the whole forest is compromised and in-place cleanup alone is not defensible.

T+6 to T+12 hours: Decide the recovery path. Choose between in-place eviction, forest recovery from a known-clean backup, or rebuild into a new forest. Escalation trigger: no backup can be shown to predate the intrusion, or the attacker has persistence in the configuration partition, the certificate services or the hypervisor management plane. In those cases escalate to Sr. Leadership for a rebuild or a controlled restore with extended monitoring.

T+12 to T+24 hours: Build the clean room and restore the first domain controller. Stand up the isolated network, restore one writable domain controller per domain from clean backup (root domain first), seize FSMO roles, clean metadata and reset the credentials that must change first. Escalation trigger: the first restore fails integrity or replication checks, or the backup catalog or backup credentials are themselves compromised.

T+24 to T+48 hours: Restore directory services at scale. Reset the krbtgt account twice, reset trust and privileged credentials, add domain controllers by fresh promotion rather than restore, and recover DNS and time. Escalation trigger: replication does not converge, or the investigations team finds attacker activity in the recovered directory.

T+48 to T+72 hours: Return applications and users in tiers. Bring back identity-dependent platforms in the agreed order, run service account and SPN validation and begin controlled user logon. Escalation trigger: a tier-zero or tier-one application cannot authenticate, or reset volumes overwhelm the service desk.

T+72 hours to T+30 days: Harden and monitor. Complete the second krbtgt reset after the required interval, rotate remaining secrets, rebuild or repair PKI and federation, remove legacy exposure and run continuous identity threat detection. Escalation trigger: any sign of re-entry restarts the decision between containment and rebuild.

Decision Points and Points of No Return

Each phase has a decision that the group must make and record. Use this list to check that every decision has an owner, evidence and a trigger for escalation.

  • Declare. Who may declare? What evidence is enough? What do we assume once declared? Owner: Incident Commander with the investigations lead. Escalation trigger: Any unexplained privileged change or encrypted domain controller.
  • Isolate. Which segments, sites and management tiers are cut off, and what is the business effect? Owner: Network lead with the Incident Commander. Escalation trigger: Evidence of lateral movement or replication of secrets.
  • Choose path. In-place eviction, forest recovery or rebuild? Owner: Incident Commander, with Sr. Leadership for rebuild. Escalation trigger: No proven clean backup, or persistence in PKI, hypervisor or federation.
  • Select restore point. Which backup, from what date, with what proof? Owner: Backup lead with the investigations lead. Escalation trigger: Evidence of dwell time longer than backup retention.
  • Restore. Which domain controller is restored first and where? Owner: Forest recovery lead. Escalation trigger: First restore fails integrity or replication checks.
  • Reset. In what order and with what interval do we reset secrets? Owner: Forest recovery lead with application owners. Escalation trigger: Evidence of forged tickets or replication of secrets.
  • Reconnect. Which sites and tiers return first, and who accepts them? Owner: Incident Commander with application owners. Escalation trigger: Any sign of attacker activity in the recovered directory.
  • Harden. What do we change now and what waits? Owner: Resilience architect with Sr. Leadership. Escalation trigger: Any sign of re-entry.

Injects

Inject #1: The directory is partly down and nobody knows why

Time frame T+0h

Situation

At 06:30 the service desk reports 1,900 sign-in failures in 40 minutes. Four domain controllers are down, two in the root region and two in the largest child domain. The remaining domain controllers show replication errors. Cached logons still work for some staff, which hides the scale of the problem from business leaders.

The investigations lead reports that a privileged account was added to Domain Admins at 05:41 and removed at 05:43, by a script running under a service account. The change cannot be explained by any ticket. Endpoint tools show encryption starting on six servers.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare a major incident and decide whether to declare an Active Directory compromise now or wait for more evidence.
  • Set up the decision log and name a scribe.
  • Establish the update cadence and the out-of-band channel.
Active Directory and Identity Engineering Lead
  • State which domain controllers are down, which hold FSMO roles and which sites depend on them.
  • Ask for the last known-good replication state and the date of the last full system state backup.
  • Advise whether to power off remaining domain controllers or keep them online for evidence.
Security Investigations and Forensics Lead
  • Preserve memory and logs from domain controllers and the privileged script host before any isolation.
  • List which privileged accounts were used from unfamiliar hosts and when.
  • State what cannot yet be trusted: logs, backups, tooling.
Backup and Recovery Lead
  • Confirm that the backup console is reachable and which recovery points exist for domain controllers.
  • Check whether the backup platform administrator accounts were used recently.
  • Protect the immutable and offline copies.
Infrastructure and Virtualization Lead
  • Confirm the state of the virtualization platform and whether management is reachable.
  • Locate break-glass credentials for hosts and storage.
  • Prepare capacity for an isolated recovery network.
Network and DNS Lead
  • Identify which segments can reach domain controllers and prepare to segment.
  • Confirm DNS resolution paths and time sources.
  • State the business effect of cutting each region.
Communications Lead
  • Move the response team to a channel that does not rely on the directory.
  • Prepare a short internal message telling staff what not to do.
  • Prepare to brief the Sr. Leadership liaison.
Sr. Leadership Liaison
  • Ask what is known, what is assumed and what decision is needed in the first hour.
  • Confirm who the board of directors will hear from.
  • Ask what the cost of delay is.

Discussion questions

  1. Who declares an Active Directory compromise, and is that authority written down?
  2. What do you assume about credentials from the first minute?
  3. Do you isolate domain controllers now, knowing it will cause outages in some regions?
  4. What channel do you use to talk to each other, and what does that channel depend on?
  5. What evidence would you collect before shutting anything off?
  6. What do you tell the board of directors in the first hour?

Facilitator notes

  • Ask each role for an individual answer before opening the discussion.
  • Look for groups that say 'we will check' without naming who and by when.
  • If the group assumes the directory can be used for messaging or authentication of the response team, ask what happens when it cannot.
  • The aim is a declaration and an operating mode, not a diagnosis.

Inject #2: Which of our credentials are safe?

Time frame T+2h

Situation

The investigations lead finds that three of the privileged accounts used from unfamiliar hosts belong to administrators who were on leave. A fourth is a service account with no owner. None of the accounts appear in the most recent privileged access review.

The service desk asks whether it should reset passwords for staff who cannot sign in. Several staff are already calling to ask whether their accounts were affected.

Discussion questions

  1. Which credentials do you presume are exposed?
  2. Do you allow the service desk to reset any passwords now?
  3. Who owns the privileged account list and where is it stored?
  4. What is the effect of disabling all privileged accounts at once?

Facilitator notes

  • Probe the reset policy during an incident: who may reset, with what verification and through which channel.
  • Listen for separation between emergency access and normal administrative accounts.

Inject #3: Replication of secrets

Time frame T+6h

Situation

The investigations lead reports directory replication requests from a host that is not a domain controller, using a privileged account. The attacker may hold the hash of every account, including krbtgt.

Sr. Leadership asks whether the company can clean up and carry on, or whether the whole directory must be rebuilt.

Discussion questions

  1. What does evidence of replication mean for the recovery path?
  2. What would you say to Sr. Leadership in plain language?
  3. What do you need to know to choose between in-place cleanup, forest recovery and a rebuild?
  4. Who decides?

Facilitator notes

  • This is the first decision point for Sr. Leadership. Ask each leader for an individual answer before asking if others agree.
  • Look for a clear statement of options with time, cost and risk.

Inject #4: Where is a clean backup?

Time frame T+8h

Situation

The backup lead reports that system state backups exist for every domain, the newest from last night. The earliest evidence of attacker activity is 21 days old. Backup retention for domain controllers is 14 days on the primary repository and 90 days in cloud storage and on tape.

The access key for cloud storage is held on the backup server, which has not been inspected.

Discussion questions

  1. Which backup do you choose and what evidence supports it?
  2. How do you check that a backup predates the intrusion?
  3. What do you do about the cloud copy whose key might be stolen?
  4. What is the cost of choosing an older backup?

Facilitator notes

  • Probe the tombstone lifetime: backups older than the tombstone lifetime cannot be used safely.
  • Look for the idea of scanning a restored copy in isolation before trusting it.

Inject #5: Building the clean room

Time frame T+12h

Situation

The infrastructure lead proposes using the existing hypervisor cluster for the clean room. The investigations lead cannot rule out that the hypervisor management plane was touched.

The network lead says an isolated network can be built in about six hours, but the team needs a way to administer it.

Discussion questions

  1. Where does the clean room run?
  2. How do administrators authenticate in the clean room?
  3. What is your plan if hypervisor management is untrusted?
  4. What is the order of build?

Facilitator notes

  • Listen for break-glass, local accounts and separate hardware.
  • Ask what the clean room depends on that might be compromised.

Inject #6: The first domain controller

Time frame T+16h

Situation

The backup lead restores the first domain controller of the root domain in the clean room. It boots, but a scan finds a scheduled task that was created 19 days ago and a modified policy.

The directory lead asks whether to continue with this restore or step back to an older one.

Discussion questions

  1. Do you continue or restore an older backup?
  2. What does the scheduled task tell you?
  3. How do you decide the earliest clean date?
  4. What does Sr. Leadership need to hear about the delay?

Facilitator notes

  • This is a decision about trading lost changes for confidence.
  • Look for a statement of which changes will be lost and who accepts the loss.

Inject #7: Seizing roles and cleaning metadata

Time frame T+24h

Situation

With a verified clean domain controller restored in the root domain, the directory lead proposes seizing the five FSMO roles, cleaning metadata for the other domain controllers and increasing the RID pool.

An engineer asks whether the other domain controllers can be restored from backup too, to save time.

Discussion questions

  1. Why seize roles instead of transferring them?
  2. Why not restore all domain controllers from backup?
  3. What must be done about the RID pool and why?
  4. What must be true before reconnecting this domain controller to anything?

Facilitator notes

  • Look for the idea that only one writable domain controller per domain is restored; the rest are rebuilt.
  • Ask about the risk of two domain controllers both thinking they own a role.

Inject #8: The krbtgt reset

Time frame T+30h

Situation

The investigations lead confirms that the krbtgt hash was exposed. The directory lead proposes resetting krbtgt twice, with a delay between resets. Application owners warn that long-running batch jobs and services may fail.

The change window is unclear because business hours are approaching in the largest region.

Discussion questions

  1. Why twice, and why the delay?
  2. Which applications will break and who knows?
  3. Do you reset now or after the first tier of applications returns?
  4. How do you verify the reset has worked in all sites?

Facilitator notes

  • Look for knowledge that tickets signed with the old secret remain valid until they expire.
  • Ask what happens to Kerberos tickets held by long-running services.

Inject #9: Trust, PKI and the hybrid path

Time frame T+36h

Situation

The PKI lead reports that an enterprise authority issued certificates for domain controllers from an unexpected host. The hybrid identity lead reports new cloud administrators and a token-signing certificate that may have been copied.

Sr. Leadership asks whether the company can restore cloud sign-in before the on-premises directory is clean.

Discussion questions

  1. What do you do about the certificate authority?
  2. What do you do about the cloud identity path?
  3. What happens if cloud sign-in is restored before the directory is clean?
  4. What do you tell Sr. Leadership about the options?

Facilitator notes

  • Ask each leader for an individual view, then ask others whether they agree.
  • Look for awareness that restoring sign-in too early restores the attacker's route.

Inject #10: Rebuilding the remaining domain controllers

Time frame T+48h

Situation

The directory is healthy in the clean room for the root and two child domains. 120 domain controllers must be rebuilt. The network lead warns that rebuilding over wide-area links could saturate them.

The infrastructure lead asks which sites need local domain controllers first.

Discussion questions

  1. In what order do you rebuild domain controllers?
  2. How do you limit replication traffic?
  3. Which sites need local domain controllers first and why?
  4. What do you do about sites that cannot reach a domain controller?

Facilitator notes

  • Look for install-from-media and for a plan by region.
  • Ask what happens to authentication in a region with no domain controller.

Inject #11: Returning applications and users

Time frame T+60h

Situation

Application owners present a list of 400 applications. 40 are tier one. Service accounts and service principal names for many are undocumented. The service desk expects 20,000 password resets.

A caller claiming to be a regional leader demands that his account be unlocked immediately.

Discussion questions

  1. In what order do applications return and who accepts each tier?
  2. How do you reset users at scale without opening a social engineering path?
  3. What happens to applications whose service accounts are unknown?
  4. How do you respond to the caller?

Facilitator notes

  • Listen for verification models that do not depend on email or chat from the compromised directory.
  • Ask who owns the final decision to release users.

Inject #12: Hardening and the second reset

Time frame T+72h to T+30d

Situation

The business is running again. The security lead warns that the attacker's path has not been fully identified. The board of directors asks what will prevent a repeat.

The directory lead reminds the group of the second reset of krbtgt and of unfinished work on PKI and federation.

Discussion questions

  1. What must be done in the first 30 days?
  2. What would make you restart the recovery?
  3. What evidence will you give the board of directors?
  4. What will you test again, and when?

Facilitator notes

  • Look for specific, owned and dated actions.
  • Ask what would trigger a rebuild even after the business has returned.

Ransomware Attack-Path Catalog

The twenty paths below are different ways an attacker could take control of Active Directory in a ransomware event. Each one has its own tabletop exercise page. Choose one to reveal during Inject 3, or run a different path each time you repeat the exercise.

Insecure Service Accounts

  • Path 1: Ransomware Operators Crack a Weak Service Account Password and Reach Domain Admin
    Offline cracking leaves little trace, so the group cannot know how far the attacker got. If the privileged account could replicate the directory, every secret is exposed and the right answer is forest recovery with full credential rotation, not a single password reset.

Credential Theft

  • Path 2: Legacy Accounts With Kerberos Pre-Authentication Disabled Are Cracked and Used to Stage Ransomware
    Without a log of every authentication request, the group cannot know which accounts were cracked. The safe assumption is that every account without pre-authentication was cracked, and that anything those accounts could reach was reached.
  • Path 7: Administrators Log On to a Shared Jump Server and the Attacker Harvests Their Credentials From Memory
    A credential used on a lower-tier host can be stolen there. Tiered administration prevents tier-zero accounts logging on to anything but tier-zero systems.
  • Path 15: A Forged-Ticket Backdoor From an Earlier Undetected Breach Survives Every Cleanup and Returns With Ransomware
    The krbtgt secret signs every ticket. With it an attacker can create tickets for any account indefinitely, regardless of password resets.
  • Path 18: Social Engineering of the Help Desk Resets a Privileged Account and Defeats Multifactor Authentication
    If the reset process can be defeated by information that is public, the process gives the attacker a free account takeover.

Excessive Privileges

  • Path 3: A Monitoring Service Account With Replication Rights Is Used to Copy Every Password in the Domain
    The right to replicate directory changes lets an account request password data. It is as dangerous as domain administrator access, even though it does not grant interactive logon.
  • Path 8: An Attacker With Write Access to Group Policy Deploys Ransomware to Every Domain-Joined System
    Group Policy is a trusted push channel. Anyone who can edit a policy linked to computers can run code on those computers.
  • Path 16: Write Access to an Account Attribute Lets an Attacker Add a Key and Authenticate as a Privileged Account
    A key written to an account lets the attacker authenticate as the account until it is removed, and a password reset does not remove it.
  • Path 17: A Chain of Inherited Permissions Leads From a Help Desk Account to Domain Admin and a Hidden Backdoor in the Protected Accounts Template
    No single permission is dangerous. The risk is in the path. Mapping paths to tier zero is a recovery task, not just a security task.

Weak Connected Infrastructure

  • Path 4: An Unconstrained Delegation Server Captures a Domain Controller Ticket During a Coerced Authentication
    A server trusted for unconstrained delegation stores the tickets of anything that authenticates to it. If a domain controller authenticates to that server, the attacker obtains a domain controller ticket.
  • Path 5: A Misconfigured Certificate Template Lets an Attacker Request a Certificate as a Domain Administrator
    A valid certificate can authenticate as the named account until it expires or is revoked. Password resets do not invalidate it.
  • Path 6: A Coerced Domain Controller Authentication Is Relayed to the Certificate Web Enrollment Service
    When a service accepts NTLM without channel binding, the attacker can forward a victim's authentication to it. A domain controller account that is relayed to an authority yields a certificate that identifies as the domain controller.
  • Path 11: The Backup Platform Is Compromised, Directory Backups Are Read and Then Destroyed Before Ransomware Runs
    The existence of a clean, verified system state backup determines whether recovery takes days or weeks. If none exists, the group must consider building a new forest.
  • Path 12: The Virtualization Management Plane Is Taken Over, Domain Controller Disks Are Copied and Hosts Are Encrypted
    If the hypervisor management plane authenticates against the directory that runs on the hypervisor, neither can start without the other. Local break-glass accounts and a separate recovery cluster resolve the loop.
  • Path 13: The Endpoint Management Server Is Compromised and Its Powerful Service Accounts Push Ransomware to Every Device
    A deployment platform can run code as SYSTEM on every device. Its administrators are effectively domain administrators.
  • Path 14: The Directory Synchronization Server Is Compromised and the Attacker Moves Between On-Premises and Cloud Identity
    An attacker can move from on-premises to cloud using connector credentials and from cloud to on-premises using synchronized rights. Both must be closed.
  • Path 19: A Poorly Secured Acquired Domain Becomes the Entry Point Into the Forest Through a Trust and Forged SID History
    A trust lets credentials from one domain have meaning in another. Without filtering, identity history can be forged to carry privileged identifiers.
  • Path 20: Legacy Mail Server Permissions on the Domain Object Let an Attacker Grant Themselves Replication Rights
    Application installers often grant permissions that are never reviewed. The right to modify permissions on the domain object is equivalent to full control of the directory.

Unpatched Systems

  • Path 9: An Unpatched Domain Controller Is Exploited Through the Netlogon Protocol to Reset Its Own Computer Account
    A domain controller is the most trusted server in the company. A known flaw on one domain controller can give an attacker the whole domain.
  • Path 10: Default Machine Account Quota and Unpatched Domain Controllers Let a Standard User Impersonate a Domain Administrator
    By default any authenticated user can add computers to the domain. That is harmless until another flaw lets a standard user impersonate a privileged one.

Active Directory Dependency Map

Active Directory does not run alone. Recovery fails when teams forget that the directory depends on other services to start, and that other services depend on the directory to run. Use this map to test whether the group knows both directions, and which loops can deadlock a recovery.

What Active Directory Needs to Start and Run

  • Network and ports. Domain controllers and clients need DNS (53), Kerberos (88), RPC endpoint mapper (135), LDAP (389), SMB (445), password change (464), LDAPS (636), global catalog (3268 and 3269) and dynamic RPC (49152 to 65535) between them. A firewall change during recovery can stop replication without any error on the domain controller.
  • DNS. Clients and domain controllers locate each other through DNS service records. If the zones are lost, replication partners cannot be found. Most environments store DNS in the directory, so the directory and DNS depend on each other, and the first restored domain controller must also provide DNS.
  • Time. Kerberos fails when clocks differ by more than the allowed skew, which is five minutes by default. The domain controller holding the PDC emulator role must take time from a reliable source; every other system follows the hierarchy.
  • Storage and virtualization. Domain controllers need disks, hosts and a hypervisor. If the hypervisor management plane authenticates with the directory, neither can start without the other. Hardware management interfaces and local host accounts break the loop.
  • Backup and restore tooling. The backup platform holds the system state needed for forest recovery. If it is domain-joined and authenticates with domain accounts, it may not start until the directory returns. A clean restore server with local credentials is the answer.
  • Certificates. Domain controllers often use certificates for secure directory traffic and certificate-based logon. A certificate authority that is domain-joined and is the issuer for domain controller certificates is both a dependent and a dependency.
  • Management, monitoring and privileged access. Monitoring, patching, remote management and privileged access vaults often run on domain accounts. During recovery they must be rebuilt or used with local accounts, and they must not be reconnected until the directory is trusted.
  • People and process. A written inventory of forests, domains, domain controllers, FSMO holders and trusts, sealed break-glass credentials and tested recovery steps. The most common cause of a slow forest recovery is a missing inventory.

What Depends on Active Directory to Start and Run

  • Tier zero identity services. DNS, DHCP authorization, certificate services, federation, directory synchronization, privileged access vaults, hypervisor management and backup. These must return first and in a defined order.
  • Sign-in and access. Single sign-on, VPN, wireless authentication, RADIUS, multifactor authentication servers and remote desktop services. If these fail, staff cannot reach anything else even when applications are running.
  • Messaging and collaboration. On-premises and hybrid messaging, chat and meeting services depend on the directory for accounts, addresses, groups and authentication. Hybrid deployments depend on synchronization and federation for cloud mailboxes.
  • Databases and clusters. Databases that use Windows authentication fail when Kerberos or NTLM fail. Failover clusters and availability groups rely on computer objects, and cluster name objects in the directory. Service accounts and service principal names are common sources of start-up failure.
  • File, print and namespaces. File servers enforce access with directory security identifiers. Distributed namespaces, shares and print queues resolve through DNS and the directory. A restored directory with different identifiers can leave data inaccessible.
  • Line-of-business and enterprise applications. Enterprise resource planning, payments, customer platforms and many in-house applications bind to the directory over LDAP, use Kerberos keytabs or accept assertions from federation. Many have service accounts that nobody owns.
  • Endpoints and servers. Domain-joined machines need a secure channel with a domain controller. Machines restored from old images or backups may have a computer account password that no longer matches, and must rejoin or reset it.
  • Non-Windows systems. Linux servers, appliances, network devices and storage arrays often use LDAP binds, Kerberos or directory groups. They keep working until a password is reset, and then fail in ways that are hard to trace.
  • Security and management tools. Endpoint detection consoles, log collectors, ticketing systems and configuration management usually authenticate staff with the directory. If they fail, the tools needed to investigate and recover are blind.

Circular Dependencies to Test

  • The directory runs on a hypervisor whose management accounts are in the directory.
  • The backup server is domain-joined and needs the directory to authenticate the restore.
  • The certificate authority needs the directory, and domain controllers need certificates from the authority.
  • The password vault holds break-glass credentials but runs on a server that needs the directory to start.
  • DNS is stored in the directory, and the directory needs DNS to find replication partners.
  • Monitoring and logging servers need the directory to log on, and are the only way to see attacker activity.
  • The service desk tools used to reset passwords authenticate with the directory.

Recovery Runbook: Disaster, Ransomware and State-Level Adversary

The recovery steps are different when the cause is a disaster, a ransomware attack or a persistent state-level adversary, because the level of trust in everything that survived is different. Use the common steps as the spine and apply the differences as the scenario develops. The steps are a summary for facilitators; confirm them against current vendor guidance and your own tested procedures before you rely on them in a real event.

Common Steps

  • 1. Confirm the failure and isolate. Establish that no healthy domain controller exists in at least one domain, or that the directory cannot be trusted. If a healthy writable domain controller remains in each domain, the better path is usually to seize roles and promote new domain controllers rather than perform a full forest recovery. Disconnect the remaining domain controllers from the network so they cannot replicate unwanted data back into the restored directory.
  • 2. Document the forest. Record every domain, domain controller, FSMO holder, site, trust, global catalog, DNS zone and functional level. Identify which domain controller in each domain has the best recent backup and is writable. Use a read-only domain controller as a source only as a last resort; it does not hold secrets in the same way.
  • 3. Choose backups younger than the tombstone lifetime. Backups older than the tombstone lifetime, which is 180 days in current forests by default, can reintroduce deleted objects and cause lingering object problems. Choose the newest backup that meets the proof of cleanliness required by the scenario.
  • 4. Restore one writable domain controller per domain, root domain first. Restore the system state offline into an isolated network, non-authoritatively, in directory services restore mode. Start with the forest root domain, then each child domain. Do not restore other domain controllers from backup.
  • 5. Make the first controller authoritative for the domain. After the restore, mark SYSVOL as authoritative if required, seize all five FSMO roles on the restored domain controller, and remove the metadata of every domain controller that was not restored. Raise the available RID pool, invalidate the current pool, reset the domain controller computer account password and configure the domain controller as a DNS server and a global catalog.
  • 6. Fix time and DNS. Set the PDC emulator to take time from a reliable source. Check that the service records and zones are present and that clients can find the restored domain controller.
  • 7. Reset the secrets that must change. Reset the krbtgt password twice per domain with enough time between resets for replication and for maximum ticket lifetime. Reset trust passwords on both sides of every trust. Reset domain controller computer account passwords and the directory services restore mode password.
  • 8. Rebuild remaining domain controllers by promotion. Install clean servers, promote them and use install-from-media for large domains to limit replication traffic. Verify replication, SYSVOL and the global catalog on each before it serves clients.
  • 9. Validate before reconnecting. Check replication, SYSVOL, DNS, time, Kerberos and LDAP. Compare privileged group membership, delegation and policies to a known baseline. Test sign-in with a small group before releasing a region.
  • 10. Return applications and users in tiers. Reconnect by site and by tier. Reset or repair secure channels for member servers. Reset service accounts as applications are brought back, and release users in waves with verified password resets.
  • 11. Harden and monitor. Implement tiered administration, privileged access workstations, long random passwords or managed service accounts, patched domain controllers and identity threat detection. Retest the plan.

If the Cause Is a Disaster

  • Typical causes: storage or hypervisor failure, site loss, corrupt replication, a failed schema change or an operator error that deletes or damages many objects.
  • Trust: the surviving backups are normally trustworthy, and credentials are not presumed stolen. Choose the newest backup within the tombstone lifetime that predates the corruption.
  • Focus: speed and accuracy of the common steps; the main risk is a mistake in role seizure, metadata cleanup or replication that makes the situation worse.
  • Credential resets: reset krbtgt twice only if there is a security reason; otherwise reset domain controller computer accounts and trusts as the procedure requires.
  • Time to first sign-in is driven by the time to restore a domain controller, which depends on backup size, storage speed and network throughput.

If the Cause Is Ransomware

  • Typical causes: any of the twenty attack paths in this series. The attacker usually holds privileged credentials, has often copied the directory and may have destroyed or poisoned backups.
  • Trust: nothing that survived is trusted until it is proven. Backups must predate the intrusion and be scanned in isolation. The recovery hosts, the backup platform and the administrative workstations must be clean.
  • Focus: evidence-based choice of restore point, a clean room, and credential resets for every privileged, service and domain controller account in addition to krbtgt and trusts.
  • Remove persistence in the directory: changes to protected account templates, delegation, group membership, Group Policy, scheduled tasks delivered by policy, certificate templates, key attributes and identity history.
  • Rebuild or repair tier-zero adjacent systems: certificate authorities, federation, directory synchronization, privileged access vaults and management tools. Restore only what you must; rebuild everything else.
  • Release users in waves with strong verification, because help desk impersonation is a common second attack.

If the Cause Is a State-Level Adversary

  • Typical causes: a patient adversary that has been present for months, has multiple footholds including cloud tenants, firmware, hypervisors and software suppliers, and may not announce itself.
  • Trust: assume the adversary controls the keys, tickets and secrets of the existing forest. Consider all existing administrative tools, accounts and some hardware untrusted.
  • Bias toward rebuild: build a new tier-zero environment on new or verified hardware and a new network with new certificate authorities, new administrative accounts and new secrets. Migrate users and groups selectively, without carrying identity history or old credentials.
  • Parallel operation: run the old forest in isolation for forensics and for data that must be migrated, and keep it disconnected from the new environment.
  • Out-of-band everything: communicate, authenticate and approve decisions using channels that were never connected to the old directory, and brief government and legal contacts early.
  • Extended monitoring and threat hunting for months, with a defined trigger to rebuild again if re-entry is found.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Owns the overall response, the decision log and the cadence of updates; decides when to declare an Active Directory compromise and when to invoke forest recovery.
  • Keeps the room on decisions rather than detail and asks each role for an individual answer before opening discussion.
  • Confirms that every decision has an owner, a time and an evidence basis before moving on.

Active Directory and Identity Engineering Lead (Forest Recovery Lead)

  • Owns the technical recovery plan: which domain controller to restore first, which FSMO roles to seize, which metadata to clean, and the order in which domains return.
  • Maintains the authoritative inventory of forests, domains, domain controllers, sites, trusts, functional levels and FSMO holders.
  • Validates replication, SYSVOL, DNS and Kerberos health before declaring any milestone met.

Security Investigations and Forensics Lead

  • Determines how the attacker got in, which credentials and systems were touched and whether persistence exists in Active Directory, in backups or in connected infrastructure.
  • Advises on the last known-clean point and tells the recovery team which backups, images and accounts cannot be trusted.
  • Runs identity threat detection and compromise assessment on the rebuilt environment before users return.

Backup and Recovery Lead

  • Identifies candidate system state and image backups per domain, proves their age and integrity and confirms that they predate the intrusion.
  • Restores into an isolated recovery network and owns the safekeeping of backup credentials, catalogs and immutable copies.
  • Reports honestly on what cannot be restored and how long each restore will take at scale.

Infrastructure and Virtualization Lead

  • Provides compute, storage and hypervisor capacity for the clean-room recovery environment and rebuilds the management plane if it was compromised.
  • Controls out-of-band access, hardware management interfaces and the physical or virtual console path to domain controllers.
  • Reports dependencies between the hypervisor management plane and the directory.

Network and DNS Lead

  • Builds and enforces isolation: segmentation of the recovery network, blocking of domain controller traffic from untrusted segments and controlled reconnection by site.
  • Owns DNS recovery, the SRV records that let clients find domain controllers and the forwarders, conditional forwarders and DHCP options that depend on them.
  • Confirms time synchronization sources so that Kerberos does not fail on clock skew.

PKI and Certificate Services Lead

  • Assesses whether certificate authorities, templates or issued certificates were abused and decides whether the PKI can be trusted, repaired or must be rebuilt.
  • Plans revocation, certificate re-issuance and the order in which domain controllers, VPN, wireless and smart card authentication return.
  • Protects the offline root and its key material.

Hybrid Identity and Cloud Lead

  • Owns directory synchronization, federation and cloud identity: containing the synchronization server, rotating connector and federation secrets and protecting cloud administrator access.
  • Decides when synchronization is paused and when it resumes against the recovered directory.
  • Confirms that cloud tenants are not being used to re-enter the recovered environment.

Application and Service Owners

  • Report which applications need directory authentication to start, which use service accounts, SPNs or Kerberos delegation and which can run in a degraded mode.
  • Supply start-up order and validation tests for their applications and confirm break-glass local accounts.
  • Accept or reject a restored identity layer as ready for their tier.

Service Desk Lead

  • Prepares for mass credential resets with verified identity procedures that do not rely on the compromised directory and are resistant to social engineering.
  • Manages the queue, scripts and out-of-band channels for staff who cannot sign in.
  • Feeds early indicators of misuse of the reset process to the investigations lead.

Privacy, Legal and Regulatory Counsel

  • Advises on notification duties, evidence preservation, law-enforcement contact and ransom-payment questions.
  • Reviews all external statements and approves what is said to regulators, customers and insurers.
  • Tracks contractual duties that are triggered by loss of identity services.

Communications Lead

  • Maintains a communication path that does not depend on the compromised directory, such as out-of-band messaging and personal contact trees for key roles.
  • Drafts internal and external messages and keeps them consistent with the decision log.
  • Controls the cadence of staff updates and tells people what not to do, such as reusing passwords or attaching unmanaged devices.

Sr. Leadership Liaison

  • Represents senior leaders, and the board of directors where needed, and carries decisions to them in plain language: the options, the cost, the time and the risk of each.
  • Brings back decisions on business priority, acceptance of risk and spending.
  • Does not direct technical steps; asks what is needed to decide and what happens if the decision is delayed.

Business Continuity Coordinator

  • Maps the order in which business processes return as identity services return and tracks manual workarounds.
  • Confirms that business owners know their manual procedures and that those procedures do not depend on the directory.
  • Records the business impact of each recovery decision.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Know your tier zero

Document every system that can control the directory: domain controllers, certificate authorities, federation, synchronization, hypervisor management, backup, privileged access vaults and any tool that can run code on domain controllers. Administer them from dedicated clean workstations and with separate accounts.

Keep an immutable, offline copy of directory system state

Maintain backups that the production credentials cannot delete, in a separately administered location, with a tested restore procedure and a recent restore time measured at scale. Restore into a clean room at least once a year.

Break the circular dependencies

Give hypervisors, backup, the password vault and monitoring local or separately managed authentication. Test the cold start of the whole estate, not just of the directory.

Maintain a recovery inventory

Keep an offline, current record of forests, domains, domain controllers, FSMO holders, sites, trusts, DNS zones, service accounts and certificate authorities. Review it quarterly.

Reduce the paths to tier zero

Map and cut permission chains, remove replication rights and unconstrained delegation, retire legacy authentication, disable unnecessary web enrollment and review delegated Group Policy rights.

Make service accounts safe

Inventory service accounts, assign owners, use managed service accounts where possible, use long random secrets elsewhere and remove them from privileged groups.

Patch and enforce on domain controllers

Treat patch status and enforcement mode on domain controllers as a tier-zero measure with named owners and weekly exception review.

Build a break-glass model

Keep sealed local credentials for hosts, storage, network devices and tier-zero systems, with a process to open them and a record of who did.

Design the reset process for a crisis

Define verified channels, approvals and scripts for mass password resets and make sure the process cannot be abused by impersonation.

Separate cloud and on-premises administration

Use cloud-only administrator accounts, protect the synchronization and federation servers and know how to revoke every cloud session in an hour.

Decide in advance what Sr. Leadership will decide

Write down the options for in-place eviction, forest recovery and rebuild with expected time, cost and risk, and the conditions that trigger an escalation. Capture them in the playbook.

Measure and rehearse

Record time to first domain controller, time to first sign-in, time to tier-one applications and time to full user release. Repeat the exercise with a different attack path each time.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Capture individual notes. Ask each participant to write down the three decisions they found hardest and one thing they would change before a real event.
  2. Review the decision log. Walk the log in order and mark each decision as made with evidence, made without evidence or deferred.
  3. Compare to the checklist. Compare the group's sequence with the recovery checklist and discuss every difference.
  4. Name gaps and owners. Record each gap with an owner, a date and a measure of done.
  5. Agree retest. Agree when the exercise will be repeated with a different attack path.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group after individual notes are written.

  1. At what point did your group first say the words 'Active Directory compromise', and was that early enough?
  2. Which assumptions about credentials turned out to be hard to defend?
  3. What would you do if the newest clean backup were older than your longest tolerance for lost changes?
  4. Who in your plan has authority to choose a rebuild over a restore, and have they agreed to that role?
  5. Which dependency loop would stop your recovery first: hypervisor, backup, PKI, DNS, vault or monitoring?
  6. How would you reset 40,000 passwords in 48 hours without exposing a social engineering path?
  7. What would you do if the attacker had access to your cloud identity and not just your on-premises directory?
  8. What do your application owners know about service accounts, keytabs and service principal names today?
  9. What would you tell the board of directors at hour 6, hour 24 and hour 72?
  10. Which of the twenty attack paths would hurt you most today, and what is one change you could make this month?
  11. Who had the authority to declare an Active Directory compromise, and did everyone agree on that before the exercise?
  12. What did you assume about the safety of backups, and what evidence would you need to turn the assumption into a fact?
  13. Which dependency surprised you most: DNS, time, PKI, hypervisor, backup, hybrid identity or an application?
  14. Where did the plan assume access to a domain account, a domain-joined tool or a domain-based channel?
  15. How long would the first restored domain controller really take, from decision to a verified, isolated, working directory?
  16. What would you tell Sr. Leadership about time to first logon, time to tier-one applications and the risk of reinfection?
