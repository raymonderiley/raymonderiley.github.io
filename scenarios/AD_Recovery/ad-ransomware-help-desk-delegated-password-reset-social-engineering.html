Social Engineering of the Help Desk Resets a Privileged Account and Defeats Multifactor Authentication

Active Directory Compromise & Recovery

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a ransomware intrusion that begins with a phone call. An attacker poses as an administrator who has lost a device and persuades the service desk to reset the password and register a new authentication method. The reset right was delegated broadly, and the desk had no way to verify identity beyond personal details.

Participants are the working-level incident team, the service desk lead and a liaison to Sr. Leadership. The exercise tests identity verification in the reset process, the scope of delegated reset rights and how to run mass resets during recovery without handing the attacker a tool.

A full run takes three to four hours, and Inject 1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Attack Path Category

Credential Theft. This is attack path 18 of 20 in the Active Directory ransomware series. Each path is a different way an attacker could take control of the directory, and each leads to the same recovery problem: restoring a trustworthy Active Directory while the attacker, or what the attacker left behind, may still be in the environment.

Scenario

The service desk can reset passwords for most accounts, including some privileged ones, because the delegation was set on a high-level organizational unit. Verification uses an employee number and a manager name, both of which appear in public sources and previous breaches. Self-service registration allows a new authentication method after a password reset.

An attacker calls twice, resets a regional administrator account, registers a new authentication method and logs on. The account has rights on a management server, which leads to domain administrator credentials. Ransomware is deployed a week later.

What Participants Know at the Start

  • The service desk reset a regional administrator account two weeks ago.
  • A new authentication method was registered that the administrator does not recognize.
  • Ransomware is running on servers.

What Is Not Yet Known

  • How many other accounts were reset by social engineering.
  • Whether staff in other regions were targeted.
  • Whether the attacker has access to the reset tool.

Likely Indicators

  • Resets of privileged accounts followed by a new authentication method registration.
  • Calls from unusual numbers asking for resets.
  • Privileged accounts reset by help desk staff.
  • Sign-ins from new locations soon after resets.

Exercise Objectives

  • Declare and scope. Participants declare an Active Directory compromise on the evidence available, state the assumptions they are making about credentials and name who has the authority to declare it.
  • Choose a recovery path. The group compares in-place eviction, forest recovery and rebuild, and records the evidence that supports the choice and the conditions that would change it.
  • Prove a clean restore point. The group states how it will prove a backup predates the intrusion and what it will do if it cannot.
  • Sequence the recovery. The group orders restoration of the first domain controller, FSMO roles, DNS, time, credential resets and additional domain controllers, and explains why that order matters.
  • Protect the recovery. Participants name the controls that stop reinfection during recovery: isolation, tiered administration, credential hygiene and monitoring.
  • Return business services. The group agrees the order in which identity-dependent applications and users return and who accepts each tier as ready.
  • Harden identity verification. The group states what the service desk must verify for each class of account, and what it must never do.
  • Run mass resets safely. The group designs a reset process that scales and resists impersonation.

Ground Rules

  • This is a no-fault exercise. The aim is to find gaps in plans, authority and tooling, not to assign blame to people or teams.
  • Treat all information in the scenario as true unless a facilitator corrects it. Do not fight the scenario by assuming a control worked unless it is documented.
  • Assume the domain directory cannot be trusted. Do not assume you can use domain accounts, domain-joined tools or domain-based messaging unless the group has agreed how.
  • Ask each role for an individual answer first, then open the discussion. Where the room is quiet, ask the next person whether they agree and why.
  • Use real names for roles and real systems from your own environment where you can, but do not use real credentials, real hostnames of sensitive systems or live data.
  • Do not run tools or commands on live systems during the exercise. Describe what you would do and what you would expect to see.
  • Record decisions, assumptions and open questions in the decision log as the exercise runs.
  • Facilitators may pause to correct a technical error but should prefer asking a question over giving the answer.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+0 to T+2 hours: Detect and declare. Privileged changes, encrypted domain controllers, failed logons at scale or an attacker message arrive without a known cause. Escalation trigger: any unexplained change to Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins or the domain controller OU, or any encrypted domain controller, means declare an Active Directory compromise and treat every domain credential as exposed until proven otherwise.

T+2 to T+6 hours: Contain and scope. Isolate domain controllers and management tiers, disable suspect accounts and preserve volatile evidence. Escalation trigger: evidence of replication of directory secrets (DCSync), theft of the ntds.dit database or a krbtgt hash means the whole forest is compromised and in-place cleanup alone is not defensible.

T+6 to T+12 hours: Decide the recovery path. Choose between in-place eviction, forest recovery from a known-clean backup, or rebuild into a new forest. Escalation trigger: no backup can be shown to predate the intrusion, or the attacker has persistence in the configuration partition, the certificate services or the hypervisor management plane. In those cases escalate to Sr. Leadership for a rebuild or a controlled restore with extended monitoring.

T+12 to T+24 hours: Build the clean room and restore the first domain controller. Stand up the isolated network, restore one writable domain controller per domain from clean backup (root domain first), seize FSMO roles, clean metadata and reset the credentials that must change first. Escalation trigger: the first restore fails integrity or replication checks, or the backup catalog or backup credentials are themselves compromised.

T+24 to T+48 hours: Restore directory services at scale. Reset the krbtgt account twice, reset trust and privileged credentials, add domain controllers by fresh promotion rather than restore, and recover DNS and time. Escalation trigger: replication does not converge, or the investigations team finds attacker activity in the recovered directory.

T+48 to T+72 hours: Return applications and users in tiers. Bring back identity-dependent platforms in the agreed order, run service account and SPN validation and begin controlled user logon. Escalation trigger: a tier-zero or tier-one application cannot authenticate, or reset volumes overwhelm the service desk.

T+72 hours to T+30 days: Harden and monitor. Complete the second krbtgt reset after the required interval, rotate remaining secrets, rebuild or repair PKI and federation, remove legacy exposure and run continuous identity threat detection. Escalation trigger: any sign of re-entry restarts the decision between containment and rebuild.

Path-specific escalation trigger

If privileged account resets cannot be reviewed within a few hours, treat all privileged accounts as compromised and escalate.

Recovery Considerations for This Attack Path

Verification is a control

If the reset process can be defeated by information that is public, the process gives the attacker a free account takeover.

Scoping delegation

Remove reset rights over privileged accounts from the service desk and restrict them to a small group with stronger verification.

Mass reset design

Use verified manager approval, in-person or video verification and one-time reset tokens delivered by channels the attacker cannot reach. Do not use email while it is untrusted.

Closing the path

Require strong verification for privileged resets, alert on reset followed by new method registration and train the desk on current social engineering patterns.

Common Failures to Watch For

  • Using personal facts as proof of identity.
  • Delegating reset rights over privileged accounts.
  • Running a mass reset through an unverified channel.

Active Directory Recovery Checklist for Facilitators

Use this list to judge the quality of the group's decisions. It is deliberately ordered. Groups that restore many domain controllers at once, reconnect to production early or skip credential resets are making the mistakes that cause reinfection.

  1. Isolate and preserve. Cut domain controllers and management tiers off from untrusted networks, keep at least one intact domain controller powered off and disconnected as evidence and as a possible restore source, and capture memory and logs before any rebuild.
  2. Establish an out-of-band operating mode. Move command and communication to channels that do not need the directory, confirm local and break-glass credentials in a sealed store and agree on who may approve decisions.
  3. Inventory what exists. Document forests, domains, trusts, sites, domain controllers, FSMO holders, functional levels, DNS zones, certificate authorities and every system that binds to the directory. Without this inventory a forest recovery stalls on the first question.
  4. Select and prove a clean backup. Identify system state backups per domain that predate the earliest evidence of compromise, verify integrity and scan restored copies in isolation. Prefer the oldest backup that still meets the business tolerance for lost changes.
  5. Restore one writable domain controller per domain. Restore the root domain first, offline and non-authoritatively, then each child domain. Do not reconnect these domain controllers to production.
  6. Seize roles and clean metadata. Seize all five FSMO roles on the restored domain controller, remove metadata for every domain controller that was not restored, increase the RID pool, reset the domain controller computer account password and set DNS and global catalog roles deliberately.
  7. Reset what the attacker may hold. Reset the krbtgt password twice with the required interval between resets, reset trust passwords, privileged and service account secrets, DSRM passwords and the computer account passwords of domain controllers, and rotate hybrid identity and federation secrets.
  8. Rebuild the remaining domain controllers by promotion. Install clean operating systems and promote them; do not restore the rest of the fleet from backup. Use install-from-media for large domains to limit replication traffic.
  9. Validate before reconnecting. Check replication, SYSVOL, DNS, time, Kerberos and LDAP health, compare privileged group membership and delegation to a known baseline and run an identity compromise assessment.
  10. Return applications and users in tiers. Reconnect by site and by tier, confirm service accounts and SPNs, bring up identity-dependent platforms in the agreed order and then release users with controlled password resets.
  11. Harden and monitor. Implement tiered administration, remove excessive rights, patch domain controllers and management servers, protect service accounts, enable identity threat detection and rehearse the plan.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Owns the overall response, the decision log and the cadence of updates; decides when to declare an Active Directory compromise and when to invoke forest recovery.
  • Keeps the room on decisions rather than detail and asks each role for an individual answer before opening discussion.
  • Confirms that every decision has an owner, a time and an evidence basis before moving on.

Active Directory and Identity Engineering Lead (Forest Recovery Lead)

  • Owns the technical recovery plan: which domain controller to restore first, which FSMO roles to seize, which metadata to clean, and the order in which domains return.
  • Maintains the authoritative inventory of forests, domains, domain controllers, sites, trusts, functional levels and FSMO holders.
  • Validates replication, SYSVOL, DNS and Kerberos health before declaring any milestone met.

Security Investigations and Forensics Lead

  • Determines how the attacker got in, which credentials and systems were touched and whether persistence exists in Active Directory, in backups or in connected infrastructure.
  • Advises on the last known-clean point and tells the recovery team which backups, images and accounts cannot be trusted.
  • Runs identity threat detection and compromise assessment on the rebuilt environment before users return.

Backup and Recovery Lead

  • Identifies candidate system state and image backups per domain, proves their age and integrity and confirms that they predate the intrusion.
  • Restores into an isolated recovery network and owns the safekeeping of backup credentials, catalogs and immutable copies.
  • Reports honestly on what cannot be restored and how long each restore will take at scale.

Infrastructure and Virtualization Lead

  • Provides compute, storage and hypervisor capacity for the clean-room recovery environment and rebuilds the management plane if it was compromised.
  • Controls out-of-band access, hardware management interfaces and the physical or virtual console path to domain controllers.
  • Reports dependencies between the hypervisor management plane and the directory.

Network and DNS Lead

  • Builds and enforces isolation: segmentation of the recovery network, blocking of domain controller traffic from untrusted segments and controlled reconnection by site.
  • Owns DNS recovery, the SRV records that let clients find domain controllers and the forwarders, conditional forwarders and DHCP options that depend on them.
  • Confirms time synchronization sources so that Kerberos does not fail on clock skew.

PKI and Certificate Services Lead

  • Assesses whether certificate authorities, templates or issued certificates were abused and decides whether the PKI can be trusted, repaired or must be rebuilt.
  • Plans revocation, certificate re-issuance and the order in which domain controllers, VPN, wireless and smart card authentication return.
  • Protects the offline root and its key material.

Hybrid Identity and Cloud Lead

  • Owns directory synchronization, federation and cloud identity: containing the synchronization server, rotating connector and federation secrets and protecting cloud administrator access.
  • Decides when synchronization is paused and when it resumes against the recovered directory.
  • Confirms that cloud tenants are not being used to re-enter the recovered environment.

Application and Service Owners

  • Report which applications need directory authentication to start, which use service accounts, SPNs or Kerberos delegation and which can run in a degraded mode.
  • Supply start-up order and validation tests for their applications and confirm break-glass local accounts.
  • Accept or reject a restored identity layer as ready for their tier.

Service Desk Lead

  • Prepares for mass credential resets with verified identity procedures that do not rely on the compromised directory and are resistant to social engineering.
  • Manages the queue, scripts and out-of-band channels for staff who cannot sign in.
  • Feeds early indicators of misuse of the reset process to the investigations lead.

Privacy, Legal and Regulatory Counsel

  • Advises on notification duties, evidence preservation, law-enforcement contact and ransom-payment questions.
  • Reviews all external statements and approves what is said to regulators, customers and insurers.
  • Tracks contractual duties that are triggered by loss of identity services.

Communications Lead

  • Maintains a communication path that does not depend on the compromised directory, such as out-of-band messaging and personal contact trees for key roles.
  • Drafts internal and external messages and keeps them consistent with the decision log.
  • Controls the cadence of staff updates and tells people what not to do, such as reusing passwords or attaching unmanaged devices.

Sr. Leadership Liaison

  • Represents senior leaders, and the board of directors where needed, and carries decisions to them in plain language: the options, the cost, the time and the risk of each.
  • Brings back decisions on business priority, acceptance of risk and spending.
  • Does not direct technical steps; asks what is needed to decide and what happens if the decision is delayed.

Business Continuity Coordinator

  • Maps the order in which business processes return as identity services return and tracks manual workarounds.
  • Confirms that business owners know their manual procedures and that those procedures do not depend on the directory.
  • Records the business impact of each recovery decision.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Capture individual notes. Ask each participant to write down the three decisions they found hardest and one thing they would change before a real event.
  2. Review the decision log. Walk the log in order and mark each decision as made with evidence, made without evidence or deferred.
  3. Compare to the checklist. Compare the group's sequence with the recovery checklist and discuss every difference.
  4. Name gaps and owners. Record each gap with an owner, a date and a measure of done.
  5. Agree retest. Agree when the exercise will be repeated with a different attack path.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group after individual notes are written.

  1. What does your service desk verify before resetting a privileged account?
  2. Who can reset administrator accounts in your directory?
  3. How would you run 40,000 resets in 48 hours safely?
  4. Who had the authority to declare an Active Directory compromise, and did everyone agree on that before the exercise?
  5. What did you assume about the safety of backups, and what evidence would you need to turn the assumption into a fact?
  6. Which dependency surprised you most: DNS, time, PKI, hypervisor, backup, hybrid identity or an application?
  7. Where did the plan assume access to a domain account, a domain-joined tool or a domain-based channel?
  8. How long would the first restored domain controller really take, from decision to a verified, isolated, working directory?
  9. What would you tell Sr. Leadership about time to first logon, time to tier-one applications and the risk of reinfection?
