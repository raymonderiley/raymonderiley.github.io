AWS Route53 Health Check Flap Causing Global DNS Flapping

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Route 53 health check that flaps between healthy and unhealthy, causing failover and latency records to switch traffic back and forth between regions. Users see intermittent errors, sessions break and capacity in each region swings between idle and overloaded.

Participants are working-level: cloud network and DNS engineers, platform and SRE engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of health check design, TTLs, routing policies and the interaction of DNS with caching resolvers and clients.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Alderbrook Telehealth runs its patient portal in us-east-1 and us-west-2. Route 53 uses a failover policy for the primary hostname with health checks that call a '/health' endpoint on each region's load balancer every 10 seconds with a failure threshold of 3. TTL is 30 seconds. The health endpoint checks the database and an external identity provider.

At 08:05 on Monday, the identity provider starts responding slowly. The '/health' endpoint in us-east-1 begins failing intermittently because it includes the provider call. Route 53 health checkers in multiple regions disagree, so the check flips between healthy and unhealthy every few minutes. DNS answers alternate between regions. us-west-2, sized for 30 percent of traffic, receives full load, scales slowly, and its own health endpoint begins failing. By 08:25, both regions flap, and some resolvers return NXDOMAIN-like failures when both are unhealthy.

What the team does not yet know: the health endpoint depends on a non-critical external service, several large ISP resolvers ignore the 30-second TTL and cache for minutes, sessions are not shared across regions, and the secondary region's Auto Scaling is bound by a warm-up period of ten minutes.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize DNS-layer flapping. Participants read Route 53 health check status, CloudWatch metrics and query logs to tell DNS flapping from application failure.
  • Stabilize routing. The team decides whether to disable the health check, fix the endpoint, set a static weight or use calculated health checks, and understands each effect.
  • Account for caching. Participants consider resolver caching, client behavior and TTL realities when predicting recovery.
  • Protect capacity and sessions. Engineers pre-scale the secondary region, handle session continuity and avoid thundering herds.
  • Fix health check design. The team decides what a health check should and should not test.
  • Improve resilience practice. Participants review failover testing, regional capacity ratios and monitoring.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Name the Route 53 record, health check or routing policy involved in every statement.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Intermittent errors and flapping health checks lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Routing is frozen to a stable state.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. The unhealthy dependency in the health endpoint is found.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. Health check and capacity changes are planned.

T+03:00 — Execution. Health check, routing and scaling changes are applied.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. DNS caches converge and error rates fall.

T+06:00 — Validation. User flows and session handling are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal routing resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Health checks flap and traffic ping-pongs between regions

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:20. Patients report being logged out and video visits dropping. Clinicians see intermittent errors. The Route 53 console shows the primary health check alternating healthy and unhealthy every two to three minutes. CloudWatch HealthCheckStatus oscillates between 1 and 0.

The DNS engineer sees checker regions disagree: North America regions report healthy while Europe and Asia report unhealthy for us-east-1, and failure reasons show 'HTTP status 503' and timeouts. The '/health' endpoint takes 4 to 9 seconds because it calls the identity provider.

The secondary region has 30 percent capacity, scales slowly and is now at 85 percent CPU. Some resolvers cache answers beyond the 30-second TTL. The AWS Health Dashboard shows no events. The identity provider status page shows degraded performance.

Facilitator provides: the health check configuration, the checker disagreement summary, the endpoint latency data and regional load graphs.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide to stabilize routing before fixing the root cause.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Network & DNS Engineering
  • Explain the flapping and propose a stable routing state.
  • Describe the effect of TTL and resolver caching on changes.
  • Plan how to override the health check.
Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering
  • Pre-scale the secondary region.
  • Plan a shallow health endpoint.
  • Check sessions.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Identify patient flows needing continuity.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Prepare a degraded identity mode.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Run external synthetic checks.
  • List missing alarms.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about checker disagreement.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform clinical operations.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan phone visits.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask who designed the health check.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell DNS flapping from a real application outage?
  2. Why do the checker regions disagree?
  3. What is the safest stable routing state and how fast will it take effect?
  4. How do resolver caches affect your recovery estimate?
  5. Should the health endpoint call the identity provider?
  6. How do you protect the secondary region from overload?
  7. What happens to sessions during each switch?
  8. What should patients and clinicians be told?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the identity provider dependency until the SRE examines the health endpoint.
  • If players want to set TTL to zero, ask what happens with resolvers that ignore it.
  • Look for the idea of choosing stability over automatic failover temporarily.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Inject #2: Disabling the health check pins traffic to a degraded region

Time frame T+00:20 · Variant A

Situation

The DNS engineer disables the health check so the primary record always answers us-east-1. Traffic stabilizes in us-east-1, but the region is slow because of the identity provider and some logins fail.

Pinning removes automatic protection if the region truly fails.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Accept and record the risk.
Cloud Network & DNS Engineering
  • Document the risk and set a time limit for the override.
Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering
  • Prepare manual failover steps.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Add external checks to compensate.

Discussion questions

  1. What do you lose by disabling a health check?
  2. How long is the override acceptable?
  3. What manual trigger replaces it?
  4. Who decides to fail over manually?

Facilitator notes

  • Tests risk acceptance.
  • Look for a time limit.
  • Check compensating monitoring.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes stable availability over perfect routing and decides on freezing DNS changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes disabling or overriding the health check.
  • Decides on patient-facing messaging.

Cloud Network & DNS Engineering

  • Reviews health check status by checker region, failure reasons and the routing policy.
  • Plans a change to a stable state such as a fixed weight or inverted health check, considering TTL and caching.
  • Checks Route 53 query logging and resolver behavior.
  • Designs a calculated or CloudWatch-alarm-based health check.

Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering

  • Pre-scales the secondary region and checks warm-up and quotas.
  • Fixes the health endpoint to be shallow and independent of external providers.
  • Reviews session handling and sticky behavior.
  • Plans traffic shifting in weighted steps.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners confirm which patient flows require session continuity.
  • The service desk handles patient and clinician calls.
  • Owners prepare a degraded mode for the identity provider.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of health check status, DNS query counts per record and regional load.
  • Confirms synthetic checks from outside AWS.
  • Checks AWS Health and the identity provider status.
  • Creates alarms for health check flapping.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a case on health check behavior and checker disagreement.
  • Asks about any Route 53 events and resolver guidance.
  • Requests review of the design.
  • Records the guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for telehealth appointments and informs clinical operations.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates fallback phone visits.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.14 redundancy; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses patient care and contractual impact.
  • Captures evidence of design decisions.
  • Logs findings on health check design.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Keep DNS health checks shallow and independent of external providers. Use a separate deep readiness check for load balancers and alarms, and use CloudWatch alarm-based or calculated health checks to avoid single noisy signals.

Design failover for real capacity. The secondary region must carry full production load or scale to it within the TTL window, with quotas pre-approved and failover tested under load.

Plan for resolver caching: choose TTLs for realistic convergence, avoid designs that need instant DNS change and provide alternate paths for critical staff.

Design sessions to survive region change using shared or replicated session state, or stateless tokens.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.14) and SOC 2 expect documented health check standards, tested failover and monitoring of routing stability.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on the health check design and the decision to override.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

  6. How did the secondary region cope?

  8. How did you decide when to re-enable the check?
  9. How accurate were messages to clinical staff and patients?
  10. What did AWS support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How is failover tested today?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. Who owns health check design?
