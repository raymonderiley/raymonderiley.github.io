Serverless API Gateway Throttle Cascade Across Multi-Cloud Mesh

Multi-Cloud (AWS & Azure, No On-Prem)

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a throttle on a serverless API gateway that triggers retries across a mesh of Lambda and Azure Functions, so account-level concurrency limits are reached and unrelated services start failing.

Participants are working-level: API platform engineers, cloud platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS and Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of concurrency limits, throttling responses, retry budgets, reserved concurrency and priority tiers.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Rowan Mobility runs a ride-booking platform on a serverless mesh: API Gateway and Lambda in AWS, API Management and Functions in Azure, with services calling each other synchronously. Account-level Lambda concurrency is 3,000 and Function plans have scale caps. Retry policies are default on most services.

At 18:00 on Friday evening peak, a promotion drives 6 times normal booking requests. The booking function throttles. Callers retry, which raises concurrent executions. The account concurrency limit is reached, so unrelated services such as driver location updates and payment callbacks are throttled. Azure Functions scale up slowly and return errors. Rides fail to book and drivers appear offline.

What the team does not yet know: the promotion cannot be stopped without a marketing decision, reserved concurrency was never configured, a partner integration is retrying aggressively, and a payment callback backlog is growing.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a throttle cascade. Participants trace throttling from one service to account-level limits.
  • Protect critical paths. The team reserves capacity for driver location and payment callbacks.
  • Cut amplification. Engineers curb retries and shed noncritical load.
  • Raise limits and scale. Participants request concurrency increases and tune scale caps.
  • Manage the promotion. The team decides how to reduce demand.
  • Improve serverless resilience. Participants review reserved concurrency, retry budgets and tiering.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Reserve capacity for critical services before shedding anything else, and record the reservations set.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Failed bookings and offline drivers lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Critical capacity is reserved and amplification cut.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Limits and retries are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A restoration and demand plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Changes are applied in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Service stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Behavior is validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

The platform engineer finds booking throttles triggered retries that consumed account concurrency, throttling driver location and payment callbacks.

  • Authorize reserving concurrency.

  • Request increases.
  • Raise caps.
  • Validate.
Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Decide demand cuts.

  1. How do you trace a cascade from one throttled service?

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes containing throttling so it does not cascade and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

API Platform Engineering

  • Reads concurrency, throttle and retry metrics.
  • Sets reserved concurrency for critical functions.
  • Reduces retries and adds queues.
  • Plans tiers.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Platform Engineering (AWS & Azure)

  • Requests limit increases.
  • Raises scale caps in Azure.
  • Validates both clouds.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Assesses booking and driver safety service availability.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Reserve concurrency for critical functions and tier services by importance.

Use retry budgets with backoff and queue between services.

Load test promotions against account limits.

Alert on concurrency utilization.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.14, 8.16) and SOC 2 expect capacity and availability evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on critical path protection and curbing amplification.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

  1. How did you trace the cascade?
  2. What did you protect first?
  3. How did you handle retries?
  4. How did you handle limits?
  5. How did you handle the promotion?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AWS & Microsoft Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
