AWS MQ RabbitMQ Broker Memory Exhaustion and Queue Stall

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an Amazon MQ for RabbitMQ broker that reaches its memory high watermark. RabbitMQ blocks publishers through flow control, queues stall, consumers back up and downstream order and notification processing halts. A recovery restart risks losing or duplicating messages.

Participants are working-level: messaging platform engineers, application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of broker memory alarms, queue depth, consumer prefetch, dead lettering, persistence and safe restarts.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Tarn Commerce uses a three-node Amazon MQ for RabbitMQ cluster deployment (mq.m5.large) to move events between its order service, inventory, payment, shipping and notification services. Most queues are classic queues with persistent messages. Consumers use unlimited prefetch and acknowledge after processing.

At 14:00 on Black Friday, a downstream shipping consumer starts failing because of a partner API timeout. Its queue grows from 2,000 to 4 million messages in 50 minutes. Memory usage on the broker rises past the high watermark at 14:55. RabbitMQ blocks all publishers on the connection, including order and payment publishers, causing checkout timeouts. An engineer restarts a broker node to clear memory, and the restart triggers queue synchronization that consumes more memory and disk I/O.

What the team does not yet know: the shipping queue has no maximum length or TTL, some consumers requeue failing messages immediately creating a hot loop, a lazy queue configuration was never applied, and a number of messages in flight were published without publisher confirms.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize broker flow control. Participants read memory, disk, queue depth and connection state metrics to identify blocked publishers.
  • Relieve memory without losing messages. The team purges, moves or dead-letters the right queue and avoids restarts that cause synchronization storms.
  • Isolate the failing consumer. Engineers stop the requeue loop and apply TTL or length limits for the failing queue.
  • Protect checkout and payments. Participants prioritize critical queues and publishers over shipping and notifications.
  • Reconcile messages. The team identifies lost or duplicated messages and reconciles orders.
  • Improve messaging design. Participants review queue limits, lazy queues, prefetch, confirms and alarms.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Name the queue, exchange or consumer in every proposal; do not purge any queue without stating what is lost.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Blocked publishers and checkout timeouts lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. The failing consumer is stopped and critical flows protected.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. The memory driver and queue state are identified.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A relief and reconciliation plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. The queue is drained or offloaded and the broker stabilizes.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Publishers unblock and consumers catch up.

T+06:00 — Validation. Order and shipping integrity are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Publishers blocked by memory alarm during peak sales

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 15:05. Checkout intermittently times out and orders are delayed. The RabbitMQ management console shows connections in 'blocked' state and a memory alarm in effect. The shipping queue holds 4.1 million messages.

The messaging engineer finds the failing consumer for shipping is retrying with immediate requeue, creating a hot loop. No maximum length or TTL is set for the queue. An engineer restarted a node at 14:58, which triggered queue sync that is consuming memory and I/O.

The AWS Health Dashboard shows no events. Some order messages may have been published without confirms. The sales peak continues for four more hours.

Facilitator provides: queue depth by queue, memory metrics, the consumer error log and the broker policy settings.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide to stop the failing consumer and pause shipping publishing.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Messaging Platform Engineering (Amazon MQ)
  • Explain flow control and memory alarm.
  • Plan relief of the shipping queue.
  • Avoid further restarts.
Application Engineering
  • Stop the failing consumer and requeue loop.
  • Plan idempotent replay.
  • Check partner API.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank queues by criticality.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Plan delayed shipping.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Confirm alarms.
  • List missing alarms.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about scaling.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform customer service.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan manual shipping.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about message loss risk.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What does a memory alarm do to publishers and why does it hit checkout?
  2. Why did the restart worsen the situation?
  3. How do you relieve the shipping queue without losing orders?
  4. How do you stop the hot loop?
  5. Which queues are critical and how do you protect them?
  6. How do you find lost or duplicated messages?
  7. Should you scale the broker now?
  8. What do customers and leadership need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal missing confirms until ENG reviews publisher settings.
  • If players purge, ask what is lost and who approved.
  • Look for a move or dead-letter approach.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes order and payment flows and decides on purging or offloading messages.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes purge, move or restart.
  • Decides on sale restrictions.

Messaging Platform Engineering (Amazon MQ)

  • Reads memory and disk alarms, queue depths and blocked connections.
  • Plans queue relief using shovel, dead-letter exchange or purge with approval.
  • Avoids restart-induced synchronization storms and plans broker scaling.
  • Applies queue policies for length and TTL.

Application Engineering

  • Stops the failing shipping consumer and fixes the requeue loop.
  • Plans replay of messages with idempotency.
  • Implements publisher confirms and prefetch limits.
  • Checks downstream partner API.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank queues by business criticality.
  • The service desk logs customer complaints.
  • Owners decide whether shipping can be delayed.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of memory, queue depth by queue, publish and ack rates and connection blocks.
  • Confirms alarms.
  • Checks AWS Health.
  • Adds queue depth alarms.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a case on memory alarms and node sync behavior.
  • Asks about scaling the broker and restart implications.
  • Requests guidance on policies.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for peak sales and informs customer service.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual shipping if needed.
  • Tracks the impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.6 capacity management; SOC 2 availability and processing integrity).
  • Assesses order accuracy and consumer commitments.
  • Captures evidence of decisions.
  • Logs findings on queue governance.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Bound every queue. Set maximum length, TTL and dead-letter exchanges, use lazy or quorum queues as appropriate and alarm on queue depth, memory and disk well before watermarks.

Protect critical flows with separate brokers or vhosts and limits so that one failing consumer cannot block checkout publishers.

Make consumers safe: bounded prefetch, delayed retry with backoff rather than immediate requeue, and idempotent handlers.

Make publishers reliable: publisher confirms, outbox patterns and reconciliation from the source of truth.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6) and SOC 2 expect capacity management evidence, monitoring and documented recovery.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on queue limits and protecting critical publishers.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

  1. How quickly did you link checkout timeouts to the broker?
  2. What caused the memory alarm?
  3. What did the restart do?
  4. How did you decide what to do with the shipping queue?
  5. How did you protect critical queues?
  6. How did you find lost or duplicated messages?
  7. How did you handle the partner outage?
  8. How were replays controlled?
  9. How accurate were updates to leadership and customers?
  10. What did AWS support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. What queue limits exist today?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alarms are missing?
  15. What would you change in messaging design?
