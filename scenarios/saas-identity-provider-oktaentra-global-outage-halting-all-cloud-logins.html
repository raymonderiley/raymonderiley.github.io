SaaS Identity Provider (Okta/Entra) Global Outage Halting All Cloud Logins

Multi-Cloud (AWS & Azure, No On-Prem)

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a global outage of the SaaS identity provider used for single sign-on to AWS, Azure and business applications, so no one can log in to consoles, workloads or SaaS tools, and break-glass access is needed to manage the response.

Participants are working-level: identity engineers, cloud platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and monitoring staff, the AWS and Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of identity dependency, break-glass accounts, cached sessions, token lifetimes and independent communication.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Halcyon Analytics uses a single SaaS identity provider for workforce sign-on to AWS through federation, Azure through Entra, and 150 SaaS tools. Admin consoles require SSO. Break-glass accounts exist in each cloud with passwords in a vault that also uses SSO. Service-to-service access uses machine identities not tied to the provider.

At 09:00 on Tuesday, the identity provider announces a global outage. New logins fail everywhere. Existing sessions expire over the next hours. Engineers cannot sign in to AWS or Azure consoles, incident chat and ticketing also use SSO, and customer support tools are down. A production alert fires on a database but engineers cannot reach the consoles.

What the team does not yet know: the outage duration, which break-glass credentials are accessible, some automation depends on tokens issued by the provider, and a phishing campaign claiming to be provider support has started to circulate.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize identity dependency. Participants map which systems depend on the identity provider.
  • Use break-glass access safely. The team uses emergency accounts with approvals and logging.
  • Keep communication alive. Engineers and managers use independent channels.
  • Protect against opportunistic attacks. Participants warn staff about phishing and fake support contacts.
  • Handle sessions and tokens. The team manages expiring sessions and automation.
  • Improve identity resilience. Participants review independence of break-glass, backup IdP and communication.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Every break-glass use must be approved, logged and reviewed, even in an outage.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. No logins and a production alert lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Break-glass is used for critical response and communication moves.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Dependencies and risks are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A continuity and security plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Actions proceed in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Operations stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Access and logs are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Provider recovery occurs and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: No one can sign in to anything

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:20. The identity provider is down globally. Engineers cannot sign in to AWS or Azure, incident chat or ticketing.

The identity engineer finds break-glass passwords sit in a vault that also uses SSO. A production database alert is firing.

Sessions expire over the next hours. A phishing campaign impersonates provider support.

Facilitator provides: provider status, dependency list, break-glass procedure and the database alert.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize break-glass use.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Identity Engineering
  • Map dependencies.
  • Retrieve credentials.
  • Plan fallback.
Cloud Platform Engineering (AWS & Azure)
  • Respond to database.
  • Validate machine identities.
  • Check sessions.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank systems.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Define approvals.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Watch phishing.
  • Review break-glass.
  • Preserve logs.
AWS & Microsoft Support Liaison
  • Open cases.
  • Ask about local accounts.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan channel switch.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about access duties.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What depends on the identity provider?
  2. How do you reach the break-glass credentials?
  3. How do you coordinate if chat and tickets are down?
  4. What do you do about the database alert?
  5. How do you handle phishing?
  6. What happens as sessions expire?
  7. What do staff and customers hear?
  8. Why was break-glass dependent on SSO?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the vault dependency until T1 tries to retrieve credentials.
  • If players share credentials in email, stop and discuss.
  • Look for independent channels.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes access to critical systems during an identity provider outage and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Identity Engineering

  • Maps dependencies on the provider.
  • Retrieves break-glass credentials via the offline process.
  • Plans fallback authentication.
  • Plans recovery sequence.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Platform Engineering (AWS & Azure)

  • Uses break-glass to respond to the database alert.
  • Validates machine identity paths.
  • Checks cached sessions.
  • Tracks all emergency actions.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank systems needing access.
  • The service desk logs access calls.
  • Owners define interim approvals.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Watches for phishing and credential attacks.
  • Reviews break-glass use.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Adds alerts for emergency logins.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

AWS & Microsoft Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with AWS and Microsoft on emergency access.
  • Asks about local account options.
  • Requests guidance on federation fallback.
  • Escalates through both TAMs and the provider.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for analytics operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.19 supplier relationships, 5.24-5.30, 8.2 privileged access; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses identity dependency and emergency access.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Keep break-glass credentials and incident communication independent of the primary identity provider.

Test break-glass regularly and monitor its use.

Warn staff about opportunistic phishing during known outages.

Understand token and session lifetimes for critical systems.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.19, 5.30, 8.2) and SOC 2 expect supplier risk and access control evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on controlled break-glass and independent communication.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you map dependencies?
  2. How did you retrieve break-glass credentials?
  3. How did you communicate?
  4. How did you handle the database alert?
  5. How did you handle phishing?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AWS & Microsoft Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
