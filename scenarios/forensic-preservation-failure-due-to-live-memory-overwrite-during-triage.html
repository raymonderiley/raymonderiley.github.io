Forensic Preservation Failure Due to Live Memory Overwrite During Triage

Ransomware & Cyber-Recovery Forensics

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines responders rebooting and running tools on infected servers during triage, overwriting live memory evidence needed to identify the attacker's tooling and persistence.

Participants are working-level: endpoint and server engineers, infrastructure operations engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and forensics analysts, the forensics and vendor support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of order of volatility, capture discipline, decision trade-offs between speed and evidence, and what to do after evidence is lost.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Larkfield Logistics runs warehouse management on 40 servers. Ransomware is detected on 12 of them. No written memory capture procedure exists, and the responders on shift are system administrators who have not been trained in forensics.

At 14:00 on Wednesday, administrators reboot eight infected servers to stop encryption and run antivirus cleanup tools on four others. Only two servers have a memory capture. The forensics analyst arrives at 16:00 and finds that most volatile evidence, including running processes, network connections and decryption keys in memory, is gone.

What the team does not yet know: how the attacker got in, whether persistence remains, whether encryption keys might have been recoverable, and what remaining evidence sources exist.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize the evidence loss. Participants understand what was lost and what remains.
  • Stop further damage. The team halts reboots and cleanup on unprocessed systems.
  • Preserve remaining evidence. Engineers capture memory and disk images from untouched systems.
  • Find alternative evidence. Participants use logs, network records and backups to reconstruct events.
  • Decide what to disclose. The team records the loss honestly for legal and insurers.
  • Improve triage discipline. Participants review procedures, training and capture tooling.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Stop reboots and cleanup on remaining systems and capture memory first, in order of volatility, before anything is changed.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Evidence loss leads to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Further changes stopped and capture begins.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Remaining sources are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A reconstruction plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Recovery and capture proceed in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Operations stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Findings are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes volatile evidence kept intact during triage and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Endpoint & Server Engineering

  • Halts reboots and cleanup tools on all remaining systems.
  • Provides capture tooling to responders.
  • Plans a written capture procedure.
  • Plans trained responders on shift.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Infrastructure Operations Engineering

  • Captures memory then disk images from remaining infected servers.
  • Protects backups and logs.
  • Validates with hashes.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and warehouse leads define which systems matter most.
  • The service desk logs reports.
  • Owners define manual procedures.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Reconstructs the intrusion from logs, firewall records and network flow.
  • Looks for persistence.
  • Preserves chain of custody.
  • Adds alerts for suspicious reboots during incidents.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with the EDR, hypervisor and forensics vendors.
  • Asks about key recovery options.
  • Requests help with capture.
  • Escalates through account teams.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for warehouse operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.28 collection of evidence, 5.26 response, 5.24 planning, 6.3 awareness and training; SOC 2 security and incident response).
  • Assesses forensic readiness and evidence handling.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Write a capture procedure that follows order of volatility and make it the first step of triage.

Pre-deploy memory capture tooling to servers and give responders access.

Train system administrators and rehearse triage with forensics.

Prefer network isolation over reboot as the default containment.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.28, 5.26, 5.24, 6.3) and SOC 2 expect evidence handling and training records.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on stop-and-capture discipline.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. What did you stop first?
  2. What order did you capture in?
  3. What evidence remained?
  4. How did you reconstruct?
  5. What did you tell insurers?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
