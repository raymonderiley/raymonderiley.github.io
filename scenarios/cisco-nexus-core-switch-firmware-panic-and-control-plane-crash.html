Cisco Nexus Core Switch Firmware Panic and Control Plane Crash

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a firmware defect that causes both Cisco Nexus core switches in a vPC pair to panic and reload their control plane within minutes of each other. Because both peers run the same software release, the redundancy intended to protect the data center core fails simultaneously, and the fabric enters reload loops.

Participants are working-level: data center network engineers, the network operations center, server, storage and virtualization operators, application owners, the service desk, the vendor and TAC liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The scenario explores console access, rollback options, vPC consistency and the dependency of storage and clusters on the network core.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Highland Logistics runs a data center core on two Nexus 9000 series switches in a vPC pair (CORE-A and CORE-B) running NX-OS 9.3(8), upgraded eight days ago to resolve a minor vulnerability. The core provides Layer 3 routing, FHRP gateways for server VLANs and the uplinks to firewalls, WAN edge and storage network ethernet segments (iSCSI and NFS). Management is via an out-of-band network whose own gateway is a small separate switch.

At 13:42 on Wednesday, a BGP route flap from a service provider triggers a defect in the routing process that leaks memory. At 13:49 CORE-A's routing daemon crashes, causing a supervisor panic and reload. The vPC peer link is lost, and CORE-B takes over. At 13:57, CORE-B hits the same condition and also panics. Both switches begin reload loops: they boot, re-converge, the BGP table fills and they crash again every 8 to 10 minutes. Servers lose gateways, iSCSI paths flap and clusters begin failing over.

What the team does not yet know: the defect is documented in a bug fix released in 9.3(10), the previous release image is on the bootflash but its signature check fails, the configuration backup in the network management system is 3 weeks old, console server access works but the console passwords are in a vault that requires network access, and the provider's BGP flap is still ongoing.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stabilize the network core. Participants identify the reload loop, stop the trigger (BGP session shutdown), and work to bring the core up in a minimal state to restore Layer 3 and gateway functions.
  • Use out-of-band access effectively. The team uses console servers, OOB management and local credentials, and works through the circular dependency of credential vaults.
  • Make a software decision under pressure. Participants decide between rolling back to the previous image, upgrading to the fixed release, or running with a workaround, considering vPC consistency checks, ISSU/ASU limitations and risk.
  • Protect storage and clusters. Server and storage teams manage flapping iSCSI and NFS paths, prevent datastore all-paths-down conditions and avoid cluster fencing storms.
  • Restore routing and external connectivity safely. The team re-establishes BGP, firewall and WAN edge connectivity in a controlled way to avoid re-triggering the defect.
  • Improve change and diversity practices. The team examines why both switches ran the same new release, testing and rollback planning.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Use your actual platform commands and procedures where known; if the team does not know the rollback procedure, record it as a gap.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Network and application alerts lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Trigger and containment. The BGP trigger is isolated and the reload loops are understood.

T+01:15 — Console access and state. Switches are accessed out of band and their state is assessed.

T+02:00 — Software decision. Rollback, upgrade or workaround is chosen.

T+03:00 — Core restoration. One core is brought up stable, then the second.

T+04:30 — Dependency recovery. Storage, clusters and applications are stabilized.

T+06:00 — Validation. Routing, vPC consistency, performance and monitoring are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Services restored and incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Both core switches panic and enter reload loops

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 14:00. The NOC dashboard shows CORE-A and CORE-B in alternating up and down states with gaps of about 8 minutes. Syslog for CORE-B shows a routing process crash followed by a supervisor panic and reload. Storage path monitoring shows iSCSI sessions flapping, and the virtualization cluster reports hosts losing datastores.

The BGP session with the primary provider flapped at 13:42 and is flapping again every few minutes. The network engineer on call sees a similar pattern in the CORE-A logs from 13:49. Over the console server, the engineer sees the switch boot, converge routes and crash again as the BGP table fills.

Applications are returning errors; the order management system and warehouse system are down, and customers are calling. The credentials for the console server are stored in the privileged access tool, which is hosted in the virtual environment affected by the network failure.

Facilitator provides: the syslog excerpt, the BGP flap timeline, the console access path and the software upgrade change record.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name leads for network, storage and applications.
  • Decide to isolate the external BGP trigger now.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Data Center Network Engineering
  • Explain what the logs imply and how to stop the reload loop.
  • Plan to shut the BGP session and bring up one core with a minimal configuration.
  • List the software options.
Network Operations Center (NOC)
  • Confirm console and OOB access and retrieve credentials from alternate sources.
  • Retrieve config backups and compare with running state.
  • Maintain the status board.
Server, Storage & Virtualization Operations
  • Prevent HA storms and protect datastores.
  • Check which paths remain via alternative storage fabrics.
  • Prepare a staged restart plan.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • List affected services and rank them.
  • Service desk: issue holding message.
  • Owners: manual dispatch.
Vendor & TAC Liaison
  • Open a priority-one case and send logs and core files.
  • Ask if the crash matches a known bug and the fix.
  • Request the fixed image.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Activate logistics continuity plans.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask how the upgrade was tested and whether rollback was planned.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What is the quickest way to stop the reload loop, and what do you sacrifice?
  2. How do you reach the consoles when the credential vault depends on the network you are trying to fix?
  3. Which software option do you choose: roll back, upgrade or workaround, and what are the risks of each?
  4. What does vPC consistency checking mean when you bring up one switch alone?
  5. How do you protect datastores and clusters from flapping network paths?
  6. When is it safe to re-enable the provider BGP session?
  7. Why did both switches run the same new release, and what did your change process say?
  8. What do you tell depots and customers?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the vault dependency until the NOC asks for console credentials.
  • If the team rolls back immediately, ask about the failing image signature and how they would verify the image.
  • Note whether the team keeps the OOB network independent.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, sets priorities (storage reachability, gateways, external connectivity) and approves the software decision.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Approves emergency vendor engagement and any risky change such as an unsigned image boot.
  • Decides when to resume BGP with the provider.

Data Center Network Engineering

  • Diagnoses the crash logs, core files and the BGP trigger on CORE-A and CORE-B via console.
  • Shuts down the provider BGP sessions to stop the trigger and brings one core up in minimal configuration.
  • Evaluates software options, vPC consistency and the risk of reloading with the older configuration.
  • Prepares the staged restoration of routing, FHRP and uplinks.

Network Operations Center (NOC)

  • Confirms the extent of the outage from monitoring, SNMP and syslog, and keeps a running status board.
  • Maintains OOB access and checks the console server.
  • Retrieves the configuration backups and compares them to known state.
  • Coordinates field technicians for physical access.

Server, Storage & Virtualization Operations

  • Server and virtualization teams identify hosts that lost gateways and iSCSI/NFS paths and prevent HA fencing storms by adjusting settings.
  • Storage team monitors datastores for all-paths-down and protects data consistency.
  • Plans to stage the recovery of cluster resources after the network returns.
  • Checks that the storage fabric alternatives, such as FC, are unaffected.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners list impacted services and rank restoration.
  • The service desk handles tickets and guidance.
  • Owners identify manual workarounds and the tolerance for downtime.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Vendor & TAC Liaison

  • Opens a priority-one TAC case with core files and logs, and requests bug confirmation and a recommended image.
  • Asks about workarounds such as limiting BGP prefixes or disabling a feature.
  • Obtains the fixed release image and verifies checksums.
  • Records every vendor instruction.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity plans for logistics operations and customer service.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership and depots.
  • Coordinates manual dispatch and paper processes.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.9 configuration management, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates contractual obligations to customers.
  • Captures evidence of the change record for the upgrade eight days ago.
  • Logs findings on software diversity, backup currency and credential vault dependency.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Redundancy of identical components is not diversity. Stagger software releases between core peers where feasible (within vendor support rules), and require a soak period on a non-critical pair before deploying to the core.

Plan rollback before upgrading: verified images in secure storage, signature checks tested, and documented rollback steps with timing. Know how to boot an image without a working network.

Protect out-of-band access: keep the OOB network and console servers independent of the production core, store credentials in a place reachable offline and test access quarterly.

Back up configurations after every change and verify them by diffing against running state; keep a known-good minimal configuration to bring up a core quickly.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.9, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect change management with testing and rollback, vendor vulnerability management and evidence of resilience tests.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on how the team stopped the reload loop and accessed the switches out of band.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. What was the time between the first symptom and stopping the trigger?
  2. How did you gain console access and was the vault dependency known?
  3. Which software option did you choose and what drove the decision?
  4. How did you validate the image before booting?
  5. How current were the configuration backups, and what was missing?
  6. How did vPC behave when only one switch was up?
  7. How did server and storage teams protect datastores and clusters?
  8. When did you re-enable BGP and what safeguards applied?
  9. What did the change record for the upgrade say about testing and rollback?
  10. Which other sites are affected by the same defect?
  11. How accurate were communications to depots and customers?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. What monitoring should have warned about memory growth?
  14. What access or credentials were missing?
  15. What would make your core genuinely diverse?
