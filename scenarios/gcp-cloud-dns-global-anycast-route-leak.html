GCP Cloud DNS Global Anycast Route Leak

Google Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Cloud DNS incident in which a routing leak and a misapplied private zone policy cause some resolvers to receive wrong or no answers, so customer traffic is partly misdirected while internal services fail to resolve each other.

Participants are working-level: network and DNS engineers, platform and SRE engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and monitoring staff, the Google Cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of public and private zones, DNS peering, forwarding, TTLs, caching and DNSSEC.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Harlow Insurance hosts customer portals and APIs on Google Cloud with public Cloud DNS zones for its domain and private zones for internal names across a hub-and-spoke VPC design with DNS peering. TTLs on key records are 3600 seconds. DNSSEC is enabled on the public zone.

At 09:10 on Friday, a change adds a private zone with the same domain name to the hub network and sets a server policy forwarding all queries to an on-premises resolver that is overloaded. Internal clients start receiving NXDOMAIN or stale answers for service names. Separately, external monitors report some regions resolving the portal to an old address after a record change made earlier with a long TTL. Claim submissions drop by 40 percent.

What the team does not yet know: the private zone shadows public records for internal clients, the forwarding policy sends public lookups through the overloaded resolver, an unrelated registrar-side DS record mismatch may break validation for some resolvers, and lowering TTLs now will not help already cached answers.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize DNS as the cause. Participants use query tests from different vantage points and logs to confirm that resolution is the issue.
  • Separate internal and external problems. The team distinguishes private zone shadowing, forwarding overload and public cache staleness.
  • Fix safely. Engineers remove the conflicting zone and policy in small, verified steps.
  • Manage caches. Participants understand TTL limits and plan communications for stale answers.
  • Protect security. The team checks for hijack indicators and DNSSEC consistency.
  • Improve DNS governance. Participants review zone design, change control, TTL strategy and monitoring.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Verify every DNS change from at least three vantage points before declaring success.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Resolution failures and drops in submissions lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Critical records are tested and the conflicting change is identified.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Zones, forwarding and cache effects are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A fix and cache plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Changes are applied in small verified steps.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Resolution stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Services and security are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: Names fail to resolve and portals go stale

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:40. Internal services cannot resolve each other and claims submissions are down 40 percent. External monitors show some regions with the old portal address.

The DNS engineer finds a private zone with the public domain name was created in the hub network at 09:05 and a server policy forwarding all queries to an overloaded on-premises resolver.

The old address comes from a long-TTL record. A DS record mismatch is suspected at the registrar.

Facilitator provides: zone list, policy config, query test results and TTL table.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide who owns DNS changes.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Network & DNS Engineering (Cloud DNS)
  • Test from vantage points.
  • Identify the conflicting zone.
  • Plan removal.
Platform & SRE Engineering
  • Check dependencies.
  • Use static workarounds.
  • Validate.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank services.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Contact partners.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Check for hijack.
  • Review logs.
  • Preserve logs.
Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about propagation.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan customer notice.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about obligations.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you prove DNS is the problem and where?
  2. Why did a private zone break internal resolution?
  3. What does a forwarding policy to an overloaded resolver do?
  4. What can you do about cached long-TTL answers?
  5. How do you rule out a hijack?
  6. How do you verify fixes?
  7. What do customers hear?
  8. Why was the change not reviewed?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the DS mismatch until OPS checks validation.
  • If players change many things at once, ask how they will verify.
  • Look for single-change discipline.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes name resolution for customer-facing and internal services and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Network & DNS Engineering (Cloud DNS)

  • Tests resolution from internal, external and partner vantage points.
  • Identifies the conflicting private zone and server policy.
  • Plans removal and verification.
  • Plans resolver capacity changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Platform & SRE Engineering

  • Checks application dependencies on internal names.
  • Uses temporary hosts entries or static endpoints for critical flows.
  • Validates services after each change.
  • Tracks temporary workarounds.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank services and decide on workarounds.
  • The service desk logs reports by region.
  • Owners coordinate partner contacts.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Checks for hijack indicators and DNSSEC validation failures.
  • Reviews query logs.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Adds DNS anomaly detections.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on zone behavior and policies.
  • Asks about propagation and resolver behavior.
  • Requests guidance on DNSSEC.
  • Escalates through the TAM.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for claims and customer portals and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.20 network security, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses service availability for claims.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Treat DNS as critical infrastructure: peer-reviewed changes, policy guardrails against duplicate zone names and staged rollouts.

Choose TTLs deliberately and shorten them ahead of planned changes.

Monitor resolution from multiple external and internal vantage points.

Keep resolver capacity and forwarding designs tested.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.20, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect change control and monitoring evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on DNS change discipline and multi-vantage verification.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you prove DNS was the problem?
  2. How did you separate the causes?
  3. How did you remove the zone safely?
  4. How did you handle forwarder overload?
  5. How did you handle caches?
  6. How did you rule out hijack?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
