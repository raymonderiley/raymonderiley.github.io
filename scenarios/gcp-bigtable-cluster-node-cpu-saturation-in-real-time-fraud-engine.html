GCP Bigtable Cluster Node CPU Saturation in Real-Time Fraud Engine

Google Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Cloud Bigtable cluster serving a real-time fraud engine that hits CPU saturation on hot nodes after a key design flaw and a campaign spike, causing feature lookups to time out so the fraud engine either fails open and misses fraud or fails closed and declines good payments.

Participants are working-level: data platform engineers, fraud engine engineers, fraud operations staff, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Google Cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of row key hotspots, node scaling, replication, app profiles and fail-open versus fail-closed decisions.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Pinecrest Bank scores every card authorization with a fraud engine that reads customer features from a Bigtable cluster of 6 nodes with a replica cluster in a second region. Row keys begin with a timestamp-based prefix. Authorization must be scored in under 80 ms.

At 12:00 on Thursday, a retail promotion triples authorization volume. A subset of rows with recent timestamps becomes hot and three nodes reach 98 percent CPU. Read latency rises from 6 ms to 400 ms, the fraud engine times out and falls back to fail-open for 30 percent of transactions. Fraud analysts see a rise in suspicious approvals while customers see declines when the fallback flips to fail-closed.

What the team does not yet know: adding nodes will take 20 minutes to rebalance and may not fix hotspots, an app profile routing to the replica could offload reads but replica data lags by seconds, an attacker may be exploiting the fail-open window, and changing the row key requires a migration.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a hotspot. Participants read node CPU, hot tablets and key visualizer data to see that load is uneven, not only high.
  • Decide fail-open versus fail-closed. The team sets an explicit, risk-based fallback and monitors fraud and decline rates.
  • Add capacity safely. Engineers scale nodes, use replica routing and manage rebalancing delays.
  • Detect exploitation. Fraud operations looks for attack patterns in the fail-open window.
  • Communicate with merchants and customers. The team provides accurate status on declines.
  • Improve design. Participants review key design, capacity planning and fallback policy.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Every fraud fallback decision must state the expected fraud loss and the expected decline rate.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Latency and mixed fraud signals lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. A fallback policy is chosen and capacity is added.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Hotspots and load drivers are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A scaling and key-design plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Scaling and routing changes are executed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Latency and fraud metrics stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Fraud losses and declines are assessed.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: Fraud lookups time out at peak

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 12:20. Fraud engine timeouts spike. Thirty percent of authorizations use the fail-open fallback and some customers are declined. Bigtable shows three hot nodes at 98 percent CPU.

The data engineer finds timestamp-prefixed row keys concentrating load. Adding nodes needs about 20 minutes to rebalance.

Fraud operations sees more suspicious approvals than usual. The replica lags by seconds.

Facilitator provides: node metrics, key visualizer, fallback settings and fraud dashboard.

  • Decide on the fallback policy.

  • Plan key migration.

  • Route reads.
  • Validate.
Fraud Operations & Service Desk

  • Prepare manual review.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Watch status.
  • Preserve logs.
Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about rebalancing.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan merchant contact.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell a hotspot from general load?
  2. Fail open or fail closed, and for which transactions?
  3. What do you expect in fraud losses and declines?
  4. How does replica lag affect scoring?
  5. Would adding nodes fix the hotspot?
  6. How do you look for exploitation?
  7. What do merchants hear?
  8. Why was the row key designed this way?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the attack until APP reviews suspicious approvals.
  • If players only add nodes, ask about hotspots.
  • Look for explicit fallback numbers.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Design row keys to spread load, test with realistic peak traffic and watch key visualizer before events.

Define fallback behavior in advance with fraud and customer-impact numbers, not as a code default.

Use replica routing deliberately with awareness of lag.

Alert on node CPU skew, not only averages.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.16) and SOC 2 expect capacity, monitoring and fraud control evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on fallback policy and hotspot design.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you identify a hotspot?
  2. How did you choose the fallback?
  3. How did you use the replica?
  4. How did you detect the attack?
  5. What did scaling achieve?
  6. How were merchants handled?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
