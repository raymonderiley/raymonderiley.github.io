Azure Policy Enforcement Blocking Emergency Infrastructure Patch

Azure Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an Azure Policy deny assignment that blocks an emergency infrastructure patch for an actively exploited vulnerability, forcing the team to balance speed against governance, handle exemptions properly and avoid weakening controls while production remains exposed.

Participants are working-level: cloud governance and policy engineers, cloud platform and SRE engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and monitoring staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of policy effects, initiatives, exemptions, remediation tasks, management group scope, emergency change procedures and audit evidence.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Vantage Logistics enforces Azure Policy at the management group level with a deny effect on public IPs, non-approved VM images, missing tags and unapproved SKUs. A critical vulnerability in a widely used reverse proxy appliance image is being actively exploited, with a vendor patch released at 06:00 Tuesday. Patching requires deploying a new image version from the vendor marketplace and attaching a temporary public IP for validation.

At 08:00, the platform team's deployment fails with policy denials: the marketplace image is not on the approved list, the temporary public IP is denied and the new SKU is not allowed. The security team observes exploit attempts against the internet-facing proxies in six regions. Governance owns the policy repository and the approver is traveling. Emergency change procedures require two approvals, one of whom is the unreachable governance lead.

What the team does not yet know: an exemption could be created but would expire only if configured, some engineers have Owner rights and can modify assignments directly, a copy of the policy to a test scope would not reflect production behavior, and modifying the initiative could affect 40 other subscriptions.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a governance block during an emergency. Participants read policy denial messages and compliance data to understand what is blocking the patch and why.
  • Use the emergency path properly. The team applies a scoped, time-limited exemption with approvals rather than disabling policy.
  • Reduce exposure meanwhile. Engineers apply compensating controls such as WAF rules, network restrictions or temporary disablement of risky features.
  • Prevent control erosion. Participants avoid broad policy changes and ensure exemptions are recorded, expire and are reviewed.
  • Handle approval gaps. The team uses delegation and break-glass governance when an approver is unavailable.
  • Improve policy resilience. Participants review emergency exemption design, delegation, pre-approved images and testing of policies.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Every policy exemption must have an owner, scope, reason, expiry and compensating control.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Denied deployments and active exploitation lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Compensating controls reduce exposure and the exemption path is chosen.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Policy blocks and scope are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. An exemption and patch plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Scoped exemptions are applied and patches deployed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Exposure ends and compliance is verified.

T+06:00 — Validation. Exemptions are expired or reviewed and evidence is collected.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: Policy denies the emergency patch while exploits continue

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:30. The patched proxy image deployment fails with three policy denials: unapproved image, public IP not allowed and SKU not allowed. Security sees active exploit attempts on the vulnerable proxies.

The platform engineer proposes an exemption. The governance lead who must co-approve is traveling and unreachable. Some engineers hold Owner rights and could change assignments directly.

Modifying the initiative would affect 40 other subscriptions. The vendor says exploitation is widespread.

Facilitator provides: denial messages, policy definitions, approver list, the emergency change procedure and exploit telemetry.

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide on delegation of approval.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Governance & Policy Engineering (Azure Policy)
  • Design a scoped time-limited exemption.
  • Check other subscriptions.
  • Plan automatic expiry.
Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering
  • Apply compensating controls.
  • Prepare image deployment.
  • Validate.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank services.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Coordinate vendors.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Monitor exploits.
  • Hunt for compromise.
  • Preserve logs.
Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about propagation.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Coordinate vendors.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about the approval record.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What is the safest way to get the patch through without weakening policy?
  2. Who can approve when the governance lead is unreachable?
  3. What compensating controls reduce exposure now?
  4. Why is changing the initiative a bad idea?
  5. What must an exemption record contain?
  6. How do you stop engineers from using Owner rights to bypass policy?
  7. How do you handle a temporary public IP safely?
  8. What does leadership need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the cross-subscription effect until T1 inspects the assignment scope.
  • If players disable the policy, ask about all subscriptions.
  • Look for scoped exemptions with expiry.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes stopping exploitation and sets who can approve exemptions.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency exemptions and delegation.
  • Decides on the risk acceptance.

Cloud Governance & Policy Engineering (Azure Policy)

  • Reads policy denial details and compliance results.
  • Designs a narrowly scoped, time-limited exemption.
  • Prepares to remove it automatically.
  • Checks effects on other subscriptions.

Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering

  • Prepares the patched image deployment and rollback.
  • Applies compensating controls such as WAF rules and rate limits.
  • Validates the fix.
  • Tracks temporary resources.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank affected services and approve brief maintenance.
  • The service desk logs impacts.
  • Owners coordinate with vendors.
  • Prepare validation tests.

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.8 technical vulnerability management, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 security and change management).
  • Assesses the exemption record.
  • Captures evidence of approvals.
  • Logs findings on emergency change governance.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Design governance for emergencies: a pre-approved, scoped, time-boxed exemption path with automatic expiry, current delegates and clear approval rules that work when key people are unreachable.

Pre-stage critical patches: approved images and repositories for security tooling and emergency pipelines so the common case does not require exceptions.

Limit Owner rights and monitor policy assignment changes in real time.

Test policy changes in a representative scope before wide enforcement.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.8, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect vulnerability management timelines, change records and exception evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on emergency exemption design and delegation.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

  2. How did you reduce exposure meanwhile?
  3. How did you handle the missing approver?

  5. How did you record the exemption?
  6. How did you handle the bypass attempt?
  7. How did you handle the image allow-list?
  8. How did you handle signs of compromise?
  9. How accurate were updates to the CEO and CISO?
  10. What did Microsoft support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How are emergency exemptions designed?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alerts are missing?
  15. What would you change in governance?
