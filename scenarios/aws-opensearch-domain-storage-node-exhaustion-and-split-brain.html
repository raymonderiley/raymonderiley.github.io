AWS OpenSearch Domain Storage Node Exhaustion and Split-Brain

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an OpenSearch domain that runs out of storage on data nodes, hits the flood-stage watermark, turns indices read-only and then suffers cluster instability in which node loss and master election problems leave parts of the cluster in an inconsistent state. Search and logging pipelines fail and recent data is at risk.

Participants are working-level: search and data platform engineers, application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of shard allocation, watermarks, index lifecycle, dedicated master nodes and snapshot recovery.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Fairmont Telecom uses an Amazon OpenSearch Service domain with six data nodes (no dedicated master nodes) across two Availability Zones for customer-facing search and for security and application log analytics. Index State Management policies delete indices older than 30 days, but a recent change to the log format created daily indices ten times larger than before. Snapshots are taken hourly to S3.

At 02:00 on Sunday, free storage on three data nodes drops below the flood-stage watermark of 95 percent. OpenSearch applies the read-only-allow-delete block to affected indices. Log ingestion starts failing with 429 and 403 'cluster_block_exception'. To free space, an engineer deletes several indices and restarts a node, but the restart overlaps with another node being blue/green replaced by the service, and master election becomes unstable. The cluster status flips between red and yellow, and a network partition during the churn leaves two nodes believing different nodes are master.

What the team does not yet know: the domain has no dedicated masters, so master-eligible data nodes are under heavy load, some indices have no replica, the most recent two hours of logs exist only in primaries on the stressed nodes, and the last successful snapshot is 70 minutes old.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize storage exhaustion and cluster blocks. Participants read FreeStorageSpace, ClusterStatus, cluster block messages and shard allocation explain output.
  • Free space safely. The team deletes or moves data using lifecycle policy, avoids deleting the wrong indices and avoids restarts that worsen instability.
  • Stabilize cluster state. Engineers address master election, shard allocation, replicas and the effect of blue/green changes.
  • Protect incoming data. Participants buffer or divert logs and search traffic while the cluster is blocked.
  • Decide on recovery from snapshot. The team weighs restoring from snapshot against waiting for shard recovery and accepts defined data loss.
  • Improve capacity and architecture. Participants review dedicated master nodes, storage alarms, ISM and shard sizing.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Never propose deleting indices without naming them and confirming the retention and snapshot status.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Ingestion failures and red cluster status lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Writes are buffered and further changes are frozen.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Storage and master election causes are identified.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A free-space and recovery plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Space is freed, cluster stabilized and shards recovered.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Ingestion resumes and indices are checked.

T+06:00 — Validation. Data completeness is verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Cluster blocks writes and status flips between red and yellow

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 02:40. Log ingestion fails with 'cluster_block_exception: index read-only / allow delete'. Customer search latency is high and some results are missing. The domain status alternates between red and yellow.

The data platform engineer sees FreeStorageSpace under 5 percent on three nodes. An engineer deleted several indices at 02:20 and restarted a node, overlapping a service-initiated blue/green change. Two nodes report different masters.

Hourly snapshots exist, with the last success 70 minutes ago. The AWS Health Dashboard shows a service-initiated configuration change in progress. Security operations asks whether logs are being lost.

Facilitator provides: the cluster health output, storage by node, index list and sizes, and the snapshot list.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Freeze further manual restarts and deletions.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Search & Data Platform Engineering (OpenSearch)
  • Explain the flood-stage block and the master issue.
  • Identify indices to delete or move.
  • Plan stabilization.
Application Engineering
  • Divert ingestion to a buffer.
  • Plan replay.
  • Slow noisy sources.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Confirm customer search priorities.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Plan degraded search.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Confirm alarms.
  • List missing alarms.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about the blue/green change.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform security operations.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about log retention obligations.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What does the flood-stage watermark do and how do you lift the block?
  2. Why did restarting a node make things worse?
  3. How do you free space without deleting the wrong data?
  4. How do you stop losing logs right now?
  5. What is the risk with no dedicated master nodes?
  6. Do you restore from snapshot or recover shards?
  7. What do security operations need to hear?
  8. Who authorizes deleting indices?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the missing replicas until OSE reviews shard allocation.
  • If players delete indices quickly, ask which and whether snapshots exist.
  • Look for freeze and buffer as first moves.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes customer search and security log retention and decides on acceptable log loss.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes deletion of named indices and snapshot restore.
  • Decides on communications to the security team.

Search & Data Platform Engineering (OpenSearch)

  • Reads cluster health, allocation explain, node storage and master status.
  • Plans safe deletion, volume or node scaling and lifecycle changes.
  • Plans master election stabilization and enabling dedicated masters later.
  • Prepares snapshot restore.

Application Engineering

  • Diverts log ingestion to a buffer such as Kinesis or S3 and slows noisy sources.
  • Plans replay of buffered logs.
  • Reduces index size or retention in the pipeline.
  • Checks search application behavior.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners confirm which search features are customer facing and which logs are security critical.
  • The service desk logs complaints.
  • Owners decide on degraded search.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of storage by node, cluster status, indexing errors and pending tasks.
  • Confirms alarms.
  • Checks AWS Health and service events.
  • Adds storage alarms.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a case on cluster instability and blue/green overlap.
  • Asks about safe node replacement and recovery.
  • Requests guidance on shard allocation.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for customer search and informs the security operations team about log gaps.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates customer messages.
  • Tracks impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.15 logging, 8.6 capacity management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses log retention obligations.
  • Captures evidence of decisions.
  • Logs findings on capacity and logging gaps.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Plan storage with headroom and alarms. Alarm on FreeStorageSpace per node well before watermarks and size shards and indices appropriately with working ISM policies.

Use dedicated master nodes across three AZs for production domains so cluster state does not compete with data load, and keep replicas for important indices.

Make recovery routine: tested snapshot restores, buffered ingestion and runbooks that forbid risky concurrent changes during service-initiated updates.

Control log volume: format changes should be reviewed for volume impact.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.15) and SOC 2 expect capacity management evidence, retention controls and tested restores.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on safe space recovery and avoiding concurrent changes.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you identify the flood-stage block?
  2. What did the restart do to cluster stability?
  3. How did you decide what to delete?
  4. How did you protect incoming logs?
  5. What was lost and who accepted it?
  6. How did you decide between restore and recovery?
  7. How did you handle the service blue/green change?
  8. How was replay controlled?
  9. How accurate were updates to security operations?
  10. What did AWS support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How are indices sized and retained?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alarms are missing?
  15. What would you change in domain design?
