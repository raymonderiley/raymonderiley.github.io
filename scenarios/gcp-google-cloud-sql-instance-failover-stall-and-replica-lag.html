GCP Google Cloud SQL Instance Failover Stall and Replica Lag

Google Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Google Cloud SQL for PostgreSQL high-availability instance whose automatic failover stalls after a zonal storage problem, leaving the primary unavailable, the standby not promoted and cross-region read replicas lagging by many minutes.

Participants are working-level: database platform engineers, application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Google Cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of HA failover, replication lag, connection handling, point-in-time recovery and promotion trade-offs.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Larkspur Credit runs its account ledger on a Cloud SQL for PostgreSQL HA instance in us-central1 with a regional standby and two cross-region read replicas used for reporting. Applications connect through a connection pool using the instance private IP. Automated backups and point-in-time recovery are enabled.

At 10:05 on Monday, the primary becomes unresponsive after a zonal persistent disk degradation. Automatic failover starts but does not complete in 10 minutes. Applications return 500 errors, connection pools fill with dead connections and the reporting replicas show 14 minutes of lag. The Google Cloud status page lists a zonal storage incident.

What the team does not yet know: the standby may be missing the last 40 seconds of writes, connection pools do not recover on their own, a manual promotion of a cross-region replica would lose up to 14 minutes of data and change the endpoint, and batch jobs scheduled at 10:30 will add load as soon as the instance returns.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a stalled failover. Participants read instance operations, logs and metrics to confirm failover is not progressing and is not an application fault.
  • Protect data integrity. The team estimates data loss for each option before any promotion and avoids split-brain writes.
  • Decide on failover versus waiting. Engineers weigh waiting for automatic failover, forcing it and promoting a replica, using explicit data loss and downtime numbers.
  • Recover applications cleanly. Participants reset connection pools, control retry storms and pause batch jobs.
  • Communicate accurately. The team provides status to customers and leadership without overstating recovery.
  • Improve database resilience. Participants review failover testing, pooling, replica roles and alerting.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • State the data loss and downtime estimate for every database option before it is chosen.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Application errors and a stalled failover lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Writes are paused and the cause is confirmed.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Options and data loss are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A failover or promotion decision is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. The chosen option is executed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Connections and load stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Data is validated and reconciled.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Primary down and failover stalled

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 10:20. The ledger application returns 500 errors. The Cloud SQL operations list shows a failover in progress for 15 minutes. Connection pools are full of dead connections.

The database engineer finds the standby's replication position 40 seconds behind the last known primary write. Cross-region replicas lag by 14 minutes. The status page shows a zonal storage incident.

Batch jobs start at 10:30. Promoting a replica would change the endpoint and lose recent data.

Facilitator provides: operation log, replication metrics, status page text and the connection configuration.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide whether to pause writes.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Database Platform Engineering (Cloud SQL)
  • Estimate data loss per option.
  • Plan forced failover.
  • Plan point-in-time recovery.
Application Engineering
  • Reset pools and limit retries.
  • Pause batch jobs.
  • Check split-brain risk.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank transactions.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Prepare manual steps.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Watch the status page.
  • Preserve logs.
Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about failover time.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan workarounds.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about data loss reporting.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell a stalled failover from a slow one?
  2. What data loss do the standby and the replica each imply?
  3. What would you wait for before forcing a promotion?
  4. How do you avoid split-brain writes?
  5. What happens to connection pools when the primary returns?
  6. Who accepts data loss and how is it recorded?
  7. What should customers be told?
  8. Why do reporting replicas sit in another region?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the 40-second gap until T1 checks replication position.
  • If players promote a replica immediately, ask about data loss.
  • Look for explicit numbers.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes order and account transactions while protecting data integrity and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Database Platform Engineering (Cloud SQL)

  • Reads instance operations, logs, replication position and the status page.
  • Estimates data loss for standby and replica options.
  • Plans forced failover or promotion with endpoint changes.
  • Plans point-in-time recovery as a fallback.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Engineering

  • Resets connection pools and limits retries.
  • Pauses batch jobs and queues writes where possible.
  • Checks for split-brain risk before reconnecting.
  • Validates transactions after recovery.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners decide which transactions can pause.
  • The service desk logs customer contacts.
  • Owners prepare manual procedures.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of instance state, lag and error rates.
  • Monitors the Google Cloud status page.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Adds lag and failover alerts.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on the stalled failover.
  • Asks about expected failover duration and standby state.
  • Requests guidance on forced failover.
  • Escalates through the TAM.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for customer-facing account services and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.13 backup, 8.14 redundancy; SOC 2 availability and processing integrity).
  • Assesses transaction availability and data integrity during the outage.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Test failover on a schedule and measure real recovery time, including application reconnection, not only the database operation.

Configure connection pools to detect and drop dead connections and to retry with backoff and jitter.

Define in advance who may force a failover or promote a replica, with data loss numbers recorded.

Alert on replication lag and failover duration.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.13, 8.14) and SOC 2 expect failover test evidence and decision records.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on failover testing and connection recovery.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How long did it take to see the failover had stalled?
  2. How did you estimate data loss?
  3. How did you decide between waiting, forcing and promoting?
  4. How did you handle connection pools?
  5. How were missing writes reconciled?
  6. How was load after recovery controlled?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
