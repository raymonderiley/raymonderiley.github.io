Hardware Security Module (HSM) Cryptographic Key Store Corruption

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines the corruption of the key store on a hardware security module cluster that protects payment encryption keys, database transparent data encryption master keys and certificate authority private keys. Because keys never leave the HSM in clear form, a corrupted store can render encrypted data unreadable and stop services that depend on cryptographic operations, even though the data itself is intact.

Participants are working-level: HSM and PKI engineers, application and payment owners, security operations and key custodians, server and backup operators, the vendor liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The scenario places particular weight on key ceremony procedures, quorum of key custodians and the availability of key backups.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Silverline Payments runs a two-node HSM cluster (HSM-A in the primary data center, HSM-B in the secondary) that holds the payment zone master keys, PIN verification keys, the TDE master keys for 14 databases and the private keys of an internal issuing CA. The cluster uses synchronized key replication with an M-of-N (3 of 5) smart card quorum for administrative operations. Key backups are written to encrypted backup tokens held in a safe at each site and refreshed quarterly.

At 02:15, during a firmware update to HSM-A, the update process fails midway and the device reboots into a recovery state reporting 'key store integrity check failed'. Replication then pushes the corrupted partition state to HSM-B before the replication was suspended at 02:31. By 02:40, both HSMs refuse key operations. Payment authorization requests return cryptographic errors, database instances using TDE cannot open encrypted tablespaces after a restart, and certificate issuance stops.

What the team does not yet know: the last backup tokens are four months old and keys created since may not be present, two of the five key custodians are traveling, the vendor says a rollback of the firmware is possible but may not repair the store, and one of the backup tokens in the safe has an expired battery.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize the scale of the failure. Participants map which services depend on the HSM: payment authorization, TDE databases, certificate issuance and code signing, and determine the immediate blast radius.
  • Preserve what remains and prevent further damage. The team stops replication and firmware operations, preserves device state and logs, and avoids actions that could destroy remaining key material.
  • Assemble the key custodian quorum. The team locates custodians, tokens and smart cards, verifies authorization procedures and manages the three-of-five requirement under time pressure.
  • Decide recovery path. Participants compare restoring from backup tokens, vendor-assisted repair, recreating keys and re-keying data, and failing over to an alternative HSM environment, including the effect on data protected by keys created since the last backup.
  • Maintain critical services. The team identifies fallback options for payment authorization, such as stand-in processing within network rules, and ensures databases do not restart unnecessarily.
  • Maintain security and compliance. The team handles key management compliance requirements and considers whether the event is a security incident.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Never state or speculate on actual key values, PINs or custodian credentials during the exercise. Refer to roles and procedures only.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Cryptographic errors across services lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment and scope. Replication and updates are frozen; blast radius is mapped.

T+01:15 — Custodian and token assembly. Custodians and backup tokens are located and verified.

T+02:00 — Vendor engagement and options. Repair, restore and rebuild options are compared.

T+03:00 — Recovery path decision. A path is chosen with data and key loss analysis.

T+04:30 — Key ceremony and restore. Key restoration is performed under dual control.

T+06:00 — Validation. Cryptographic operations, payment flows and databases are tested.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Services released, incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Both HSMs refuse key operations after a failed firmware update

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 02:48. Payment authorization success rates fall from 99.6 percent to 4 percent. Application logs show cryptographic errors such as 'key handle invalid' and 'device in error state'. The on-call HSM engineer finds HSM-A in a recovery state and HSM-B reporting a 'partition integrity check failed' after replication.

The database team reports that three TDE databases restarted during a patching reboot at 02:35 and cannot open their encrypted tablespaces. The other 11 databases are still running with keys in memory. The internal CA cannot issue certificates.

The key backup tokens are in the safe at each site. The security lead says two of five custodians are traveling, one abroad. The vendor hotline puts the team in a queue with 40 minutes' wait.

Facilitator provides: the HSM state screens, the application error samples, the custodian roster and the key inventory at the last backup.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident, set severity and name the HSM lead, custodian coordinator and scribe.
  • Decide to freeze all HSM, firmware and database restart activity.
  • Set the next update at T+00:30.
Cryptography & PKI Engineering (HSM)
  • Explain the state of both HSMs and why replication spread the problem.
  • Stop replication and firmware operations, and preserve logs.
  • List what the backup tokens hold and what is missing.
Application & Payments Owners
  • Payments owner: assess stand-in processing and notify the card network.
  • Database owners: stop further restarts of TDE databases.
  • PKI owner: list what depends on the CA.
Security Operations & Key Custodians
  • Verify the custodian roster and quorum possibilities.
  • Review HSM administrative logs for tampering.
  • Watch for fraud during stand-in processing.
Server, Storage & Backup Operations
  • Stop reboots of servers that need keys.
  • Check the TDE wallet backups.
  • Prepare secure transport for tokens.
Vendor & Support Liaison
  • Open the case and request a named engineer.
  • Ask whether the corruption is a known firmware defect.
  • Request a spare unit.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Activate the payment continuity plan.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask whether the firmware update followed change control and a rollback plan.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. Which services stopped and which are running only because keys are in memory? What must not be restarted?
  2. Why did the corruption replicate, and what should have prevented it?
  3. What does the backup token contain, and which keys created since are lost?
  4. Who are the custodians, how do you reach quorum, and can remote custodians participate?
  5. Is this an accident or a possible attack and how would you know?
  6. What are the options: restore, vendor repair, rebuild keys, and what is the impact of each on data?
  7. What stand-in processing options exist and what limits apply?
  8. What do you tell the card network and customers?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the expired battery token until the team checks the tokens.
  • If the team wants to re-initialize the device, ask what they would lose and who authorizes.
  • Check how many people know the ceremony procedure.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, sets priorities and authorizes the key ceremony under dual control.
  • Decides on the use of stand-in processing and acceptance of any risk.
  • Runs a 30-minute cadence, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Ensures security and compliance leaders are informed early.

Cryptography & PKI Engineering (HSM)

  • Diagnoses the HSM state, suspends replication and prevents further firmware changes.
  • Determines which keys were present at the last backup and which keys were created since.
  • Prepares the restore procedure for the backup tokens, including firmware and partition prerequisites.
  • Plans the health checks and test vectors for validation.

Application & Payments Owners

  • Payments owner identifies authorization flows affected and the stand-in processing options, and informs the card network.
  • Database owners prevent restarts of TDE databases to keep their keys in memory and plan for key retrieval.
  • PKI owner lists certificates and signing operations dependent on the CA key.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Security Operations & Key Custodians

  • Verifies custodian identities and the integrity of tokens and smart cards before the ceremony.
  • Considers whether the event includes malicious tampering and reviews HSM administrative access logs.
  • Monitors for fraud or abuse during stand-in processing.
  • Preserves evidence.

Server, Storage & Backup Operations

  • Prevents restarts of database servers and application servers that would require key retrieval.
  • Checks backups of the TDE wallets and whether they are usable without the HSM.
  • Prepares systems for key restoration and verification.
  • Supports secure transport of tokens.

Vendor & Support Liaison

  • Opens a severity-one case, shares device logs and firmware versions, and requests a named engineer.
  • Asks the vendor whether the failure is known and what recovery methods exist.
  • Obtains written guidance for any risky action such as key store re-initialization.
  • Arranges a spare or loaner device.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity plans for payments and informs partners and card networks as required.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership and customers.
  • Coordinates manual processes.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.24 use of cryptography; SOC 2 availability and security).
  • Evaluates payment card industry and regulatory obligations for key management incidents.
  • Captures evidence of dual control and the key ceremony.
  • Logs findings on backup age, custodian coverage and firmware change control.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Treat the HSM cluster as a single logical system with a shared fate for replication. Use staged firmware updates: update one device, validate, and only then replicate; never allow a device in an error state to replicate its partition.

Back up keys frequently and test restores. Schedule backups after any key creation, verify that the tokens read, replace batteries and keep copies at separate locations.

Plan custodian coverage: more qualified custodians than the quorum needs, geographic distribution, alternates and a documented remote-participation policy that satisfies compliance.

Reduce dependency on a single key store: separate payment keys from database and PKI keys, maintain recoverable TDE wallet backups and avoid automatic restarts that need key retrieval.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.24) and SOC 2, and payment standards, expect evidence of key management procedures, dual control, backup tests and change control for firmware.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on key custodian coverage, backup currency and containment actions.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team stop replication and what stopped it?
  2. Why did corruption replicate and what is the firmware update procedure?
  3. How current are the backup tokens, and when were they last tested?
  4. Who are the custodians, and how long to assemble quorum?
  5. What is your policy on remote custodians and is it documented?
  6. Which services kept running only due to keys in memory and what protected them?
  7. What stand-in processing arrangements exist with the card network?
  8. How did the team decide between restore, repair and rebuild?
  9. How would keys created since the last backup be recovered or replaced?
  10. What controls applied to the key ceremony and who witnessed?
  11. How did security assess whether tampering occurred?
  12. What did you tell partners and customers?
  13. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  14. What access or credentials were missing?
  15. What would you change in firmware change control?
