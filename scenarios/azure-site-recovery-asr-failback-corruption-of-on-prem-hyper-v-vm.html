Azure Site Recovery (ASR) Failback Corruption of On-Prem Hyper-V VM

Hybrid Cloud (Azure Focus)

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an ASR failback from Azure to on-premises Hyper-V in which a reverse replication interruption and a storage issue leave the target VM disk corrupted, so the application boots but data is inconsistent, while the Azure copy continues to take production writes.

Participants are working-level: cloud infrastructure engineers, virtualization engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of reverse replication, failback options, resynchronization, application-consistent points and data validation.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Beacon Manufacturing failed over its plant scheduling and quality systems to Azure during a datacenter power event two days ago. Power is restored and the team begins failback to on-premises Hyper-V with reverse replication. The system holds quality inspection records used for regulatory release.

At 07:00 on Friday, the failback starts. During the final synchronization the on-premises storage array reports a controller failover and the replication job reports success. The VM boots on Hyper-V but the quality database fails integrity checks and some files are missing. Meanwhile, Azure still holds the last consistent copy but traffic has been switched to on-premises and production writes have gone to the corrupted VM for 40 minutes.

What the team does not yet know: the reverse replication was interrupted for 6 minutes during the array failover, 40 minutes of production writes exist only on the corrupted copy, the Azure VM is still available and could be made primary again but would lose those writes, and the plant must ship lots by noon.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a corrupted failback. Participants run integrity checks before trusting a failback.
  • Stop further writes. The team freezes production on the corrupted VM and preserves it.
  • Choose the recovery path. Engineers weigh returning to Azure, repairing the on-premises VM and merging writes.
  • Protect quality records. Participants maintain integrity of regulatory release data.
  • Keep the plant running. The team prepares manual or degraded operations.
  • Improve failback practice. Participants review checklists, validation gates and storage assumptions.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • No failback is declared complete until application integrity checks have passed, not just replication status.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Failed integrity checks after failback lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Writes are frozen and copies preserved.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Corruption and write exposure are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery path is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. The chosen path is executed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Systems stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Data is validated and reconciled.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: Failback boots but data is corrupt

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 07:50. The plant scheduling VM is back on Hyper-V but the quality database fails integrity checks. Production traffic has run on it for 40 minutes.

The infrastructure engineer finds a storage controller failover during final sync and a 6-minute reverse replication interruption.

The Azure VM still holds the last consistent copy. The plant ships lots at noon. Quality records support regulatory release.

Facilitator provides: ASR job log, array event log, integrity check output and write volume estimate.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize freezing production on the corrupted VM.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Infrastructure Engineering (Site Recovery)
  • Preserve Azure copy.
  • Plan reprotect.
  • Plan export.
Virtualization Engineering (Hyper-V)
  • Freeze and snapshot VM.
  • Run checks.
  • Plan merge.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Decide data loss tolerance.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Prepare manual records.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Preserve logs.
  • Watch storage.
Microsoft, Carrier & Vendor Support Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about interruption.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan plant messaging.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about quality record duties.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you prove the failback is corrupt?
  2. Which copy do you trust and why?
  3. What happens to the 40 minutes of writes?
  4. How do you preserve the corrupted VM?
  5. What can the plant do manually?
  6. Why did replication report success?
  7. What do quality and regulators hear?
  8. What validation must precede declaring failback done?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the interruption until T1 reads job logs.
  • If players continue production, stop and discuss.
  • Look for freeze first.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes recovering the failed-back systems with intact data and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Cloud Infrastructure Engineering (Site Recovery)

  • Reads ASR job logs and recovery points.
  • Preserves the Azure VM and reactivates it if chosen.
  • Plans reverse replication again.
  • Plans data export of recent writes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Virtualization Engineering (Hyper-V)

  • Freezes the on-premises VM and snapshots it.
  • Runs integrity checks and extracts the 40 minutes of writes.
  • Plans merge into the chosen primary.
  • Validates the application.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and quality managers decide acceptable data loss and manual steps.
  • The service desk logs plant contacts.
  • Owners prepare manual inspection records.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of replication, array status and application health.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Watches storage alerts.

  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

  • Assesses quality record integrity.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Treat failback as a recovery event with its own plan: freeze storage changes, validate with application integrity checks and keep the source copy until validation passes.

Never move production writes to the target before gates are met.

Test failback as often as failover.

Alert on replication interruptions and storage events during sync.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.14, 5.30, 8.13) and SOC 2 expect DR test, validation and integrity evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on failback validation gates and source copy retention.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you prove corruption?
  2. What did you freeze?
  3. Which copy did you trust?
  4. How did you handle the 40 minutes of writes?
  5. How did you handle the lot release?
  6. How did you plan to retry?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Microsoft, Carrier & Vendor Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
