Autonomous AI Agent Swarm Coordinating Distributed Denial-of-Wallet Extortion Attack

AI Failure & Autonomous Agent Disruption

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an autonomous agent swarm that calls expensive pay-per-use endpoints such as AI inference and serverless APIs at scale, running up costs to extort payment.

Participants are working-level: cloud cost and security engineers, cloud platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AI platform and cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of rate limits, quotas, budget controls, abuse detection and provider coordination.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Pinewood Software offers a public API with AI inference and report generation billed per call. A free tier uses weak identity checks. Budget alerts exist but trigger no automatic shutdown. Rate limits apply per key and the swarm creates many keys.

At 03:00 on Sunday, agents register thousands of free accounts and call the most expensive endpoints. Costs rise 10,000 dollars per hour. A message arrives demanding 80,000 dollars in cryptocurrency to stop. By 08:00, charges exceed 70,000 dollars and legitimate users see throttling and errors.

What the team does not yet know: how long the attack will run, whether the provider will credit abusive charges, whether the swarm adapts to blocks, and which controls can stop costs without harming real customers.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize denial-of-wallet. Participants link cost spikes to abusive account patterns.
  • Cap spend. The team imposes hard quotas and disables free-tier access to costly endpoints.
  • Block abuse. Engineers use identity friction and behavior-based blocks.
  • Handle extortion. Participants assess the demand and legal obligations.
  • Engage the provider. The team seeks credits and support.
  • Improve cost resilience. Participants review budgets, quotas and identity at signup.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Impose hard spending caps and disable costly endpoints for the free tier before analyzing, because every hour adds 10,000 dollars.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Cost spikes and an extortion demand lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Caps imposed and abusive access blocked.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Pattern and exposure are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery and cost plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Controls proceed in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Costs stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Controls are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes services that cannot be bankrupted by traffic and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Cloud Cost & Security Engineering

  • Applies hard budget caps and quotas.
  • Disables free-tier access to expensive endpoints.
  • Plans signup verification.
  • Plans cost anomaly alarms.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Platform Engineering

  • Blocks abusive accounts and patterns.
  • Preserves capacity for paying customers.
  • Validates services.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and product leaders define customer tiers to protect.
  • The service desk logs customer complaints.

  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison

  • Asks for credits.
  • Requests guidance on quotas.
  • Escalates through account teams.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for software platform operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 8.6 capacity management, 8.16 monitoring, 5.15 access control, 8.20 network security; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses financial exposure and service availability.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Set automatic hard spending caps and per-account cost limits on pay-per-use endpoints.

Require stronger identity checks before access to expensive features.

Apply cost-aware rate limiting by account, network and behavior.

Alert on signup surges and spending velocity with automatic response.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.16, 5.15, 8.20) and SOC 2 expect capacity and monitoring evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on cap-first and protecting paying customers.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you recognize it?
  2. What did you cap first?
  3. How did you protect customers?
  4. What did you decide on the demand?
  5. What did you ask the provider?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
