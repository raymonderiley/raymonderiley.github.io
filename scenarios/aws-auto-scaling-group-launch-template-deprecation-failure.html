AWS Auto Scaling Group Launch Template Deprecation Failure

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a failure where Auto Scaling groups can no longer launch instances because the launch template references a deprecated AMI, an instance type unavailable in an Availability Zone, or a snapshot that was cleaned up. Existing instances keep running, but scale-out, replacement and self-healing silently stop working until a surge or failure exposes the gap.

Participants are working-level: platform and SRE engineers, release and image engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of launch templates, AMI lifecycle, instance type capacity, scaling activity history and golden image pipelines.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Wexford Logistics runs its order and routing services on 22 Auto Scaling groups. Launch templates reference AMIs built monthly by an image pipeline. A cost-cleanup job introduced in the spring deregisters AMIs older than 90 days and deletes their snapshots. Launch template versions are not updated automatically; some groups reference 'Default' versions last edited a year ago.

At 06:30 on Monday, morning order volume ramps up and the groups attempt to scale out. Scaling activities fail with 'The image id does not exist' for six groups. Over the weekend, an instance retirement event and a spot interruption also terminated instances that could not be replaced. By 07:15, three groups are 40 percent below desired capacity and CPU on the remaining instances is above 90 percent.

What the team does not yet know: the cleanup job deleted AMIs still referenced by launch templates, the newest AMI built last week fails a health check due to an agent version bug, the instance family in one group has no capacity in two Availability Zones, and the Auto Scaling alarms only notify a mailbox read weekly.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize silent scaling failure. Participants read Auto Scaling activity history, desired versus in-service counts and launch failures to find why capacity is short.
  • Restore capacity quickly. The team repoints launch templates to valid AMIs, selects instance types with available capacity and uses mixed instance policies.
  • Avoid compounding errors. Engineers validate new AMIs on a canary before mass rollout and avoid wiping out working instances with instance refresh.
  • Prioritize services. Participants decide which groups to fix first based on business impact.
  • Preserve evidence of the cleanup failure. The team documents the cleanup job's behavior, approvals and logs.
  • Improve image and capacity hygiene. Participants review AMI lifecycle, launch template versioning, alarms and capacity reservations.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Cite the specific group, launch template version or AMI ID (as described by facilitators) in every action.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Capacity shortfalls and high CPU lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Scaling is stabilized and priorities are set.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Launch failure causes are identified for each group.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A fix plan and canary approach are agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Launch templates are repaired and capacity restored.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Groups reach desired capacity and load normalizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Services and health checks are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Groups cannot scale out and CPU saturates

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 07:20. The routing API is slow and the order intake dashboard shows queue growth. Three Auto Scaling groups show desired capacity 30 but only 18 in service. Scaling activities fail with 'The image id does not exist'. CPU on remaining instances is above 90 percent.

The platform engineer finds six groups with failing launch template references. The AMI cleanup job deleted images older than 90 days. One group uses an instance family with insufficient capacity in two Availability Zones. The newest AMI exists but is untested.

Scaling failure alarms went to a mailbox not read since last week. The AWS Health Dashboard shows no events. Order volume will peak at 09:00.

Facilitator provides: scaling activity history, launch template versions, the AMI list and cleanup job logs.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide priorities across groups.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering
  • Identify failing groups and reasons.
  • Plan launch template updates and instance type changes.
  • Raise capacity floors where possible.
Release & Image Engineering
  • Identify a valid AMI and test it.
  • Stop the cleanup job.
  • Plan canary launches.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank services and prepare load shedding.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Prepare queueing.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Fix alert routing.
  • List missing alarms.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case on capacity.
  • Ask about reservations.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform depots.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about the cleanup job approval.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you find why each group is short of capacity?
  2. What stops scale-out for six groups and how do you fix each?
  3. Do you trust the newest AMI and how do you test it quickly?
  4. What do you do about the instance family capacity problem?
  5. Which service gets capacity first?
  6. How do you avoid instance refresh breaking running instances?
  7. Why did alarms not reach anyone?
  8. What do depots and leadership need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the AMI agent bug until REL launches a canary.
  • If players trigger instance refresh everywhere, ask what happens to current capacity.
  • Look for a canary first approach.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes order intake and routing services and decides on load shedding.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency launch template changes.
  • Decides on temporary service limits.

Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering

  • Reviews scaling activity history and launch failure messages for each group.
  • Plans launch template version updates and mixed instance policies.
  • Sets minimum capacity floors and temporarily raises desired counts where possible.
  • Monitors CPU and health after changes.

Release & Image Engineering

  • Identifies valid AMIs and verifies the newest image's health and agent versions.
  • Checks the cleanup job's scope and stops it.
  • Plans canary launches before broad rollout.
  • Rebuilds an AMI if needed.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank services by business impact and tolerance for slowness.
  • The service desk handles delay reports.
  • Owners prepare load shedding and queueing.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of desired versus in-service capacity and failed scaling activities.
  • Confirms scaling notifications and fixes routing.
  • Checks AWS Health and capacity events.
  • Adds alarms for scaling failures.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a case on instance type capacity in specific Availability Zones.
  • Asks about capacity reservations and alternatives.
  • Requests guidance on launch template behavior.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for order and dispatch operations and informs depots.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual dispatch if needed.
  • Tracks the impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.6 capacity management, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses contractual impact.
  • Captures evidence of the cleanup job control gap.
  • Logs findings on image lifecycle.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Treat images as managed assets. Link AMI cleanup to launch template references, use deprecation before deletion and test a scale-out regularly in each group.

Version launch templates deliberately, pin to tested versions and use instance refresh with safe minimum healthy percentages.

Diversify capacity: mixed instance policies, multiple AZs, and capacity reservations for critical groups.

Alarm on scaling failures and desired-versus-in-service gaps, routed to an on-call owner.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect capacity management evidence, change control for automation and tested scaling.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on image lifecycle and scaling visibility.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you learn scaling was failing and why so late?
  2. What stopped each group from launching?
  3. How did you validate a replacement AMI?
  4. How did you handle insufficient capacity?
  5. What happened with instance refresh?
  6. How did you handle the cleanup job?
  7. How were load shedding decisions made?
  8. How was capacity prioritized across services?
  9. How accurate were updates to depots and leadership?
  10. What did AWS support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How are AMIs retired today?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alarms are missing?
  15. What would you change in image management?
