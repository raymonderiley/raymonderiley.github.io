GCP Pub/Sub Subscription Backlog and Message Retention Expiry

Google Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Pub/Sub subscription whose consumers stall after a dependency failure, so the backlog grows toward the retention limit and unacknowledged messages begin to expire, which would silently lose shipment events and billing records.

Participants are working-level: messaging platform engineers, application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Google Cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of acknowledgement deadlines, backlog age, retention, dead-letter topics, replay and seek.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Brindle Logistics publishes shipment events to a Pub/Sub topic. A subscription feeds the billing service with a 7-day retention and a 60-second acknowledgement deadline. A dead-letter topic is configured after 5 delivery attempts. Message replay is enabled on the topic.

At 16:00 on Friday, the billing service starts failing because its database connection pool is exhausted after a schema lock. Messages are redelivered, hit the delivery attempt limit and begin moving to the dead-letter topic, which has no subscription. The oldest unacknowledged message age climbs. By Sunday morning a reviewer notices a backlog of 22 million messages with the oldest at 6 days 20 hours, close to the 7-day expiry.

What the team does not yet know: some messages in the dead-letter topic have no subscription so they are retained only briefly, the consumer cannot process at more than 3,000 messages per second, a seek to replay would redeliver duplicates, and the weekend staffing is minimal.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize silent backlog risk. Participants read backlog size, oldest message age and dead-letter metrics to see how close messages are to expiry.
  • Prevent message loss. The team extends retention, creates a dead-letter subscription and protects messages that are about to expire.
  • Restore consumption. Engineers fix the dependency and scale consumers within safe limits.
  • Replay safely. Participants plan idempotent replay and duplicate handling.
  • Reconcile billing. The team compares events to billing records to find gaps.
  • Improve messaging resilience. Participants review alerting on backlog age, retention policy and dead-letter handling.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Protect the oldest messages first and prove it before working on speed.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Backlog age alerts lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Messages are protected and the dependency is fixed.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Backlog and dead-letter state are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A drain and replay plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Consumers are scaled and replay runs.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Backlog age falls.

T+06:00 — Validation. Billing is reconciled.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

It is Sunday 08:10. The billing subscription shows 22 million unacknowledged messages and the oldest age is 6 days 20 hours.

The messaging engineer finds a dead-letter topic with no subscription and billing consumers failing since Friday 16:00.

Consumers can handle 3,000 messages per second. Expiry will begin in about four hours.

Facilitator provides: metrics, subscription settings, dead-letter config and billing error logs.

  • Approve retention changes.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Messaging Platform Engineering (Pub/Sub)

  • Create dead-letter subscription.
  • Plan seek.
Application Engineering
  • Fix the lock and pool.
  • Scale consumers.
  • Ensure idempotency.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Set billing priorities.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Plan reconciliation.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Watch expiry.
  • Preserve metrics.
Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about retention.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan finance contact.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about billing record obligations.

Discussion questions

  1. What is the first action to prevent loss?
  2. How do you protect dead-lettered messages?
  3. What is the effect of extending retention?
  4. How fast can you drain and is it enough?
  5. How do you avoid double billing on replay?

  7. How do you reconcile billing?
  8. What do finance and customers hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the dead-letter gap until T1 checks the topic.

  • Look for protection before speed.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes preventing message loss before retention expires and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Messaging Platform Engineering (Pub/Sub)

  • Reads backlog size, oldest age and dead-letter metrics.
  • Extends retention and creates a dead-letter subscription.
  • Plans seek or replay.
  • Plans consumer scaling.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Engineering

  • Fixes the database lock and connection pool.
  • Scales consumers within limits.
  • Ensures idempotent billing writes.
  • Validates counts.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners decide billing priorities and acceptable delay.
  • The service desk logs finance questions.
  • Owners plan reconciliation.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.16 monitoring; SOC 2 processing integrity).
  • Assesses billing completeness and record retention.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Alert on oldest unacknowledged message age against retention, not just on backlog size.

Give every dead-letter topic a monitored subscription and an owner.

Set retention to match recovery time realities including weekends.

Make consumers idempotent so replay is safe.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.16, 8.13) and SOC 2 expect monitoring and processing integrity evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on backlog-age alerting and safe replay.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you find the backlog and how late?
  2. What did you protect first?
  3. How did you save dead letters?
  4. How did you drain in order?
  5. How did you avoid double billing?
  6. How did you reconcile?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
