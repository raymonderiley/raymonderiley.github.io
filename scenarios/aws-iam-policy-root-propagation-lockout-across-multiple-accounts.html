AWS IAM Policy Root Propagation Lockout Across Multiple Accounts

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a lockout caused by an IAM or organization-level policy change that propagates from the management account to dozens of member accounts. A deny that was meant to restrict one risky action matches far more broadly than intended, and administrators, pipelines and break-glass roles lose the permissions they need to undo it.

Participants are working-level: cloud identity and IAM engineers, platform and landing zone engineers, application and pipeline owners, security operations, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The scenario highlights guardrail design, break-glass access and the limits of what can be fixed from the console when the permissions to fix it are the thing that is broken.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Harmon Energy Trading runs 63 AWS accounts under one AWS Organization, organized into OUs for production, non-production, security and shared services. Access is through IAM Identity Center with permission sets, and CI/CD pipelines use IAM roles assumed through OIDC. Service control policies (SCPs) are managed in the management account by a small platform team using infrastructure as code with a two-person review.

At 14:05 on Wednesday, a platform engineer merges a change to an SCP intended to deny IAM user creation and access key creation outside approved roles. A condition key was written with a wildcard and a NotAction construct that unintentionally denies iam:*, sts:AssumeRole and organizations:* actions for principals not tagged 'platform-approved'. The pipeline applies the SCP to the root of the organization at 14:12. Within minutes, engineers cannot assume roles, pipelines fail on AssumeRoleWithWebIdentity, and the Identity Center permission sets stop working in member accounts. The management account is exempt from SCPs, but the only people with management account access use SSO, which is now partly broken.

What the team does not yet know: the break-glass IAM user in the management account exists but its credentials are in a vault that requires SSO, the root user's MFA device is held by a manager who is on leave, the policy change was approved by one reviewer who did not notice the NotAction logic, and production trading workloads continue to run but cannot be scaled, deployed or inspected.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a policy-driven lockout. Participants interpret AccessDenied messages that cite an explicit deny in a service control policy, use CloudTrail, IAM Access Analyzer and policy simulator evidence and distinguish this from an AWS service issue.
  • Regain control from the management account. The team determines who can act in the management account, how to detach or fix the SCP, and what break-glass paths exist, including root user recovery and its approvals.
  • Avoid making the lockout worse. Participants avoid mass changes that could disrupt running production workloads, and sequence rollback of the SCP carefully.
  • Protect running workloads. The team confirms that trading workloads continue to run, identifies what cannot be changed, and decides on manual scaling or freeze windows.
  • Check whether the change was malicious. Security determines whether the change was an accident or an attempt to lock out defenders, reviewing the pipeline, reviewers and identities.
  • Improve guardrail change management. The team examines policy testing, staged rollout to a test OU, break-glass design and approval quality.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Do not state or invent credentials, MFA codes or account IDs beyond the exercise. Refer to roles and procedures only.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Access failures and pipeline errors lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Cause identification. The SCP change is identified as the likely cause.

T+01:15 — Break-glass access. The team determines how to reach the management account.

T+02:00 — Rollback plan. SCP detach or correction is planned and reviewed.

T+03:00 — Execution. The SCP is detached or fixed and access is restored in stages.

T+04:30 — Validation. Roles, pipelines and permission sets are verified.

T+06:00 — Security review. Logs are reviewed for abuse of the window and policy intent.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operations resume and incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: AssumeRole fails across the organization after an SCP change

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 14:25. The service desk and engineering channels report that nobody can access AWS accounts through Identity Center and that pipelines fail with 'AccessDenied: explicit deny in a service control policy'. Twenty-eight deployments are stuck.

An IAM engineer inspects a CloudTrail event and sees the deny cited against the root organization SCP updated at 14:12. The change was merged by a platform engineer and approved by one reviewer. Trading applications in production accounts continue to run, but autoscaling actions requiring IAM permissions are failing.

The management account is not subject to SCPs, but the platform team logs in with SSO. The break-glass IAM user's credentials are in a vault protected by SSO. The root user's MFA device is held by the cloud director, who is on leave and unreachable.

Facilitator provides: the SCP diff, the AccessDenied sample, the pipeline failure list and the break-glass procedure summary.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide to freeze all changes in all accounts.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Identity & IAM Engineering
  • Identify the offending statement and affected principals.
  • Describe the fix and how to test it.
  • List the permission sets and roles to verify afterward.
Cloud Platform & Landing Zone Engineering
  • Explain who can act in the management account and how.
  • Prepare the revert or manual detach.
  • Plan staged reattachment.
Application & Pipeline Owners
  • List failed deployments and hold releases.
  • Confirm trading systems are running and what cannot change.
  • Plan manual workarounds.
Security Operations
  • Review who approved and merged the change.
  • Check for suspicious activity during the window.
  • Preserve evidence.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a high-severity case.
  • Ask about root user recovery.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform the trading desk.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for the documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask how SCP changes are tested and approved.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you tell an SCP deny from a permission boundary or a resource policy issue from the error and CloudTrail?
  2. Who can act in the management account right now, and how?
  3. The break-glass credentials depend on SSO. What is your plan, and what does the root user recovery involve?
  4. What is the safest way to fix the policy: detach, revert or edit, and in what order across OUs?
  5. Are running trading workloads at risk and what can you not change?
  6. Was this an accident or an attack, and what would change your plan?
  7. How did a single reviewer approve a NotAction wildcard?
  8. What should the trading desk and leadership be told?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the vaulted break-glass credentials until the team asks for them.
  • If players want to detach the SCP everywhere at once, ask what protections the SCP provided and who accepts that risk.
  • Check whether a test OU exists for guardrail changes.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Inject #2: Pipeline role blocked from fixing

Time frame T+00:20 · Variant A

Situation

The platform engineer tries to push the revert through the infrastructure pipeline. The pipeline's role cannot assume the management account deployment role because the same SCP denies sts:AssumeRole. The revert job fails within seconds.

The team realizes the fix path runs through the thing that is broken, and the on-call lead suggests editing the policy by hand in the console.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Authorize a manual change and name who performs it with a second person observing.
Cloud Identity & IAM Engineering
  • Confirm exactly which statement blocks the pipeline and whether a narrow edit would unblock it.
Cloud Platform & Landing Zone Engineering
  • Identify who has direct management account console access and by what method.
  • Explain risks of a manual edit that drifts from the code, and plan to reconcile afterward.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Record the emergency change and its approver.

Discussion questions

  1. What is the manual path and who is allowed to use it?
  2. How do you control a hand edit during an incident?
  3. How will the code and the live policy be reconciled?
  4. Why should recovery roles be exempt from deny guardrails?

Facilitator notes

  • Let players discover the circular dependency.
  • Ask for a second person on every emergency action.
  • Link to architect guidance on exempting recovery principals.

Inject #3: Root user needed

Time frame T+00:20 · Variant B

Situation

The only remaining route to the management account is the root user. The root email mailbox belongs to a distribution list, and the hardware MFA key is in a safe at the headquarters that only two people can open. One is on leave and the other is in a meeting.

Using root is allowed by policy only with the CISO's approval, and the CISO's delegate cannot be found.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Decide whether to invoke root and who authorizes it.
  • Set a deadline after which the alternative path is chosen.
Security Operations
  • Plan to monitor and record all root activity and rotate credentials afterward.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case and ask about account recovery options if the MFA is unavailable.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Record the approval chain and any deviation from policy.

Discussion questions

  1. Who can authorize root use and how is it controlled?
  2. What is the recovery path if the MFA key is unavailable?
  3. What monitoring applies while root is in use?
  4. What must change about root custody afterward?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not allow a hand-wave; ask who physically holds the key.
  • Note the single-point-of-failure custody.
  • Tests knowledge of AWS recovery without MFA.

Inject #4: Suspicious API calls during the window

Time frame T+01:15 · Variant A

Situation

Security Operations finds API calls from an unfamiliar IP address using a long-term access key for an IAM user in a shared services account, made during the first twenty minutes of the lockout. The calls listed roles and policies.

Nobody can say whether this is related to the lockout or a coincidence.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Decide how to run security and recovery in parallel without conflict.
Cloud Identity & IAM Engineering
  • Check whether the key's permissions were affected by the SCP.
Security Operations
  • Preserve CloudTrail, correlate with the SCP change timeline and determine the key's owner.
  • Decide whether to disable the key now, accepting the effect on its owner.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Assess whether a security incident notification process applies.

Discussion questions

  1. Is the lockout related to this activity and what evidence would show it?
  2. Do you disable the key during recovery?
  3. How do you run the two efforts in parallel?
  4. What triggers a formal security incident declaration?

Facilitator notes

  • Keep ambiguous; do not confirm compromise.
  • Watch how players weigh evidence.
  • Check evidence preservation.

Inject #5: Autoscaling failing in production

Time frame T+01:15 · Variant B

Situation

A production trading API cluster hits a market-data surge. Auto Scaling tries to add instances but the launch fails because the service-linked role actions are denied by the SCP. Latency rises and the trading desk is concerned.

Manual console launch is also blocked for most engineers.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Decide whether to prioritize a partial policy fix that restores scaling first.
Cloud Platform & Landing Zone Engineering
  • Pre-scale by modifying desired capacity from a path that still works.
  • List other services depending on service-linked roles.
Application & Pipeline Owners
  • Shed noncritical load and warn the trading desk about limited capacity.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Coordinate with the desk on manual risk limits.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you protect capacity while access is broken?
  2. Could a partial fix restore scaling sooner and what are the risks?
  3. What other services rely on service-linked roles?
  4. How are trading risk limits adjusted?

Facilitator notes

  • Tests operational impact beyond administration.
  • Look for the idea of a narrowly scoped carve-out.
  • Ask who owns trading risk decisions.

Inject #6: Revert merges but the pipeline queue jams

Time frame T+02:00 · Variant A

Situation

Access to the management account is restored and the revert commit merges. The pipeline has 28 failed runs queued ahead and starts replaying them, including an unrelated infrastructure change that would modify networking in production.

The pipeline owner is unsure how to cancel the queue safely.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Reinforce the change freeze.
Cloud Identity & IAM Engineering
  • Verify the revert in a test OU before the root.
Cloud Platform & Landing Zone Engineering
  • Cancel or pause queued runs and apply the revert first.
  • Review the unrelated change before allowing it.
Application & Pipeline Owners
  • Confirm which held releases may proceed afterward.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you protect production from queued changes?
  2. What order should changes be applied in?
  3. Who approves releasing held deployments?
  4. How is the revert verified?

Facilitator notes

  • Look for freeze discipline.
  • Ask about a test OU.
  • Tests release management under pressure.

Inject #7: Wrong OU detached

Time frame T+02:00 · Variant B

Situation

Under time pressure, an engineer detaches the SCP from the Production OU rather than from the organization root, exposing production accounts to actions the original policy intended to block, including IAM user creation.

The error is spotted twenty minutes later during review.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Reinforce a two-person rule on every change.
Cloud Identity & IAM Engineering
  • Reattach the correct configuration and assess which actions occurred in the exposure window.
Security Operations
  • Review CloudTrail for IAM user and key creation during the window.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Record the error as a recovery-stage deviation.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you manage human error during recovery?
  2. What should be checked after the exposure window?
  3. How can two-person execution be enforced under pressure?
  4. What does this say about guardrail design?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not blame; examine process.
  • Ask who checks each change.
  • Link to change control evidence.

Inject #8: Leadership demands an ETA

The CIO and the head of trading operations join the bridge. Regulators expect resilient controls, and the head of trading asks whether any trade capture or risk systems are affected.

The team has partial restoration in some accounts and not others.

Inject #9: Access returns but sessions are stale

Time frame T+04:30 · Variant A

Situation

The corrected SCP is attached. Some users regain access, but many still see AccessDenied because cached credentials, existing sessions and Identity Center assignments take time to refresh. Some OIDC roles still fail trust evaluation.

The service desk is flooded with calls and conflicting advice circulates.

Role actions

Cloud Identity & IAM Engineering
  • Explain session expiry and what to refresh, and check trust policies for OIDC roles.
Cloud Platform & Landing Zone Engineering
  • Re-run key pipelines in a controlled order.
Application & Pipeline Owners
  • Service desk: publish one set of instructions and a status page.
Security Operations
  • Track success rates of role assumption over time.

Discussion questions

  1. What do you tell users who still cannot access?
  2. Which components cache permission decisions?
  3. How do you verify recovery systematically?
  4. How do you stop conflicting advice?

Facilitator notes

  • Tests post-fix reality.
  • Look for a single source of truth for status.
  • Avoid declaring victory early.

Inject #12: Close-out and exit criteria

Time frame T+07:30 · Variant B

Situation

Access is restored and pipelines are green. The break-glass user was used and its credentials need rotation. The root user was accessed and its MFA must be re-sealed. Several temporary console edits differ from the code.

The scribe's timeline is incomplete.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Set exit criteria including credential rotation and code reconciliation.
Cloud Platform & Landing Zone Engineering
  • Reconcile live policies with code.
Security Operations
  • Rotate break-glass credentials and review root activity.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Finalize timeline and actions.

Discussion questions

  1. What must be true before closing?
  2. Which temporary changes remain?
  3. Who rotates and re-seals credentials?
  4. When is the post-incident review?

Facilitator notes

  • Leads into the debrief.
  • Check reconciliation of manual edits.
  • Confirm owners and dates.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, sets priority for restoring administrative access and protecting running trading workloads.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes use of the break-glass identity and any root user recovery.
  • Decides on a change freeze across all accounts.

Cloud Identity & IAM Engineering

  • Reads AccessDenied messages and CloudTrail events citing an explicit deny from an organization SCP and identifies the offending statement.
  • Uses the policy simulator and Access Analyzer to confirm which principals are affected.
  • Prepares the corrected policy and a safe sequence, testing it on a sandbox OU first when time allows.
  • Verifies Identity Center permission sets and OIDC roles after the fix.

Cloud Platform & Landing Zone Engineering

  • Determines who can act in the management account and how the SCP is managed in code, and prepares the revert commit and the manual detach.
  • Checks that the infrastructure pipeline's own role is not blocked from applying the fix.
  • Plans staged reattachment of a corrected SCP to OUs, starting with non-production.
  • Maintains an inventory of accounts and OUs affected.

Application & Pipeline Owners

  • Pipeline owners list failed deployments and in-flight releases and place them on hold.
  • Application owners confirm which trading systems continue to run and what cannot be changed.
  • Owners identify manual workarounds, including pre-scaled capacity.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Security Operations

  • Reviews CloudTrail in the management account for who approved and merged the change and any suspicious activity in the window.
  • Checks for use of long-term keys and unexpected principals while controls were disrupted.
  • Assesses whether the change aimed to hinder defenders and preserves evidence.
  • Monitors GuardDuty and Security Hub findings that may be affected.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a high-severity support case from the management account and asks for root user recovery steps if needed.
  • Asks about how SCPs affect service-linked roles and what AWS can and cannot do.
  • Records recommendations.
  • Escalates through the technical account manager.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates the continuity plan for trading operations and informs trading desk leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership.
  • Coordinates manual risk controls during the freeze.
  • Tracks time to restore administrative access.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.2 privileged access rights, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Evaluates regulatory implications for trading system change controls.
  • Captures evidence of approvals and the review gap.
  • Logs findings on break-glass design, root user custody and policy testing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Guardrails need guardrails. Test every SCP in a sandbox OU with the policy simulator, roll out OU by OU, require two real reviewers and add automated checks that fail on broad NotAction or wildcard denies of iam, sts and organizations actions.

Design break-glass for failure of SSO: emergency IAM roles or users in the management account with credentials and MFA stored offline in a split-custody process, tested quarterly and alerted on every use.

Keep a documented root user recovery path with named custodians and alternates, hardware MFA in two locations, and rehearsed procedures.

Exempt essential recovery principals from deny guardrails by design, using principal tags or ARNs reviewed by security, so that remediation always remains possible.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.2, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect evidence of privileged access management, change review quality and tested emergency access.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on break-glass access and the quality of the SCP review.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the lockout to the SCP change and what data showed it?
  2. Who can act in the management account and did that match your assumption?
  3. Where are break-glass credentials and did they depend on the broken path?
  4. Who holds the root user MFA and what is the recovery procedure?
  5. How was the SCP tested before it reached the organization root?
  6. How did the review process miss the NotAction logic?
  7. What was the sequence for fixing and why?
  8. What happened to running workloads and scaling?
  9. What did security find in the logs for the window?
  10. How was human error during recovery managed?
  11. How accurate were communications to leadership and the trading desk?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What would you change in guardrail rollout?
  15. How often will break-glass be tested?
