Zero-Day Firmware Implant in Core Enterprise Routers During Incident

Ransomware & Cyber-Recovery Forensics

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a firmware implant in core enterprise routers, exploited as a zero day, which survives reboots and reloads, redirects selected traffic and complicates recovery from an unrelated ransomware incident.

Participants are working-level: network engineers, infrastructure operations engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and forensics analysts, the forensics and vendor support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of device integrity verification, out-of-band management, traffic rerouting and replacement logistics.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Winterhaven Bank is recovering from ransomware when analysts notice odd routing behavior on core routers. Forensic comparison shows modified firmware images on four core routers, installed through a vulnerability that has no vendor patch. The implant mirrors traffic for selected subnets and sends it out of band.

At 14:00, the vendor confirms an active zero-day in this router model. The implant survives reloads, and a standard configuration reset does not remove it. Traffic for payment systems and recovery tools is among the mirrored subnets. Replacement routers are available for two of the four.

What the team does not yet know: how long the implant has been present, what traffic was mirrored, which other devices are affected, and how to run the network while replacing core routers.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize compromised network devices. Participants understand that the network itself cannot be trusted.
  • Protect sensitive traffic. The team moves recovery and payment traffic onto trusted paths.
  • Verify and replace. Engineers verify firmware integrity and replace routers.
  • Assess what was seen. Participants estimate the exposure of mirrored traffic.
  • Rebuild trust. The team re-baselines devices and credentials.
  • Improve network integrity. Participants review firmware verification and replacement readiness.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Assume the compromised network sees everything, and move recovery and payment traffic to trusted paths before replacing devices.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Implant confirmation leads to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Sensitive traffic moved and devices verified.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Exposure and scope are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A replacement plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Replacement proceeds in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Network stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Integrity is validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes a network that can be trusted again after an implant and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Network Engineering

  • Plans trusted paths for sensitive traffic using encryption and out-of-band links.
  • Verifies firmware on all routers.
  • Plans replacement.
  • Plans integrity monitoring.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Infrastructure Operations Engineering

  • Replaces the first routers.
  • Validates routing.
  • Handles capacity.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and payments teams identify traffic that must be protected.
  • The service desk logs reports.
  • Owners define manual procedures.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Analyzes mirrored traffic destinations.
  • Hunts for other devices.
  • Preserves images.
  • Adds alerts for firmware changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with the router vendor and forensics firm.
  • Asks for firmware verification tools and advisories.
  • Requests priority replacement hardware.
  • Escalates through account teams.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for bank network operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 8.20 network security, 8.21 network services security, 8.9 configuration management, 5.28 evidence collection; SOC 2 security).
  • Assesses network integrity and data protection.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Verify device firmware against vendor hashes and alert on image changes.

Encrypt sensitive traffic end to end so network devices cannot read it.

Keep spare core hardware and a tested replacement plan.

Maintain an out-of-band management path independent of the production network.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.20, 8.21, 8.9, 5.28) and SOC 2 expect network security and configuration evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on assume-compromise-first.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. What does the implant imply?
  2. What did you move first?
  3. How did you verify firmware?
  4. How did you plan replacement?
  5. What did you assess about exposure?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
