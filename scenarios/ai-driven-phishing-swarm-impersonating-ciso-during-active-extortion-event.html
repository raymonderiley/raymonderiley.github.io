AI-Driven Phishing Swarm Impersonating CISO During Active Extortion Event

AI Failure & Autonomous Agent Disruption

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an AI-driven phishing swarm that impersonates the CISO to thousands of staff and partners during an active extortion event, directing them to fake incident portals and conflicting instructions.

Participants are working-level: email security engineers, collaboration platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations analysts, the AI platform and cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of authoritative messaging, mail containment, brand protection and staff guidance.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Aldermoor Software is facing an extortion threat after a data theft claim. The CISO has sent one official staff notice. The company uses email authentication but allows look-alike domains to reach inboxes with a warning banner. Staff look to the CISO for instructions.

At 09:00 on Tuesday, 6,400 staff and 300 partners receive messages that appear to come from the CISO, in her writing style, giving contradictory instructions: reset passwords at a fake portal, disable endpoint protection for diagnostics, and ignore other emails. Replies from some staff contain credentials. A fake chat account in the CISO's name starts direct messages.

What the team does not yet know: how many staff clicked, whether look-alike domains passed filters, whether any admin credentials were given, and how to be sure staff trust the real notice.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize impersonation at scale. Participants spot messages and domains imitating the CISO.
  • Contain mail and accounts. The team purges messages and resets exposed credentials.
  • Re-establish authority. Engineers and communications issue a verified notice.
  • Protect partners. Participants alert partners and takedown teams.
  • Investigate exposure. The team counts clicks and credentials.
  • Improve resilience to impersonation. Participants review authenticated messaging and domain protection.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Purge the impersonation messages and reset exposed credentials before sending a correction, and send corrections only through a channel staff can verify.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Mass impersonation leads to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Messages purged and credentials reset.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Exposure is diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A verified communication plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Fixes proceed in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Staff confidence stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Controls are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes staff who can tell the real incident lead from an impersonator and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Email Security Engineering

  • Purges messages and blocks domains.
  • Resets credentials for those who entered them.
  • Reviews mail logs.
  • Plans domain monitoring.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Collaboration Platform Engineering

  • Disables the fake chat account.
  • Validates mail rules.
  • Checks endpoint protection status.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and communications define a verified channel.
  • The service desk logs reports and clicks.
  • Owners define staff guidance.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Counts recipients, clicks and credentials.
  • Searches for related domains.
  • Preserves evidence.
  • Adds detection for leader-style impersonation.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with the mail and chat providers and domain registrars.
  • Asks for takedowns.
  • Requests guidance on look-alike filtering.
  • Escalates through account teams.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for software company security operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 8.23 web filtering, 5.14 information transfer, 6.3 awareness, 8.7 malware protection; SOC 2 security).
  • Assesses communications integrity and phishing resilience.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Block look-alike domains and monitor new registrations that imitate leaders and the company.

Define one verified channel for leadership communications and train staff to use it.

Restrict chat account creation and verify identities.

Prepare templated corrections and partner contacts.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.23, 5.14, 6.3, 8.7) and SOC 2 expect email security and awareness evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on purge-first and a verified channel.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

  2. What did you purge first?
  3. How did you protect admins?

  5. How did you rebuild trust?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
