PBX SIP Trunk Provider BGP Route Leak and Isolation

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines the loss of voice service when the SIP trunk provider that carries the organization's inbound and outbound calls is hit by a BGP route leak. Prefixes announced incorrectly redirect or blackhole traffic across the internet, so SIP signaling and media between the organization's session border controllers (SBCs) and the provider fail. The PBX is healthy, but calls cannot be completed and the organization is effectively cut off from the telephone network.

Participants are working-level: telephony and SIP engineers, network and BGP operators, contact center supervisors, security operations and SBC administrators, the service desk and application owners, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise explores the difference between an internal fault and an upstream internet routing incident, and how limited the options are when the problem sits outside your network.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Everbright Insurance runs an on-premises PBX with two SBCs in the primary and secondary data centers. Calls reach the PSTN through SIP trunks from a single provider, VoiceLink, whose session controllers sit in two points of presence and are reached over a dedicated IP-VPN underlay and an internet-based backup using TLS and SRTP. The contact center handles 2,100 calls per hour at peak, including claims first notice of loss and roadside assistance.

At 08:41 on Tuesday, a mid-size transit network misconfigures its route filters and leaks a large set of prefixes, including those of VoiceLink's SBC addresses. Traffic bound for the provider's SIP endpoints is pulled through the leaker, which cannot handle it. SIP OPTIONS pings fail, TLS handshakes time out and media packets are dropped. By 08:50 all trunks are down on both SBCs. The IP-VPN underlay is also affected because the provider's edge routers rely on the same transit for part of the path. Inbound callers hear fast busy or ringing with no answer.

What the team does not yet know: the provider's status page shows green and its support line is saturated, an emergency call-forwarding option with a secondary carrier exists on paper but has never been tested, the organization's own edge routers show the provider's prefixes with an unexpected AS path, and the roadside assistance partner's call routing depends on the same provider.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize an upstream routing incident. Participants distinguish a PBX or SBC fault from a trunk, provider or internet routing issue using SIP OPTIONS, traceroutes, BGP looking glass data and AS path analysis.
  • Engage the provider and the wider ecosystem. The team escalates beyond the provider's support line, uses public routing data to confirm a leak and coordinates with other affected customers and the transit network.
  • Restore telephony through alternatives. Participants evaluate a secondary carrier, cloud calling, DID diversion and mobile forwarding, and what each takes to activate.
  • Protect the contact center and customers. The contact center prioritizes claims and emergency roadside calls, uses callback, web and SMS channels and publishes alternate numbers.
  • Apply defensive routing measures. The network team considers local mitigations: preferring alternate paths, filtering unexpected AS paths, announcing more-specific routes and relying on RPKI origin validation.
  • Improve voice resilience. The team examines single-provider dependence, failover testing, external reachability monitoring and contract commitments.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Do not assume the provider or the transit network will fix the leak quickly. Plan around what you control.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Trunk failures and contact center reports lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Fault isolation. The team determines the problem is outside the PBX and SBCs.

T+01:15 — Provider and routing confirmation. BGP analysis and provider engagement confirm a leak.

T+02:00 — Alternate voice paths. Secondary carrier and forwarding options are evaluated and activated.

T+03:00 — Contact center operations. Priority call handling and customer messaging are set.

T+04:30 — Routing mitigation and recovery. The leak is cleared or bypassed and trunks are re-established.

T+06:00 — Validation. Call quality, routing and capacity are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Normal call flow resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: All SIP trunks drop and calls cannot be completed

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:55. The telephony on-call engineer sees all trunks down on both SBCs. SIP OPTIONS pings to VoiceLink's two points of presence time out and TLS handshakes fail. The PBX is healthy and internal calls work. The contact center has 190 callers abandoning in the first minutes.

The network engineer runs traceroutes to the provider's SBC addresses and sees packets crossing an unfamiliar transit network and stopping. A public BGP looking glass shows the provider's prefixes originating from an unexpected autonomous system with more-specific announcements. The provider's status page shows green and its support line is busy.

The roadside assistance partner calls to say their customers cannot reach the number. Claims first notice of loss calls, which have regulatory timelines, are failing. The secondary carrier arrangement for emergency diversion exists on paper but has never been tested.

Facilitator provides: the SBC logs, the traceroute output, the looking glass view and the secondary carrier agreement summary.

  • Plan activation of the secondary trunk or diversion.

  • Prioritize claims and roadside calls.
  • Track abandoned calls.
Security Operations & SBC Administration
  • Assess whether it is a hijack and check TLS and SRTP.
  • Watch for toll fraud.
  • Preserve evidence.
Service Desk & Application Owners
  • Guide staff to mobile phones.
  • List voice-dependent systems.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Activate the continuity plan.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ask when failover was last tested.

Discussion questions

  1. What evidence shows the problem is outside your network, and how fast can you gather it?
  2. What can you do about a BGP leak that sits in neither your network nor your provider's?
  3. What does activating the secondary carrier require, and what has never been tested?
  4. How do you protect claims first notice of loss calls that carry regulatory timelines?
  5. Is this a leak or a hijack, and how would that change your response?
  6. How do you tell customers and partners the alternate way to reach you?
  7. What other systems use voice that you have not listed?
  8. Who in the provider organization can you escalate to beyond the support line?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the secondary carrier's untested status until telephony tries to plan activation.
  • If players wait for the provider status page to turn red, ask what independent evidence they have.
  • Look at whether teams use public routing tools such as looking glasses and route collectors.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, sets priority for claims first notice of loss and emergency calls and approves alternate voice paths.
  • Runs a 30-minute cadence, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency spend with the secondary carrier and activation of the untested forwarding.
  • Decides the public message about alternative contact methods.

Telephony & SIP Engineering

  • Examines SBC and PBX logs (SIP OPTIONS failures, TLS errors, registration state) to prove the PBX is healthy.
  • Prepares reconfiguration for the secondary trunk or cloud calling, including routing plans, codecs and capacity.
  • Arranges call forwarding or DID diversion with the secondary carrier.
  • Verifies emergency calling and caller ID requirements.

Network, BGP & Carrier Operations

  • Analyzes BGP tables, AS paths and looking glass data to confirm the leak against normal paths.
  • Applies local mitigations: prefer alternate paths, filter unexpected AS paths, announce more-specifics where appropriate.
  • Joins a call with the transit provider and the SIP provider's NOC and shares evidence.
  • Monitors for route stability before failback.

Contact Center Operations

  • Activates contact center contingency: callback queues, SMS and web intake and published alternate numbers.
  • Prioritizes claims and roadside calls and tracks abandoned calls.
  • Moves agents to alternate numbers or mobile softphones.
  • Plans backlog handling after recovery.

Security Operations & SBC Administration

  • Checks whether the leak is accidental or a hijack with malicious intent, since media interception is possible.
  • Verifies TLS and SRTP protections and watches for toll fraud and call redirection.
  • Reviews SBC access logs and considers blocking suspicious paths.
  • Preserves evidence.

Service Desk & Application Owners

  • The service desk guides staff to mobile phones and alternate numbers.
  • Application owners list voice-dependent systems such as MFA calls and alarm dialers.
  • Owners identify fallbacks.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates the continuity plan for claims and customer service and coordinates with the roadside assistance partner.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership and customers, including alternate numbers.
  • Coordinates with regulators if claims obligations are at risk.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 5.19-5.22 supplier services; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates regulatory and contractual obligations for claims handling timelines.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and provider communications.
  • Logs findings on single-provider dependence, untested failover and monitoring.

Resilience Architect Guidance

A single provider is a single point of failure. Contract a second carrier on diverse paths and test failover, including DID diversion and emergency numbers, at least twice a year.

Monitor reachability from the outside: probe provider SBC addresses with SIP OPTIONS from more than one network, watch AS paths and subscribe to routing security alerts. Know how to read public looking glasses.

Apply routing security: RPKI validation, strict filters and a working relationship with upstreams that can act quickly.

Plan contact center resilience: callback, SMS, web and mobile alternatives and pre-approved public messages with alternate numbers.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.19-5.22) and SOC 2 expect supplier risk reviews, tested failover and evidence of monitoring of critical services.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on the evidence that the fault was external and the readiness of the secondary carrier.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How long did it take to prove the problem was outside your network and what evidence did you use?
  2. Who monitors provider reachability and what alerts exist?
  3. How did the team use public BGP data and what skills were missing?
  4. What escalation path exists beyond the provider's support line?
  5. What did activating the secondary carrier require and what was untested?
  6. How were claims and emergency calls prioritized?
  7. What did customers and partners hear and was it accurate?
  8. How was media security assessed during the leak?
  9. How did you manage fraud risk?
  10. What contract terms apply to this outage?
  11. How was failback decided?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. Which voice-dependent systems were missed?
  14. What access or credentials were missing?
  15. What will you change about provider diversity?
