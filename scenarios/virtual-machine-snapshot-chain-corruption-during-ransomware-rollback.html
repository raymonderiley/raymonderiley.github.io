Virtual Machine Snapshot Chain Corruption During Ransomware Rollback

Ransomware & Cyber-Recovery Forensics

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines rollbacks of ransomware-hit virtual machines failing because long snapshot chains are corrupted, with some chains also containing the attacker's changes.

Participants are working-level: virtualization engineers, infrastructure operations engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and forensics analysts, the forensics and vendor support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of snapshot dependencies, consolidation risks, restore point selection and fallback to backups.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Wexford Distribution runs 600 virtual machines whose snapshots are used as informal restore points. Many machines carry chains of eight or more snapshots taken over months. Ransomware has encrypted about 180 machines.

At 08:00 on Tuesday, engineers start reverting to earlier snapshots. On 30 machines the revert fails with chain errors. On 12 machines the revert succeeds but the restored image shows the attacker's remote tool, because the snapshot predates the encryption but postdates the intrusion. A consolidation attempt on one machine fills the datastore.

What the team does not yet know: which snapshots are intact and clean, how much free datastore space remains, whether backups cover the failed machines, and the intrusion start date.

Exercise Objectives

  • Stop risky operations. Participants halt consolidations and bulk reverts.
  • Establish a clean point. The team determines the intrusion start and picks restore points before it.
  • Protect storage. Engineers manage datastore space and avoid chain damage.
  • Use backups. Participants fall back to backups for failed chains.
  • Prioritize recovery. The team restores in business priority order.
  • Improve snapshot practice. Participants review snapshot limits and backup coverage.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Halt consolidations and bulk reverts and pick a restore point from the intrusion timeline, not from convenience.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Failed reverts lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Risky operations halted and clean point sought.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Chain health and scope are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Recovery proceeds in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Platform stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Machines are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes rollbacks that restore working systems and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Virtualization Engineering

  • Halts consolidation and bulk reverts and frees datastore space safely.
  • Reviews chain health.
  • Plans snapshot limits.
  • Plans monitoring of datastore capacity.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Infrastructure Operations Engineering

  • Restores failed machines from backups.
  • Picks restore points before the intrusion date.
  • Validates restored machines.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and distribution leads define priority machines and manual procedures.
  • The service desk logs reports.
  • Owners define acceptable data loss.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Determines the intrusion start date.
  • Scans restored images for the attacker's tools.
  • Preserves evidence.
  • Adds alerts for long chains.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with the hypervisor and backup vendors and forensics firm.
  • Asks for chain repair guidance.
  • Requests capacity help.
  • Escalates through account teams.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for distribution operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 8.13 backup, 8.6 capacity management, 5.30 ICT readiness, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses recovery from snapshots.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Use snapshots for short-term changes only and keep real backups for recovery.

Limit chain depth and alert on datastore capacity.

Select restore points from the intrusion timeline and scan restored images.

Test reverts and consolidations before relying on them.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.13, 8.6, 5.30, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect backup and capacity evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on restore-point discipline.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. Why did you halt consolidation?
  2. How did you choose restore points?
  3. What happened with infected images?
  4. What did backups cover?
  5. How did you protect the datastore?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Forensics & Vendor Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
