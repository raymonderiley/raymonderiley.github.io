1. This Is a No-Fault Exercise

Read this section aloud at the start of the session.

This is a no-fault exercise. Nobody is being tested and nobody is being graded. Today's scenario is uncomfortable on purpose: in it, our own product is the reason our customers are under attack. We want your honest instincts, including the ones that put the company first and the ones that put customers first, because the tension between those two is exactly what we are here to examine. If you don't know who owns a decision, say so. That answer is a finding about our plans, not about you. Nothing said in this room will be attributed to an individual in the written report.

  • Findings are recorded against plans, decision rights and gaps, never against individuals.
  • There is no single correct answer to most of today's decisions. Reasoned disagreement is a useful result.
  • Answer as yourself, in your real role, based on what you would actually do.
  • Some decisions today could look different in a deposition, a regulator's office or a customer's boardroom. Say what you would really do, then we'll talk about how it would look.

2. How This Exercise Differs

Operation Pressure Point tested how leadership responds when the company is the victim. Operation Broken Chain turns that around. Here the company is both the victim and the source of harm: a compromised third-party build component has put malicious code into a routine software update, and the company has pushed that update to its own customers. Nothing is encrypted and nothing is down. The product works, which is the problem.

  • From availability to integrity. The issue is not getting systems back but whether anything we ship can be trusted.
  • From our losses to our customers' losses. Customers' environments, data and regulators are now part of our incident.
  • From ransom to silence. The extortion demand is not for decryption but for keeping quiet.
  • From one adversary to two pressures. The attacker is one; a vendor that may share responsibility, but does not want to be named, is another.

3. Objectives

  • Decide how the company behaves when it is both victim and vector, and whether leadership leans customer-first or company-first, deliberately or by default.
  • Confirm who has authority to disable our own product in customer environments, and the threshold for doing so.
  • Test whether and when we notify customers before the facts are confirmed, weighing duty of care against legal exposure.
  • Surface each leader's position on pure data extortion: paying for silence when nothing is encrypted.
  • Define our posture toward a third-party vendor that contributed to the incident: partner, adversary or both, and who decides.
  • Establish how customers can tell a genuine company message from an attacker's, when the attacker is impersonating us to them.
  • Agree what "safe again" means, who certifies it, and what we are willing to attest to customers and their regulators.
  • Capture outputs for the Crisis Decision Playbook: Supply Chain and Customer-Impact Edition (separate template).

Decisions This Exercise Forces

  • Inject 1, Is It Us? Do we disable our own product in every customer environment, and do we tell customers now or once we are sure? Also covers who leads the crisis team, escalation and making decisions without complete information.
  • Inject 2, The Price of Silence. An extortion group demands $750,000 in Monero not to publish our source code and a list of exposed customers. Also covers extortion governance, insurer involvement, counsel selection, and a vendor asking us not to name them.
  • Inject 3, Trust Is the Product. Attackers impersonate our security team and our CEO to customers. Also covers who speaks publicly, how customers verify us, reputational damage, and whether we pay for customers' remediation.
  • Inject 4, Rebuild or Patch Forward. A fast fix versus a full rebuild, while customers' regulators demand attestations from us. Also covers recovery plan approval, disclosure and materiality, and who certifies the product as safe.

Additional decisions added to the mix

  • Kill switch authority: who can turn off our product inside a customer's environment, and when?
  • Customer triage: which customers do we help first when we cannot help everyone at once (hospitals, banks, largest accounts)?
  • Customer remediation funding: do we pay for customers' incident response, and is that an admission of liability?
  • Vendor posture: do we name the vendor publicly, pursue legal action, or keep working with them to get the facts?
  • Pausing sales and releases: do we stop selling, stop all releases, or stop marketing while the incident is open?
  • Trading blackout: do we close the insider trading window for leaders and employees with knowledge of the incident?
  • Attestation: who signs a statement to a customer's regulator that our product is safe, and what evidence do they need?

4. Participants

Players

  • Chief Executive Officer (CEO)
  • Chief Financial Officer (CFO)
  • Chief Operating Officer (COO)
  • General Counsel (GC) / Chief Legal Officer
  • Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)
  • Chief Product Officer or Chief Technology Officer (owns the product and release pipeline)
  • Chief Customer Officer or Head of Customer Success
  • Chief Communications Officer or VP of Communications
  • Head of Third-Party Risk or Procurement (owns the vendor relationship)
  • Chief Risk Officer or Risk and Insurance Manager
  • Board liaison or Corporate Secretary (optional)

Exercise staff

  • Lead facilitator: runs the clock, delivers injects, directs questions to individuals.
  • Customer voice (optional but recommended): a staff member who plays the CIO of an affected hospital customer and "calls in" during Injects 1 and 3.
  • Scribe: records every decision, its owner, who agreed, who disagreed and every "we don't know."
  • Observer(s): watch for decision-rights confusion, customer-first versus company-first patterns, and groupthink.

6. Facilitator Guidance

The same rule applies as in every exercise in this series: individual answers first, then the cross-check. The facilitator chooses which questions to ask; the question bank in each inject is a menu, not a script.

The core technique: direct, then cross-check

  • Direct each question to one named leader and let them answer fully before anyone else speaks.
  • Cross-check with a second leader, usually the CEO or the leader whose function pays the price of the decision.
  • Invite the dissent: ask a third leader what concerns them about what they just heard.
  • The CEO speaks last on the core decisions.

Techniques specific to this scenario

  • Silent vote. Use index cards before the core decisions in Injects 1, 2 and 4. Read them aloud anonymously, then discuss the spread.
  • The customer in the room. Have the customer voice call in as the CIO of a hospital customer. Ask the leader who owns the relationship to respond live, in front of the room.
  • The deposition test. After a decision, ask the GC: "If this decision were read aloud in a deposition a year from now, how would it sound?" Then ask the Chief Customer Officer: "How would it sound to the customer?"
  • Company-first or customer-first. At the end of each inject, ask the observer to say which way the room leaned. Ask the CEO whether that was deliberate.
  • The empty chair. At least once, make the Chief Product Officer unreachable. Who decides on the kill switch?
  • Hold the line on details. Redirect technical debate: "Assume engineering is handling it. What do you need to decide?"

7. Scenario Background

Read aloud or send as a pre-read.

Halden Systems sells a cloud-based IT management platform to about 900 business customers, including regional banks, credit unions, healthcare clinics and hospitals, manufacturers and local governments. Customers install the Halden agent on their servers and laptops, about 310,000 devices in total. The agent patches software, monitors device health and runs scripts the customer's IT team schedules, so it runs with high privileges inside every customer environment. Updates to the agent are pushed automatically unless a customer opts out. Halden's subscription revenue is roughly $95M a year.

On Friday at 11:00 p.m., Halden released agent version 8.4.2, a routine maintenance update. By Saturday morning it had reached about 70% of customer devices. Halden's build pipeline uses a third-party component from ForgeLine, a vendor that provides build and code-signing tooling to hundreds of software companies.

It is Monday at 7:15 a.m. The security team at one of Halden's largest customers, a regional bank, has called to report that the Halden agent on their servers is making outbound connections to a domain nobody recognizes. Halden's security team has looked overnight and confirms that version 8.4.2 contains code Halden did not write. You have been called into an emergency meeting.

8. Inject 1: Is It Us?

Time in scenario: Monday, 9:30 a.m. (about 2 hours after the customer's call) | Facilitation time: 16 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • Version 8.4.2 contains a hidden component that can receive commands from an outside server. Security cannot yet say what commands have been sent or to how many customers.
  • Early evidence points to the ForgeLine build component, but this is not confirmed. ForgeLine has not responded to two calls.
  • Engineering can push an emergency command that disables the Halden agent on every customer device within an hour. It would stop the malicious code, and it would also stop patching, monitoring and scheduled scripts for every customer, including hospitals that use Halden to manage clinical workstations.
  • A second option is to revoke Halden's code-signing certificate, which blocks the bad version but also breaks every older version of the agent.
  • Three other customers have opened tickets about unusual outbound traffic. Nobody has told customers anything yet.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: our update contains malicious code, it is installed on roughly 217,000 customer devices, and we can switch the agent off.
  • Unknown: what the attacker has done with the access, whether older versions are affected, whether ForgeLine is the source, and what disabling the agent will break for customers.

Decisions to force

  • Do we disable our own product in every customer environment now? Who has the authority to pull that trigger?
  • Do we tell customers now, before we know what happened, or wait until we have facts?
  • Who leads the crisis management team when the incident is happening inside our customers' environments, not ours?
  • What is the escalation path: board, insurer, outside counsel, ForgeLine, law enforcement?

Silent vote

Before discussion, each leader writes on a card: Disable now / Disable after notifying customers / Don't disable yet, and Notify customers now / Notify when we know more.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the Chief Product Officer: You can switch off our product across 310,000 devices in an hour. Is that your call to make? What breaks for customers if you do?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: would you overrule a decision to disable? Would you overrule a decision not to?
  • Ask the CISO: Every hour the agent stays on, the attacker has privileged access to our customers' systems. How long can we wait for better information?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer: what does a hospital customer lose if the agent goes dark without warning?
  • Ask the General Counsel: If we tell customers now and some of what we say turns out to be wrong, what is our exposure? If we wait and they find out we knew, what is it then?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: which of those two risks are you more willing to carry?
  • Ask the Chief Customer Officer: A hospital CIO is on the phone and wants to know if they are safe. What do you say, right now?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC whether they would approve that answer, and the Chief Communications Officer whether it would survive being forwarded to a reporter.
  • Ask the CEO: Who runs this incident? The CISO, because it is a security event; the Chief Product Officer, because it is our product; or someone else?
    • Then cross-check: Ask each leader to name the crisis lead on a card without discussion. Record the spread.
  • Ask the Head of Third-Party Risk: ForgeLine isn't answering. What does our contract with them say about incident notification and cooperation, and who on our side owns that relationship today?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: do we go through their executive team, their lawyers or both?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • Customer voice: "This is the CIO at Riverside Health. Our nurses use workstations your agent manages. If you switch it off, will our clinical systems keep working?"
  • The Chief Product Officer is on a plane for the next four hours. Who authorizes the kill switch?

What good looks like (for observers)

  • A named leader with clear authority to disable the product, and a named alternate.
  • The decision to disable, or not, is made explicitly and weighs harm to customers on both sides, not only harm to the company.
  • Customers are told something early, even if it is only what we know, what we don't, and when we'll update them.
  • The crisis lead is named and is not also the person running the technical response.

9. Inject 2: The Price of Silence

Time in scenario: Tuesday, 11:00 a.m. (about 28 hours in) | Facilitation time: 16 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • The agent was disabled at 1:00 p.m. Monday. Customers were sent a short advisory at 3:00 p.m. Monday.
  • An extortion group has posted on a criminal forum claiming it holds Halden's full source code and a list of every customer still running 8.4.2, with instructions for exploiting them. It demands $750,000 in Monero within 48 hours, or it will publish everything. Nothing has been encrypted; this is purely a demand for silence.
  • Security confirms the customer list sample is genuine. About 60 customers have devices the kill switch did not reach because they were offline.
  • ForgeLine's General Counsel has sent a letter: they deny responsibility, say their own investigation is ongoing, and ask Halden not to name ForgeLine publicly while it continues.
  • The insurer has been notified. The policy includes a $500,000 cyber extortion sublimit. The insurer wants Halden to use its panel law firm and says publicly naming ForgeLine could complicate recovering costs from them later.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the demand, the deadline, that the customer list is real, that about 60 customers are still exposed.
  • Unknown: whether the group really has the source code, whether it is connected to the original attacker, whether paying would stop publication, and whether the group is a sanctioned party.

Decisions to force

  • Do we pay for silence when nothing is encrypted and paying cannot undo the compromise?
  • If we pay, on what terms? A single controlled channel through a professional negotiator? Would we pay in Monero, or insist on a currency that can be traced and screened?
  • Do we name ForgeLine publicly, keep quiet as they ask, or say nothing either way? Who decides?
  • Whose counsel do we use: the insurer's panel firm, our own outside counsel, or both?
  • What do we do for the 60 customers who are still exposed before the deadline?

Silent vote

Each leader writes on a card: Pay / Don't pay / Need more information, and Name ForgeLine / Don't name ForgeLine.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CEO: In Operation Pressure Point the ransom bought a decryption key. Here it buys only a promise not to publish. Does that change your answer?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO and the CISO separately whether they agree. Record both answers.
  • Ask the CISO: If the customer list is published, what happens to the 60 exposed customers? Can we protect them before the deadline without paying?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer: have we called those 60 customers personally? Who should?
  • Ask the General Counsel: The demand is in Monero, which is designed to be hard to trace. How do we screen the recipient against sanctions lists, and could we legally pay at all?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: will the insurer reimburse a payment we can't screen?
  • Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: The insurer wants its panel firm and wants us quiet about ForgeLine. Whose interests does that serve, ours or theirs?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: do we accept the panel firm, keep our own counsel, or both? Who makes that call?
  • Ask the Chief Communications Officer: ForgeLine asks us not to name them. Our customers want to know how this happened. What do we say about the cause?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Head of Third-Party Risk: if we name them, can we still get the cooperation we need from them? Ask the CEO to decide.
  • Ask the CFO: The extortion sublimit is $500,000 and the demand is $750,000. If we pay, where does the other $250,000 come from, and who approves it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the board liaison: does the board expect to approve any extortion payment, regardless of amount?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The group publishes a 10-customer sample early to prove it is serious. Two of those customers are banks.
  • A customer's general counsel emails asking whether Halden intends to pay, and says their board will "draw conclusions" from the answer.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • Leaders separate the extortion decision from the duty to protect exposed customers. Protecting customers starts regardless of the decision on payment.
  • Sanctions screening, insurer requirements and board approval are treated as conditions of any payment.
  • The decision about naming the vendor has a named owner and weighs customer transparency, legal recovery and investigative cooperation.
  • The choice of counsel is deliberate and protects the company's privilege and interests, not only the insurer's.

10. Inject 3: Trust Is the Product

Time in scenario: Wednesday, 2:00 p.m. (about 55 hours in) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • Customers have received emails from "Halden Security Response" containing a remediation tool to clean up 8.4.2. Halden did not send them. The tool is malware. Two customers have already run it.
  • A short video of Halden's CEO is circulating on social media, saying the problem is fully fixed and customers can turn the agent back on. The CEO never recorded it.
  • A national technology publication is running a story at 6:00 p.m. and wants comment. A trade journal for community banks is asking separately.
  • A competitor is emailing Halden customers offering free migration and six months at no cost.
  • The CIO of Riverside Health has posted publicly that Halden "left hospitals blind for two days without a phone call."
  • Customer Success has a list of 140 customers asking whether Halden will pay for their own incident response costs.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: someone is impersonating our security team and our CEO to our customers, the media is about to publish, and customers want compensation.
  • Unknown: how many customers received the fake email, whether the impersonators are the original attacker, and how many customers are considering leaving.

Decisions to force

  • Who speaks publicly for Halden, and what do they say by 6:00 p.m.?
  • How does a customer know a message is really from us when the attacker is copying our name, our security team and our CEO?
  • Do we pay for customers' incident response costs, offer credits, or neither? Is an offer an admission of liability?
  • How do we respond to public criticism from a customer, and to a competitor targeting our customers?

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the Chief Communications Officer: Who is our spokesperson today? Is it the CEO, the CISO or you? What is the one sentence we give the publication by 6:00 p.m.?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO whether you agree you should or should not be the face of this. Ask the GC whether you would approve that sentence.
  • Ask the CISO: The attacker is sending tools in our name. What single, simple rule can we give every customer so they can tell a real message from a fake one?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer: can every customer success manager explain that rule today? Ask the Communications Officer: how do we get it to customers through a channel the attacker can't fake?
  • Ask the CEO: A video of you is telling customers the problem is fixed. What do you personally do in the next hour?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Communications Officer: do we respond to the fake video publicly, or would that spread it further?
  • Ask the CFO: 140 customers want us to pay for their incident response. What would that cost, and who has authority to say yes?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: is paying an admission of liability? Ask the Chief Customer Officer: what happens to retention if we say no?
  • Ask the Chief Customer Officer: Riverside Health's CIO is criticizing us publicly. Who calls them, and what do we offer?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: should that call come from you?
  • Ask the Chief Operating Officer: A competitor is offering our customers free migration. Do we pause sales and marketing while this is open? Do we respond to the competitor at all?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: what does a pause cost us this quarter?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • Customer voice: "Your account manager just called offering us a discount. We don't want a discount. We want to know if our patient data was touched."
  • An employee posts on a public forum that leadership "knew about the ForgeLine problem weeks ago." It isn't true, but it is spreading.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • One spokesperson and one approval path, with legal review built in.
  • A single customer verification rule, for example: we will never send a tool by email, and every advisory is posted on one official status page.
  • The compensation decision has a named owner, a defined budget limit and legal input on how it is framed.
  • Affected customers are treated as partners in recovery, not only as a retention risk.

11. Inject 4: Rebuild or Patch Forward

Time in scenario: Thursday, 9:00 a.m. (about 74 hours in) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • Engineering presents two recovery options:
  • Patch forward: a clean version 8.4.3 in two days, built on the existing pipeline after it is cleaned and the ForgeLine component is removed. Engineering's confidence is moderate. Customers turn the agent back on automatically.
  • Rebuild: a new build pipeline, new code-signing keys and a full code review, ready in 12 days. Customers must reinstall the agent manually on about 310,000 devices. Engineering's confidence is high.
  • Two banking customers say their regulators, as part of third-party risk oversight, have asked for a written attestation from Halden that the product is safe, within five business days.
  • Forensics now shows the attacker used the agent to copy files from 41 customer environments, including some personal data. Several customers face their own 72-hour regulatory notification clocks and want Halden's facts today.
  • The CFO estimates customer credits, lost renewals and response costs could reach $14M, about 15% of annual revenue. The board chair has requested a special meeting.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the two recovery options, the attestation request, that data was copied from 41 customers, the estimated financial impact.
  • Unknown: whether older versions of the agent are clean, whether the patch-forward pipeline is fully trustworthy, and how many customers will leave.

Decisions to force

  • Do we approve patch forward or rebuild? Who signs off on the recovery plan and the risk it carries?
  • Who certifies that our product is safe, and what evidence must they see before they sign an attestation to a customer's regulator?
  • What do we give the 41 customers whose data was copied, so they can meet their own regulatory deadlines?
  • Is this material? What does the board need to hear, and does this trigger disclosure to investors or lenders?

Silent vote

Each leader writes on a card: Patch forward / Rebuild, and the name of the person who should sign the attestation.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the Chief Product Officer: Patch forward or rebuild? If you choose patch forward and it turns out the pipeline wasn't clean, what happens?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CISO: would you put your name on the patch-forward option? Ask the Chief Customer Officer: can customers absorb a 12-day wait and a manual reinstall?
  • Ask the CEO: Who signs the attestation to the customers' regulators: you, the CISO or the Chief Product Officer? What would you need to see before signing it yourself?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: what wording would you approve, and what must it never say?
  • Ask the General Counsel: Forty-one customers need facts for their own regulators within 72 hours. What do we give them, and how do we keep it consistent with what we tell everyone else?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CISO: what can we state as confirmed today, and what is still under investigation?
  • Ask the CFO: Is $14M material? Who makes that determination, and when does the board need it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the board liaison: what decision does the board want to make at the special meeting, and what does it want management to decide without it?
  • Ask the COO: When customers turn the agent back on, which ones go first? Do hospitals and banks get priority, or our largest accounts?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer and the CFO separately. Do their answers match?
  • Ask the Head of Third-Party Risk: ForgeLine's component is in our pipeline. What other third-party components could do the same thing, and who knew they were there?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: does this change what we require from vendors, and who owns that change?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • ForgeLine announces publicly that it was breached and that "a small number of software customers" were affected. It does not name Halden. Does that change our disclosure?
  • A major customer says it will stay only if Halden chooses the rebuild option and pays for an independent code review.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • The recovery choice is a documented business decision with a named approver who accepts the risk explicitly.
  • The attestation is signed by someone with the authority and the evidence to sign it, and its wording separates confirmed facts from ongoing investigation.
  • The company supports customers' own regulatory deadlines with consistent facts.
  • Materiality is assessed by a defined group with a documented rationale, and the board's role is clear.

12. Debrief

Allow at least 18 minutes. Keep the no-fault tone. This scenario raises harder questions about responsibility than a ransomware exercise, so remind the room that findings are about plans and decision rights, not individuals.

How to run it

  • Go around the room first, starting with the most junior leader and finishing with the CEO.
  • Revisit the silent votes, especially kill switch, pay for silence, and patch forward versus rebuild.
  • Ask the observer to report whether the room leaned customer-first or company-first in each inject.
  • Name an owner and date for every gap, and map each decision into the playbook as it is captured.

The three core questions

1. What decisions did we struggle with?

  • Which decision took longest, or was never made?
  • Where did two leaders believe they owned the same decision, such as the kill switch or the attestation?
  • Where did our duty to customers and our need to protect the company pull in different directions, and how did we resolve it?

2. What information did we need but didn't have?

  • Did we know which customers depend on our product for safety-critical or regulated work?
  • Did we know what our vendor contracts require of ForgeLine, and of us, during an incident?
  • Did we know what our insurance covers for extortion, customer claims and dependent business interruption?

3. What should we formalize before a real event?

  • Who can disable our product in customer environments, and under what conditions?
  • Our threshold for telling customers before the facts are confirmed.
  • Our position on paying for silence, and how we would protect customers regardless.
  • How customers verify official Halden communications.

Additional debrief questions for this scenario

  • Were we customer-first or company-first? Was that a deliberate choice, and would we make the same choice again?
  • How did the vendor's role change our decisions? Did it make us slower, more cautious or more defensive?
  • What would our most affected customer say about how we treated them in the first 24 hours?
  • Which commitments did we make to customers that we may not be able to keep?
  • Who certified the product as safe, and on what evidence? Would that person sign again?
  • Which third parties in our own supply chain could put us in this position tomorrow, and does anyone own that list?
  • Where did fear of liability delay a decision that customers needed us to make?
  • On a scale of 1 to 5, how confident are you that we could handle an incident where we are the source of harm? Ask each leader individually.
  • What is the single most important action we should take in the next 30 days?

Closing statement (read aloud)

Thank you. Today we looked at the hardest kind of incident for a software company: one where our customers are harmed through something we sent them. Every gap we found is one we won't have to discover in a real event. Nothing said here will be attributed to individuals. We will circulate a summary of findings, the decisions we captured and the draft supply chain edition of the Crisis Decision Playbook within five business days, with an owner and a date for every action.

13. After-Action Outputs

  • After-action summary within five business days: decisions made, decisions not made, customer-first versus company-first observations, and confidence scores.
  • Action register: every gap with an owner, target date and status.
  • Draft Crisis Decision Playbook (Supply Chain and Customer-Impact Edition) populated with the owners, thresholds and verification rules agreed today.
  • Third-party dependency follow-up: a list of critical build and delivery vendors, with owners, for review by third-party risk.
  • Parking lot: technical items raised during the session, routed to the right team.

Appendix B: Observer Checklist

  • Did leaders answer individually, or defer to the most senior person?
  • Was the kill switch decision owned by one named leader?
  • Did leaders weigh harm to customers on both sides of each decision?
  • Were customers told something early, even with incomplete facts?
  • Was the vendor's role handled deliberately, with a named owner?
  • Was a concrete verification rule set for customer communications?
  • Was the person who certified the product as safe also the person with the evidence?
  • Did fear of liability visibly slow decisions? Where?

Facilitator Notes
