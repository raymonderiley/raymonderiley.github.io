Crisis Decision Playbook: Dark Region Edition Template

1. This Is a No-Fault Exercise

Read this section aloud at the start of the session.

This is a no-fault exercise. Nobody is being tested and nobody is being graded. There is no attacker in today's scenario. Our cloud provider has a long outage, and every decision we make is about waiting, moving or spending, without knowing when it will end. Many of the gaps we find today will be the result of design and investment choices made years ago, in good faith. They are not anyone's fault. If you don't know who owns a decision or what our limits are, say so. That is exactly what we want to find. Nothing said in this room will be attributed to an individual.

  • Findings are recorded against plans, design choices and decision rights, never against individuals.
  • Past architecture and budget decisions are context, not blame.
  • Answer as yourself, in your real role, based on what you would actually do.
  • Disagreement is useful. It shows where our tolerances have never been agreed.

2. How This Exercise Differs

The earlier exercises in this series had an adversary: ransomware, a compromised supply chain, an insider. Operation Dark Region has none. A major cloud provider region fails for days during the busiest week of the year. Nothing is stolen and nothing is encrypted. The decisions are about how much disruption we are willing to accept, how much data we are willing to lose, how we share scarce capacity, and whether we are prepared to change how we are built.

  • No adversary, no ransom, no investigation. The threat is time and uncertainty.
  • The provider controls the clock. Their status page says "investigating" and gives no time for recovery.
  • Recovery has a price tag. Failing over loses data and money; waiting loses customers and revenue.
  • Scarcity forces choices. Everyone else on the same provider is trying to move at the same time.
  • The real question comes last. Leadership must decide how much resilience it is willing to pay for.

3. Objectives

  • Confirm who has authority to fail over to our disaster recovery region and to accept the data loss that comes with it.
  • Agree on our impact tolerance: the longest outage and the most data loss we can accept for our most important services.
  • Test how leadership prioritizes customers and services when recovery capacity is limited, and whether those priorities exist in writing.
  • Decide when to return to normal operations (failback) and who accepts that risk during a peak trading period.
  • Determine our contractual and public stance when the outage is the provider's fault: SLA credits, force majeure and attribution.
  • Test emergency spending authority and how staff fatigue is managed during a multi-day event.
  • Decide how much concentration risk we are willing to carry, and whether to fund multi-region or multi-provider resilience.
  • Capture outputs for the Crisis Decision Playbook: Extended Outage and Concentration Risk Edition (separate template).

Decisions This Exercise Forces

  • Inject 1, No ETA. The provider's primary region is down and won't say when it will be back. Fail over now and lose data, or wait? Also covers business continuity plan activation, who leads, escalation, and communicating when it's not our fault.
  • Inject 2, Everyone Is Leaving at Once. Disaster recovery capacity is scarce. Which customers and services come back first? Also covers emergency spend, a customer offering to pay for priority, impersonation of the provider and staff fatigue.
  • Inject 3, Whose Fault Is It? The region is partly back on the eve of Black Friday. Fail back now or stay put? Also covers SLA credits, force majeure, who speaks publicly, blaming the provider, and regulatory reporting.
  • Inject 4, Never Again? A week later, the board wants answers. Also covers impact tolerance, a $9M-a-year resilience investment, reconciling lost transactions, insurance and renegotiating the provider contract.

Additional decisions added to the mix

  • Accepting data loss: who can approve losing up to 40 minutes of transactions, and how are affected customers told?
  • Paid priority: do we let a large customer pay to be restored first?
  • Change freeze: do we stop all non-recovery changes, including planned holiday releases?
  • Staff fatigue: who can order engineers to stand down, even mid-recovery?
  • Attribution: do we name the provider publicly as the cause?
  • Resilience investment: what level of outage are we prepared to pay to avoid?

4. Participants

Players

  • Chief Executive Officer (CEO)
  • Chief Financial Officer (CFO)
  • Chief Operating Officer (COO)
  • General Counsel (GC) / Chief Legal Officer
  • Chief Technology Officer (CTO) or Chief Information Officer, who owns the platform and the cloud relationship
  • Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)
  • Chief Customer Officer or Head of Customer Success
  • Chief Revenue Officer or Head of Sales
  • Chief Communications Officer or VP of Communications
  • Chief Human Resources Officer (CHRO), for staff fatigue and welfare
  • Chief Risk Officer or Risk and Insurance Manager
  • Board liaison (optional)

Exercise staff

  • Lead facilitator: runs the clock, delivers injects, directs questions to individuals.
  • Provider voice (optional): a staff member who plays the cloud provider's account manager and gives only vague updates.
  • Scribe: records each decision, its owner, who agreed and who disagreed.
  • Observer(s): watch for decision-rights confusion, untested assumptions about recovery and groupthink.

6. Facilitator Guidance

The series rule still applies: individual answers first, then the cross-check. Choose questions from each inject's bank; it is a menu, not a script.

The core technique: direct, then cross-check

  • Direct each question to one named leader and let them answer fully before anyone else speaks.
  • Cross-check with a second leader, usually the CEO or the leader whose function carries the consequence.
  • Invite the dissent: ask a third leader what concerns them about what they just heard.
  • The CEO speaks last on the core decisions.

Techniques specific to this scenario

  • Silent vote. Use index cards before the core decisions in Injects 1, 3 and 4.
  • Write the number. Before discussing impact tolerance, ask every leader to write down the longest outage they believe our customers would tolerate. Compare the numbers.
  • The vague provider. Have the provider voice give answers like "engineers are working on it" and "we expect progress soon." Watch whether leaders plan around hope.
  • The clock keeps running. Announce lost revenue every few minutes during Inject 1 ("Another $180,000 lost").
  • The tired engineer. In Inject 2, read a message from an engineer who has been working for 22 hours. Ask the CHRO and the CTO what happens next.
  • Hold the line on details. Redirect technical debate: "Assume engineering can do it. What do you need to decide?"

7. Scenario Background

Read aloud or send as a pre-read.

The company runs online storefronts, checkout and payment processing for about 3,100 retailers, from small independent shops to several national brands. During the holiday season, the company processes up to $45M in sales per day and earns a percentage of every transaction. About 40% of its annual revenue is earned between Thanksgiving and New Year.

The company runs on one major cloud provider. Its production platform sits in a single primary region. A disaster recovery region exists, with data copied every few minutes, but the last full failover test was 18 months ago, and the recovery region is sized to run about 60% of normal traffic. The company's payment service is licensed in two countries, and its retailer contracts include a 99.95% availability commitment with service credits.

It is the Tuesday before Thanksgiving, 6:10 a.m. The provider's primary region has suffered a major failure: a power and cooling incident followed by problems with the provider's own management systems. The company's storefronts and checkout are down for every retailer. The provider's status page says "investigating." You have been called into an emergency meeting.

8. Inject 1: No ETA

Time in scenario: Tuesday, 9:00 a.m. (about 3 hours into the outage) | Facilitation time: 16 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • Every storefront and checkout is down. The company is losing about $180,000 in fees per hour, and retailers are losing about $1.9M in sales per hour.
  • The provider's account manager says engineers are "making progress" but will not give a recovery time. Industry chatter suggests it could be anywhere from hours to days.
  • Engineering can fail over to the disaster recovery region in about six hours. Because of how data is copied, up to 40 minutes of transactions from before the failure may be lost or need to be rebuilt by hand. The recovery region can carry only about 60% of normal traffic.
  • The failover procedure hasn't been fully tested for 18 months. Engineering gives it a 75% chance of working the first time.
  • Once we fail over, coming back to the primary region (failback) takes another 8 to 12 hours of planned downtime.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: we are fully down, we are losing money every hour, failover is possible but costly, and we have done it before, though not recently.
  • Unknown: when the provider will recover, whether the failover will work first time, and exactly which transactions would be lost.

Decisions to force

  • Do we fail over now, accepting lost transactions and reduced capacity, or wait for the provider?
  • Who has the authority to make that call and to accept the data loss?
  • Do we activate the business continuity plan, and what does that mean for retailers who can't take orders?
  • What do we tell retailers when we don't know the recovery time and the cause is the provider's?

Silent vote

Each leader writes on a card: Fail over now / Wait __ more hours, then fail over / Wait for the provider.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CTO: Six hours to fail over, a 75% chance it works the first time, and up to 40 minutes of lost transactions. Do you recommend we go? Whose decision is it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: do you agree it is the CTO's call? Would you overrule it?
  • Ask the CFO: Every hour of waiting costs $180,000 in fees and much more for our retailers. At what point does waiting become the more expensive choice?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CTO: does the cost of a failed failover change that math?
  • Ask the Chief Customer Officer: If we fail over, some retailers lose orders that customers think they've already placed. Which is worse for retailers: more downtime, or lost orders they have to fix?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: what do our contracts say about data loss compared with downtime?
  • Ask the CEO: Have we ever agreed how long we are willing to be down before we fail over? If not, what is the number today?
    • Then cross-check: Ask each leader to write their number on a card. Compare the spread.
  • Ask the Chief Communications Officer: Retailers are calling. What do we tell them about the cause, the recovery time and our plan, when we don't know the recovery time?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC whether you would approve naming the provider as the cause in that message.
  • Ask the COO: Who leads the crisis team for a multi-day event with no attacker, and how do we run it in shifts?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CHRO: who decides when a leader needs to hand over and rest?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • Provider voice: "We're seeing positive signs. We'd strongly recommend not making any big moves for the next couple of hours." Does that change the decision?
  • Halfway through the failover, a database copy step fails. Engineering needs another four hours. Do we push on or abort?

What good looks like (for observers)

  • A named leader with authority to fail over, and an agreed threshold or a decision time, rather than waiting indefinitely.
  • Data loss is explicitly accepted by someone with the authority to accept it.
  • Communication to retailers is honest about what is unknown and gives a time for the next update.
  • The crisis lead plans for a multi-day event, including shifts and handovers.

9. Inject 2: Everyone Is Leaving at Once

Time in scenario: Tuesday, 8:00 p.m. (about 14 hours in) | Facilitation time: 16 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • The failover was approved at 10:00 a.m. and the recovery region is partly running. Thousands of the provider's other customers are moving to the same region, and the provider is rationing new computing capacity.
  • the company can run only about 45% of normal traffic. Engineering needs leadership to decide which retailers and which services come back first.
  • A national retailer that makes up 9% of the company's revenue offers to pay $250,000 for guaranteed priority. Several small retailers have posted that they will go out of business if they miss this weekend.
  • The provider offers reserved capacity for $2.1M for the next two weeks, with payment needed tonight. This is above any executive's normal spending authority.
  • An email that appears to come from the provider's support team offers "priority capacity" if the company pays through a payment link. It is a scam.
  • Several engineers have been working for 22 hours straight. One has just made an error that took a service back down for 30 minutes.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: we can run less than half our traffic, capacity can be bought, a large retailer wants to pay for priority, and the team is exhausted.
  • Unknown: when the primary region will return, whether more capacity will become available without paying, and how many retailers will leave.

Decisions to force

  • Which retailers and services come back first, and who decides?
  • Do we accept paid priority from a large retailer?
  • Do we spend $2.1M tonight on reserved capacity, and who can authorize it?
  • Who can order engineers to stop and rest, even if it slows recovery?

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the Chief Revenue Officer: We can bring back less than half our retailers. Do we restore by revenue, by risk of going out of business, or by contract terms?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer: is that order fair? Ask the CEO: is it written down anywhere today?
  • Ask the CEO: A retailer offers $250,000 for priority. Do we take it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: does accepting create obligations to everyone else? Ask the Chief Communications Officer: how would it look if it became public?
  • Ask the CFO: $2.1M tonight for capacity. Can you approve that alone? If not, who can, and can we reach them tonight?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the board liaison: does the board have an emergency approval process, and has anyone ever used it?
  • Ask the CHRO: Engineers have worked 22 hours and one has just made a costly mistake. Who has the authority to send people home?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CTO: can recovery continue if half the team rests for 8 hours? What do you need from leadership?
  • Ask the CISO: An email offering paid priority, supposedly from the provider, is a scam. How does anyone in finance or engineering know which provider messages are real?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: what stops someone approving a payment from that email tonight?
  • Ask the Chief Customer Officer: Small retailers are posting that they'll close if they miss this weekend. What do we say to them?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO whether you would make a public commitment to small retailers.

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The payments service and the storefront service are competing for the same capacity. Without payments, storefronts can show products but can't take money. Which comes first?
  • A partner offers to host a basic checkout page for retailers for free on a different cloud provider, but it has never been security-reviewed.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • A clear prioritization of services and retailers, decided by a named leader on stated principles.
  • A deliberate position on paid priority, with legal and reputational implications considered.
  • Emergency spending authority exists and can be used at night, or the gap is recorded.
  • Staff welfare is treated as a recovery risk, with a named person who can order rest.
  • Provider communications are verified before any payment or action.

10. Inject 3: Whose Fault Is It?

Time in scenario: Thursday, Thanksgiving Day, 4:00 p.m. (about 58 hours in) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • The provider says its primary region is "substantially recovered." The company is still in the recovery region at about 70% of normal capacity.
  • Failing back to the primary region would need 8 to 12 hours of planned downtime, which would end just as Black Friday begins. Staying in the recovery region means running at reduced capacity through the biggest sales weekend of the year.
  • Retailers are demanding service credits. Under the 99.95% commitment, credits could total about $3.8M. The GC believes the contracts' force majeure clause might excuse the company, but invoking it would anger retailers.
  • A national news outlet is running a story titled "Payments Platform Down for Thanksgiving." The provider has asked customers not to discuss the cause until its own report is published.
  • Financial regulators in both countries where the company's payment service is licensed have asked for an incident report within 72 hours of the start of the outage. That deadline is tomorrow morning.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the primary region is mostly back, failback would collide with Black Friday, credits are owed or disputed, and regulators want a report tomorrow.
  • Unknown: whether the primary region is truly stable, the provider's final explanation of the cause, and how retailers will react to force majeure.

Decisions to force

  • Do we fail back now and risk downtime at the start of Black Friday, or stay in the recovery region at reduced capacity?
  • Do we pay service credits, invoke force majeure, or something in between? Who decides?
  • Do we name the provider publicly as the cause, despite their request?
  • What goes in the regulatory report when we don't yet have the provider's explanation?
  • Who speaks publicly for the company?

Silent vote

Each leader writes on a card: Fail back now / Stay in recovery region through the weekend, and Pay credits / Invoke force majeure / Something else.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CTO: Fail back now or stay put through the weekend? What is the risk of each?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Revenue Officer: what does running at 70% cost us over Black Friday weekend? Ask the CEO to decide.
  • Ask the General Counsel: Force majeure might excuse $3.8M in credits. Do you recommend invoking it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer: what happens to retailer relationships if we do? Ask the CFO: what happens to the quarter if we don't?
  • Ask the Chief Communications Officer: The provider asked us not to discuss the cause. Our retailers and the press are asking. Do we name them?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CTO: does naming them affect our relationship with the provider while we still depend on them? Ask the CEO to decide.
  • Ask the COO: Regulators want a report tomorrow. What do we say about the cause when the provider hasn't published its findings?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: who signs the report, and what must it never say?
  • Ask the CEO: Do you personally speak publicly about this outage, or does someone else?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Communications Officer whether you agree.
  • Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: Does our insurance cover losses from a provider outage? Is there a waiting period, and have we started the clock on a claim?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: is the potential recovery large enough to matter to this decision?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The provider's primary region has a brief 20-minute wobble at 7:00 p.m. Does that change the failback decision?
  • A large retailer publicly announces it is moving to a competitor that "stayed up all week" because it runs across two providers.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • The failback decision is made deliberately by a named leader, weighing technical and commercial risk.
  • The credits versus force majeure decision considers long-term relationships, not only short-term cost.
  • Attribution to the provider is decided deliberately, with legal and relationship implications understood.
  • The regulatory report separates confirmed facts from what is still unknown, and commits to an update.

11. Inject 4: Never Again?

Time in scenario: the following Wednesday, at a special board meeting (8 days after the outage began) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • the company failed back to the primary region on Monday night. Total outage and reduced-capacity time was about 4 days.
  • Estimated impact: $11M in lost fees, credits and response costs, about 6% of annual revenue. Seven retailers, about 4% of revenue, have given notice to leave.
  • About 12,000 orders from the 40 minutes of lost transactions were shipped by retailers but never paid for. Retailers want the company to cover the loss.
  • Engineering presents two options. Option A: active-active across two regions of the same provider, about $4M a year, recovering in minutes for most failures but not for a provider-wide problem. Option B: multi-provider, about $9M a year plus an 18-month program, protecting against a single provider's failure.
  • The board chair asks: "What is the longest outage this company is prepared to accept, and why didn't we know that number before last week?"

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the impact, the lost retailers, the unpaid orders and the cost of each resilience option.
  • Unknown: whether this kind of outage will happen again, whether more retailers will leave, and how much insurance will pay.

Decisions to force

  • What is our impact tolerance: the longest outage and the most data loss we will accept for our most important services?
  • Option A, Option B, or neither? Who owns the recommendation to the board?
  • Do we cover the 12,000 unpaid orders for retailers, and how much of the cost do we absorb?
  • Do we renegotiate the provider contract, pursue compensation, or both?

Silent vote

Each leader writes on a card: the longest acceptable outage for checkout and payments, and Option A / Option B / Neither.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CEO: The board asks for our impact tolerance. What number do you give them for checkout and payments?
    • Then cross-check: Read aloud the numbers each leader wrote on their cards. Ask the CFO and the CTO whether they can deliver against the CEO's number.
  • Ask the CTO: Option A or Option B? What does each protect us from, and what does it leave exposed?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: can we afford Option B? Ask the Chief Risk Officer: can we afford not to?
  • Ask the CFO: Retailers want us to cover 12,000 unpaid orders. What does it cost, and do we pay?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer: what happens to retention if we don't? Ask the GC: are we obligated to?
  • Ask the Chief Risk Officer: Did our risk register ever say that one provider region was a single point of failure? Who accepted that risk, and when?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the board liaison: does the board believe it accepted that risk?
  • Ask the General Counsel: What can we recover from the provider, and do we push for it while we still depend on them?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CTO: what would we ask for in a renegotiated contract?
  • Ask the Chief Customer Officer: Seven retailers have given notice. What would it take to keep them, and is it worth it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Revenue Officer whether you agree with that assessment.

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • A board member says: "This was a once-in-a-decade event. Why spend $9M a year on it?" Who answers?
  • Regulators in one country write that they expect licensed payment providers to show they can recover critical services within a defined time, and ask for the company's plan within 60 days.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • Leadership commits to an impact tolerance, or explicitly assigns someone to bring one to the board by a date.
  • The investment choice is tied to the impact tolerance, not to the memory of last week.
  • Concentration risk is recognized as a risk the board must accept or reduce, not something technology decides alone.
  • Commitments to retailers are deliberate and affordable.

12. Debrief

Allow at least 18 minutes. Remind the room that most of today's gaps come from design and budget choices made in good faith, and that findings are about plans, tolerances and decision rights.

How to run it

  • Go around the room first, starting with the most junior leader and finishing with the CEO.
  • Revisit the written numbers: the outage leaders said customers would tolerate in Inject 1, and the impact tolerance in Inject 4. How far apart were they?
  • Name an owner and a date for every gap, and map each decision into the playbook as it is captured.

The three core questions

1. What decisions did we struggle with?

  • Which decision took longest: failover, prioritization, failback or investment?
  • Where did we wait for the provider instead of deciding for ourselves?
  • Which decisions were really about money, and which were really about trust?

2. What information did we need but didn't have?

  • Did we know how long our recovery takes, and how much data we would lose, from a recent test rather than an estimate?
  • Did we know which retailers and services matter most, in writing?
  • Did we know what our contracts, insurance and regulators require during a provider outage?

3. What should we formalize before a real event?

  • Our impact tolerance for each critical service.
  • Who can fail over, accept data loss and authorize emergency spend, including at night and on holidays.
  • Our restoration priority order for services and customers.
  • Our position on paid priority, service credits and force majeure.

Additional debrief questions for this scenario

  • Did we plan around hope? Where did we wait because the provider said "soon"?
  • When was our failover last really tested, and what would we do differently if it failed halfway?
  • How did we treat small customers compared with large ones? Are we comfortable with that?
  • Who protected our people from exhaustion, and should that be someone's explicit job?
  • Is concentration risk on our risk register, and has the board ever consciously accepted it?
  • Would we make the same investment decision if this outage had happened in February instead of Thanksgiving week?
  • On a scale of 1 to 5, how confident are you that we could survive a four-day provider outage? Ask each leader individually.
  • What is the single most important action we should take in the next 30 days?

Closing statement (read aloud)

Thank you. Today there was no attacker, only a long outage, a busy week and choices about time, money and fairness. The gaps we found are the result of choices made in good faith, and now we can make them deliberately. Nothing said here will be attributed to individuals. We will circulate the findings, the decisions we captured and the draft Extended Outage and Concentration Risk Edition of the Crisis Decision Playbook within five business days, with an owner and a date for every action.

13. After-Action Outputs

  • After-action summary within five business days: decisions made, decisions not made, and confidence scores.
  • Action register: every gap with an owner, target date and status.
  • Draft Crisis Decision Playbook (Extended Outage and Concentration Risk Edition) populated with the authorities, priorities and limits agreed today.
  • Impact tolerance proposal for each critical service, for board approval.
  • Failover test plan: a full, timed failover test of the recovery region within 90 days.
  • Concentration risk entry in the risk register, with an owner and a board-level decision.

Appendix B: Observer Checklist

  • Did leaders answer individually, or defer to the most senior person?
  • Did anyone set a time limit on waiting for the provider?
  • Was data loss explicitly accepted by someone with the authority to accept it?
  • Was there a written restoration priority, or was it invented in the room?
  • Could emergency spend be approved at night or on a holiday?
  • Was staff fatigue treated as a recovery risk?
  • Were provider messages verified before any payment?
  • Did leadership commit to an impact tolerance, or defer it?
