Crisis Decision Playbook Template

1. This Is a No-Fault Exercise

Read this section aloud at the start of the session. It sets the tone for everything that follows.

This is a no-fault exercise. We are not testing people, and nobody is being graded. We are testing our plans, our decision rights and our assumptions. There are no wrong answers today, only useful ones. If you do not know the answer, saying "I don't know" or "I'm not sure who owns that" is the most valuable thing you can contribute, because that is exactly the gap we want to find now rather than during a real event. Nothing said in this room will be attributed to an individual in the written report; findings are recorded against the organization and its plans.

  • Findings are attributed to processes, plans and gaps, never to individuals.
  • Disagreement between leaders is a positive outcome. It tells us where decision rights are unclear.
  • Participants are asked to answer as themselves, in their real role, based on what they would actually do, not what the policy says they should do.
  • The scenario is deliberately hard and the information is deliberately incomplete. Real events look like this.

2. Purpose and Objectives

The organization is past the technical question of how systems are restored. This exercise focuses on the business risk the leadership team must carry and decide on during recovery. It is structured around decisions, not details. The facilitator should steer the conversation away from technical troubleshooting and back to the decision in front of the room.

Objectives

  • Confirm who has the authority to make each critical decision, and who must be consulted before it is made.
  • Test whether leaders will activate the business continuity plan and accept the cost of shutting down revenue-generating services.
  • Surface each leader's individual position on ransom and extortion before a real demand arrives.
  • Determine what goes into a regulatory report when the facts are still incomplete, and who approves it.
  • Test communication under pressure, including who speaks publicly and how leaders verify each other when impersonation is in play.
  • Capture decisions, owners and gaps to populate the Crisis Decision Playbook (separate template).

Decisions This Exercise Forces

Four injects, each built around a core decision. Supporting decisions are folded into each inject.

  • Inject 1, We Are Down: do we activate the business continuity plan and shut down revenue? Also covers who leads the crisis management team, escalation, making decisions without complete information, and balancing business impact against the security response.
  • Inject 2, The Demand: the attacker wants $1M in bitcoin. Do we pay, and if so, on what terms and through what channel? Also covers ransom and extortion governance, insurer involvement, choice of legal counsel and external support, and sanctions screening.
  • Inject 3, Who Is Speaking for Us: stolen data appears on a leak site, the media calls, and someone impersonating the CEO is contacting customers and staff. Also covers reputational damage, who speaks publicly, stakeholder communication strategy and communication under pressure.
  • Inject 4, The 48-Hour Clock: regulators want a response within 48 hours. What is in the report? Also covers disclosure and materiality, regulatory reporting, external communication to customers and regulators, and approval of the recovery plan.

Additional decisions added to the mix

  • Restore fast or restore clean: do we bring services back before we are confident the attacker is gone?
  • Board notification: when is the board told, by whom, and does the board want a vote on ransom?
  • Delegation of authority: if the CEO or another key decision-maker is unreachable, who decides?
  • Legal privilege: are investigation work products being directed through counsel so they stay privileged?
  • Employee communications: what do we tell staff, and what do we tell them not to say?
  • Reopening revenue: what criteria must be met before we turn customer-facing services back on?
  • Customer contractual obligations: which customers have notification clauses or SLA credits that start the clock immediately?

3. Participants

Players

  • Chief Executive Officer (CEO)
  • Chief Financial Officer (CFO)
  • Chief Operating Officer (COO)
  • General Counsel (GC) / Chief Legal Officer
  • Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)
  • Chief Information Officer (CIO) / Chief Technology Officer (CTO)
  • Chief Communications Officer or VP of Communications
  • Chief Revenue Officer or Head of Sales / Customer Success
  • Chief Human Resources Officer (CHRO)
  • Chief Risk Officer or Risk and Insurance Manager
  • Board liaison or Corporate Secretary (optional)

Exercise staff

  • Lead facilitator: runs the clock, delivers injects, directs questions. Does not offer opinions on the right answer.
  • Scribe: records every decision, who made it, who agreed, who disagreed, and every "we don't know."
  • Observer(s): watch for decision-rights confusion, information gaps and groupthink. Feed notes into the debrief.

5. Facilitator Guidance: Individual Answers, Not Groupthink

The value of this exercise comes from hearing each leader's own position. Executives tend to defer to the most senior person in the room, and once the CEO speaks, the conversation usually converges. The techniques below are designed to prevent that. The facilitator decides which questions to ask and in what order; the question bank in each inject is a menu, not a script.

The core technique: direct, then cross-check

  • Direct the question to one named leader. "CFO, this is your call to make. What do you decide?" Do not open questions to the whole room.
  • Let them answer fully before anyone else speaks.
  • Cross-check with a second leader, usually the CEO or the person who would be affected most. "CEO, do you agree with that decision? Would you overrule it?"
  • Then ask a third leader for the dissenting view. "GC, what concerns you about what you just heard?"
  • The CEO speaks last on core decisions, not first. If the CEO answers first, the room will anchor on that answer.

Additional techniques

  • Silent vote before discussion. For the three core decisions (activate BCP, pay or not, what goes in the report), have each leader write their answer on an index card before any discussion. Collect the cards and read them aloud anonymously, then reveal the spread. A split vote is a finding.
  • "Whose call is it?" Before discussing any decision, ask the room to point to the person who owns it. If people point at different people, stop and record it.
  • The empty chair. At least once, tell the room a key leader is unreachable (on a flight, in hospital, not answering). Ask who decides in their absence.
  • Time pressure. Give leaders a hard deadline for some decisions ("You have 90 seconds"). Real decisions happen without time to deliberate.
  • Hold the line on details. If the conversation drifts into how a system is restored, redirect: "Assume the technical team is handling it. What do you need to decide?"
  • Record disagreement explicitly. The scribe notes who agreed, who disagreed and why. Unanimity is not the goal.

6. Scenario Background

Read aloud or send as a pre-read.

Crestline Software Group provides a subscription platform used by about 1,200 business customers for billing, payments and customer records. The platform processes roughly $1.1M in customer transactions per day and is the company's sole source of revenue. Crestline holds personal information on several hundred thousand end users of its customers' businesses, including names, contact details and some partial payment data. Some customers are in regulated industries and have contractual notification requirements.

It is Tuesday at 6:40 a.m. The overnight on-call engineer has escalated an alert: several production systems are encrypted and a ransom note has appeared on administrator consoles. The security team has confirmed this is a real incident. You have been called into an emergency meeting.

7. Inject 1: We Are Down

Time in scenario: Tuesday, 8:00 a.m. (about 1.5 hours after detection) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • The customer-facing billing platform is partially available. Some customers can log in, but payment processing is failing intermittently.
  • The security team believes the attacker may still have access to the environment. They cannot yet say how they got in or what else they touched.
  • The CISO's team is recommending a full shutdown of customer-facing services to contain the attack. That would stop all revenue, roughly $46,000 per hour.
  • Customer support has received 140 tickets in the last hour. Two of the company's top ten customers have called their account managers directly.
  • The business continuity plan was last updated 14 months ago. Nobody in the room has read it recently.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: systems are encrypted, the attacker is likely still present, revenue is already degraded.
  • Unknown: how long recovery will take, whether data was stolen, whether backups are clean, the full blast radius.

Decisions to force

  • Do we activate the business continuity plan? Who has the authority to declare it?
  • Do we shut down revenue-generating services to contain the attack, or keep them running in a degraded state?
  • Who leads the crisis management team, and who is their alternate?
  • What is our escalation path from here: board, insurer, outside counsel?

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CEO: Who in this room has the authority to declare a crisis and activate the business continuity plan? Is it you, or have you delegated it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the COO and the CISO separately who they believe holds that authority. Do the answers match?
  • Ask the CISO: You are recommending a full shutdown. Make the case in one minute: what happens if we do not shut down?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: do you accept that recommendation at $46,000 per hour? What is your limit?
  • Ask the CFO: How many hours or days of lost revenue can the company absorb before this becomes a liquidity or covenant issue?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: does that number change your decision on the shutdown?
  • Ask the Chief Revenue Officer: Two top-ten customers are on the phone right now. What do your account managers tell them in the next 30 minutes?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: is there anything in that message that creates legal exposure?
  • Ask the COO: If we activate the BCP, what does the business actually do differently in the next four hours? Can customers be served manually in any way?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CIO: is that realistic with the systems we still have?
  • Ask the CEO: Who leads the crisis management team? If it is you, who runs the company while you run the crisis?
    • Then cross-check: Ask each executive to name the crisis lead without discussion. Record the spread.

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The CEO is on a flight to Singapore and unreachable for the next nine hours. Who decides on the shutdown?
  • A product leader argues that shutting down will violate the 99.9% SLA for 300 customers and trigger penalty credits. Does that change the decision?

What good looks like (for observers)

  • A single, named decision-maker for BCP activation and for the shutdown, with a named alternate.
  • The shutdown decision is made deliberately, with the revenue impact stated and accepted, rather than defaulting to whatever the technical team does.
  • A crisis management team lead is named, and that person is not also expected to run day-to-day operations.
  • Leaders explicitly acknowledge they are deciding without complete information and set a time to revisit the decision.

8. Inject 2: The Demand

Time in scenario: Wednesday, 12:30 a.m. (about 18 hours after detection) | Facilitation time: 17 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • Services have been shut down since 9:00 a.m. Tuesday. Revenue lost so far is approximately $720,000.
  • The attacker has made contact through a link in the ransom note. They demand $1,000,000 in bitcoin within 72 hours. If paid, they promise a decryption tool and deletion of stolen data.
  • The attacker claims to have stolen 340 GB of customer data and has posted a sample of 50 customer records as proof. The security team confirms the sample is genuine.
  • The recovery team estimates five to eight days to restore from backups. Two backup sets appear clean; a third may be compromised.
  • The cyber insurance policy has a $5M limit and a $250,000 retention. The insurer has not yet been notified.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the demand amount and deadline, that some data was stolen, the backup timeline estimate.
  • Unknown: whether the decryptor works, whether the attacker will actually delete the data, whether the attacker group is on a sanctions list, how much data was really taken.

Decisions to force

  • Do we pay the ransom? Who has the authority to approve payment, and does the board need to vote?
  • If we pay, on what terms? Do we insist on a single, controlled payment channel, use a professional negotiator, require proof of decryption first, or refuse bitcoin and push for a different arrangement?
  • When do we notify the insurer, and do we use the insurer's panel counsel, negotiator and forensics firm, or our own?
  • What is our ransom and extortion governance? Who is allowed to communicate with the attacker at all?

Silent vote

Before discussion, each leader writes on a card: Pay / Don't pay / Need more information, plus one sentence explaining why. Read the cards aloud anonymously, then begin the questions.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CFO: Leave the ethics aside for a moment. Purely on the numbers, $1M in ransom against five to eight more days of downtime: which option is cheaper, and how confident are you?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: is cost the deciding factor for you? If not, what is?
  • Ask the General Counsel: What are the legal constraints on paying? How do we confirm the attacker is not a sanctioned party, and who does that check?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: does the policy cover ransom payments, and what does the insurer require before we pay?
  • Ask the CEO: If the room is split, who makes the final call on ransom? Is it you alone, you with the board, or the board?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the board liaison: would the board expect to vote on this? How fast can you convene them?
  • Ask the CISO: If we pay and receive a decryptor, how much faster is recovery really? Does paying change what we have to do to make sure the attacker is gone?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CIO: would you trust a decryptor from the attacker on production systems?
  • Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: The insurer has not been notified. What is our notification deadline under the policy, and what happens if we have already engaged our own counsel and forensics firm?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: do we use the insurer's panel counsel or our own outside counsel? Who decides?
  • Ask the General Counsel: If we decide to pay, do we insist on one controlled channel through a professional negotiator? Would we accept anything other than bitcoin, or require proof that decryption works first?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: do we even have the ability to acquire $1M in cryptocurrency within 72 hours? Who in finance would do it?
  • Ask the Chief Communications Officer: If we pay and it becomes public, what is the reputational impact? If we don't pay and the data is published, what is the impact?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Revenue Officer: which outcome do our customers forgive more easily?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The attacker reduces the demand to $600,000 if paid within 24 hours. Does the deadline pressure change anyone's vote?
  • An engineer, acting on their own, has already replied to the attacker from a personal email account. What now?

What good looks like (for observers)

  • The ransom decision has a named owner and a defined approval path (for example CEO with board consultation), agreed before a demand arrives.
  • Nobody communicates with the attacker except through a designated, professional channel.
  • Sanctions screening and insurer notification are treated as prerequisites, not afterthoughts.
  • The team separates the decryption question from the data-deletion question. Paying rarely guarantees deletion.
  • Leaders state their own position rather than waiting for the CEO.

9. Inject 3: Who Is Speaking for Us

Time in scenario: Wednesday, 2:00 p.m. (about 31 hours after detection) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • The attacker has published Crestline's name on their leak site with a countdown timer and 2,000 customer records.
  • A reporter from a national business publication has emailed the Chief Communications Officer asking for comment by 5:00 p.m. today. A second reporter has messaged a sales director on LinkedIn.
  • Three customers have forwarded an email that appears to be from the CEO, apologizing for the breach and asking them to update payment details at a new link. The CEO did not send it.
  • The finance team received a voicemail that sounds like the CFO, asking for an urgent wire transfer to a "recovery vendor."
  • Employees are posting about the outage on social media. One post says, "We got hacked and leadership won't tell us anything."

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: data is public, media is asking, someone is impersonating leaders.
  • Unknown: whether the impersonation is the same attacker, how many customers received the fake email, whether any wire transfer was made.

Decisions to force

  • Who speaks publicly for the company, and what do they say by 5:00 p.m.?
  • How do we protect against impersonation of leaders? How do customers, staff and finance verify a message is real?
  • What is our communication strategy across customers, employees, media, partners and investors, and in what order?
  • How do we manage reputational damage when the attacker controls the timing of what becomes public?

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the Chief Communications Officer: Who is the spokesperson? Is it the CEO, you, or someone else? What is the one sentence we give the reporter by 5:00 p.m.?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: do you agree that you should or should not be the face of this? Ask the GC: would you approve that sentence?
  • Ask the CEO: Someone is sending emails in your name to customers. How does a customer know whether a message from you is real?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CISO: what verification method do we have today, and does anyone outside this room know it?
  • Ask the CFO: Finance received a voicemail that sounded like you asking for a wire. What stops that payment today? What is the verification rule?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the COO: is that rule written down anywhere, and does every person who can release a wire know it?
  • Ask the CHRO: Employees are posting publicly and feel left in the dark. What do we tell staff today, and what do we ask them not to say?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Communications Officer: does the employee message conflict with what we are telling the media?
  • Ask the Chief Revenue Officer: In what order do we tell stakeholders: customers, employees, media, regulators, investors? Who hears first?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: does any regulator or contract dictate that order for us?
  • Ask the CEO: Are we communicating through channels the attacker may be able to see? Where is the leadership team talking right now?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CISO: should we move to an out-of-band channel, and does everyone have access to it?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The CEO is asked to give an interview on live television tomorrow morning. Yes or no?
  • A well-known industry analyst posts that Crestline "hid the breach for two days." Do we respond?

What good looks like (for observers)

  • One designated spokesperson and one approval path for all external statements, with legal review built in.
  • A pre-agreed verification method for leader communications (callback to a known number, code phrase, or out-of-band confirmation).
  • A hard rule that no payment is released on voice or email instruction alone during a crisis.
  • Employees are treated as a stakeholder group with their own message, not an afterthought.
  • Leadership moves to an out-of-band communication channel if the corporate environment may be compromised.

10. Inject 4: The 48-Hour Clock

Time in scenario: Thursday, 9:00 a.m. (about 50 hours after detection) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • A data protection regulator has contacted Crestline after seeing press coverage. They want a written response within 48 hours describing the incident, the data involved, affected individuals and the actions taken.
  • Two customers in regulated industries have formally invoked their contract's breach notification clause and want written details within 24 hours.
  • Forensics now believes the attacker entered 17 days before encryption, through a compromised vendor remote-access account.
  • The recovery team has a plan to restore the billing platform in three days from the clean backups. It requires the business to accept that some transactions from the last 36 hours before the attack will be lost and must be reconstructed manually.
  • The CFO's team estimates total impact so far at $2.3M in lost revenue, plus recovery costs. The auditors have asked whether this is material.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the regulator deadline, the entry point, the recovery plan and its trade-off.
  • Unknown: the final count of affected individuals, whether other vendors are affected, whether the attacker is fully removed.

Decisions to force

  • What is in the regulatory report? What do we state as fact, what do we state as still under investigation, and who signs it?
  • Is this incident material? Who decides, and does it trigger disclosure to investors, lenders or auditors?
  • Do we approve the recovery plan, including the data loss it requires? Who signs off on the business continuity and recovery plan?
  • What criteria must be met before we reopen revenue and turn customer-facing services back on?

Silent vote

Before discussion, each leader writes on a card the three things that must be in the regulator report and one thing that must not be. Compare the cards.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the General Counsel: Walk us through what goes in the report. What do we say about data we have not finished analyzing?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CISO: is anything in that report technically wrong or likely to change? Ask the CEO: are you comfortable signing it?
  • Ask the CFO: Is this material? What is the threshold, and who makes the materiality determination?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: if we are wrong about materiality, what is the exposure? Ask the board liaison: does the audit committee need to be involved?
  • Ask the COO: The recovery plan loses 36 hours of transactions. Do you approve it? Who else needs to approve it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Revenue Officer: can our customers live with that loss? Ask the CFO: what is the financial impact of reconstructing those transactions?
  • Ask the CISO: What must be true before you are comfortable reopening customer-facing services? Are you confident the attacker is out?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: if the CISO says "not yet" but the business is bleeding, who wins? Who breaks the tie?
  • Ask the Chief Revenue Officer: Two regulated customers want details in 24 hours, before we tell the regulator. What do we give them?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: is what we tell customers consistent with what we will tell the regulator?
  • Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: The entry point was a vendor account. Do we have a claim against the vendor, and does that affect what we say publicly?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: when do we notify the vendor, and who does it?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The regulator asks why they learned about the incident from the press. What is our answer?
  • A second regulator in another jurisdiction contacts you with a 72-hour deadline. Who tracks every reporting obligation and deadline?

What good looks like (for observers)

  • The report distinguishes clearly between confirmed facts and facts still under investigation, and commits to supplemental updates.
  • One person owns the inventory of all reporting deadlines across regulators, customers, insurers and investors.
  • Materiality is assessed by a defined group with a documented rationale, not decided informally.
  • Recovery plan approval, including accepted data loss, is a documented business decision with a named approver.
  • Reopening criteria are agreed between security and the business before the pressure to reopen peaks.

11. Debrief

Allow at least 18 minutes. The debrief is the most valuable part of the exercise. Keep the no-fault tone: findings are about plans and decision rights, not people.

How to run it

  • Go around the room first. Ask each leader for one answer to each core question before opening the floor. Start with the most junior leader and finish with the CEO.
  • Revisit the silent votes. Show the spread of answers from the cards. Ask: "We were split on this. What would it take to agree before a real event?"
  • Name the owner for every gap. Every gap identified leaves the room with an owner and a target date.
  • Capture straight into the playbook. The scribe maps each decision to the Crisis Decision Playbook template during the debrief.

The three core questions

1. What decisions did we struggle with?

  • Which decision took the longest, or never got made?
  • Where did two leaders believe they owned the same decision, or where did nobody believe they owned it?
  • Which decision would you make differently now that you have heard the discussion?

2. What information did we need but didn't have?

  • What did you need to know to make your decision, and who should have been able to tell you?
  • Was the information available somewhere in the organization but not in the room?
  • What did we assume that we have not actually verified (for example insurance coverage, backup integrity, contract terms)?

3. What should we formalize before a real event?

  • Which decisions need a pre-agreed owner, alternate and approval path?
  • Which thresholds should we set now (for example revenue shutdown limits, ransom position, reopening criteria)?
  • What pre-approved relationships do we need in place (outside counsel, forensics, negotiator, PR firm, insurer contacts)?

Additional debrief questions

  • Where did we default to the most senior person instead of the person who owns the decision?
  • Who was missing from the room who should have been part of these decisions?
  • What would have happened if a key leader were unavailable for 24 hours?
  • Where did the business and security priorities conflict, and how should that conflict be resolved next time?
  • What is our current position on ransom? Is it written down, and does the board agree with it?
  • What did we say we would communicate that we don't actually have a template for?
  • Which of our plans did we reference today, and which did nobody mention? A plan nobody remembers is not a plan.
  • What is the single most important action we should take in the next 30 days?
  • On a scale of 1 to 5, how confident are you that we could make these decisions in a real event? Ask each leader individually.

Closing statement (read aloud)

Thank you. Every gap we found today is one we will not have to discover during a real incident. Nothing said here will be attributed to individuals. We will circulate a summary of findings, the decisions we captured, and the draft Crisis Decision Playbook within five business days. Each action item will have an owner and a date.

12. After-Action Outputs

  • After-action summary within five business days: decisions made, decisions not made, gaps, and confidence scores.
  • Action register: every gap with an owner, target date and status.
  • Draft Crisis Decision Playbook populated with the decision owners, alternates and thresholds agreed today.
  • Parking lot: technical or detailed items raised during the session, routed to the right team.
  • Next exercise: schedule a follow-up session to validate the playbook and test any decisions left unresolved.

Appendix A: Scribe Decision Log

For each decision raised during the exercise, the scribe records the following. Use one block per decision.

  • Decision: what was being decided
  • Inject: 1, 2, 3 or 4
  • Who the room said owns it: and whether everyone agreed
  • Decision made: or "not made"
  • Who agreed / who disagreed: and the reason given for any disagreement
  • Information that was missing
  • Gap or action item: with proposed owner
  • Playbook section it belongs in

Appendix B: Observer Checklist

  • Did leaders answer individually, or did they defer to the most senior person?
  • Was each core decision assigned to one named owner?
  • Did anyone reference the business continuity plan, ransom policy or communication plan by name?
  • Were insurer notification and legal counsel engaged early enough?
  • Did leaders acknowledge uncertainty and set a time to revisit decisions?
  • Did the conversation drift into technical detail, and how quickly was it redirected?
  • Was the impersonation risk recognized and addressed with a concrete verification rule?
  • Were employees, customers, regulators and media treated as separate audiences?
