Crisis Decision Playbook: False Balance Edition Template

1. This Is a No-Fault Exercise

Read this section aloud at the start of the session.

This is a no-fault exercise. Nobody is being tested and nobody is being graded. Today's scenario involves a colleague who may have done something seriously wrong, or who may be completely innocent. It is fictional and does not refer to anyone in this organization. We are testing how our plans, our controls and our decision rights hold up when the threat is inside, when our records can't be trusted, and when real people's pay is at stake. If you don't know who owns a decision, or you aren't sure what the law allows us to do, say so. That is a finding about our plans, not about you. Nothing said in this room will be attributed to an individual.

  • Findings are recorded against plans, controls and decision rights, never against individuals.
  • The suspect in this scenario is fictional. Please do not speculate about real colleagues.
  • Answer as yourself, in your real role, based on what you would actually do.
  • Disagreement is useful. It shows where our decision rights are unclear.

2. How This Exercise Differs

Operation Pressure Point tested leadership when systems were down. Operation Broken Chain tested leadership when our product harmed customers. Operation False Balance removes the outside attacker and the outage. Every system is up. The problem is that nobody can say which of our records are true, there is no clean backup to go back to, and the evidence points at a long-serving colleague who may have been framed.

  • From outage to integrity. Systems are running. The data inside them is wrong in ways we can't yet measure.
  • From outside attacker to insider. The likely actor has legitimate access, knows our controls and has colleagues in this room.
  • No clean restore point. Nine weeks of backups contain altered data. Recovery means rebuilding the truth, not restoring it.
  • Real people, real money. The people harmed are workers who didn't get paid, not abstract customers.
  • Our own assurance is in question. The controls report our customers' auditors rely on may no longer be reliable.

3. Objectives

  • Decide whether to keep processing on data we can't fully trust, delay, or fall back to an older known-good state, and who has the authority to choose.
  • Test how leadership balances fairness to an accused employee with protecting the company, its customers and the evidence.
  • Confirm who leads an insider investigation (HR, Legal, Security or Internal Audit) and when law enforcement is involved.
  • Resolve the tension between preserving evidence and correcting the data.
  • Decide whether we make affected people whole before we know the full facts or before insurance responds, and who approves the money.
  • Determine what we owe customers' auditors and regulators when our own controls report may no longer be reliable.
  • Agree who certifies that our data is trustworthy again, and what evidence they need.
  • Capture outputs for the Crisis Decision Playbook: Data Integrity and Insider Edition (separate template).

Decisions This Exercise Forces

  • Inject 1, Which Numbers Are True? Payroll for 380,000 workers is due in three days, and nine weeks of data may be altered. Run it, delay it, or run it from old data? Also covers business continuity plan activation, pausing changes on the platform, and deciding without complete information.
  • Inject 2, One of Our Own. The evidence points to a senior database administrator with 11 years' service. Also covers who leads the investigation, suspending access, law enforcement, which insurance policy responds, and what we tell staff.
  • Inject 3, Paychecks Are Personal. Workers whose pay was diverted are going public, and employers are furious. Also covers making people whole, who speaks publicly, reputational damage and communicating with people who aren't our direct customers.
  • Inject 4, Restating the Record. Tax filings are wrong, our controls report may be unreliable, and our own revenue may be misstated. Also covers regulatory reporting, disclosure and materiality, and approval of the recovery plan.

Additional decisions added to the mix

  • Preserve or fix first: do we correct records immediately, or freeze them so the evidence stays intact?
  • Collective suspension: do we suspend every administrator's access, knowing it would cripple recovery?
  • Presumption of innocence: what do we say to and about an employee who may not be responsible?
  • Trusting the investigators: who investigates the people who have the same access as the suspect?
  • Customers' auditors: do we tell customers their auditors should not rely on our controls report?
  • Separation of duties: which single person today can change data and cover their tracks, and who decides to fix that?

4. Participants

Players

  • Chief Executive Officer (CEO)
  • Chief Financial Officer (CFO)
  • Chief Operating Officer (COO), who owns payroll operations
  • General Counsel (GC) / Chief Legal Officer
  • Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)
  • Chief Information Officer (CIO) or Chief Technology Officer
  • Chief Human Resources Officer (CHRO)
  • Head of Internal Audit or Chief Compliance Officer
  • Chief Customer Officer or Head of Client Services
  • Chief Communications Officer or VP of Communications
  • Chief Risk Officer or Risk and Insurance Manager
  • Board liaison or Audit Committee representative (optional)

Exercise staff

  • Lead facilitator: runs the clock, delivers injects, directs questions to individuals.
  • Scribe: records each decision, its owner, who agreed and who disagreed. Do not record personal opinions about the fictional suspect.
  • Observer(s): watch for decision-rights confusion between HR, Legal, Security and Internal Audit, and for groupthink.

6. Facilitator Guidance

The series rule still applies: individual answers first, then the cross-check. Choose questions from each inject's bank; it is a menu, not a script.

The core technique: direct, then cross-check

  • Direct each question to one named leader and let them answer fully before anyone else speaks.
  • Cross-check with a second leader, usually the CEO or the leader whose function carries the consequence.
  • Invite the dissent: ask a third leader what concerns them about what they just heard.
  • The CEO speaks last on the core decisions.

Techniques specific to this scenario

  • Silent vote. Use index cards before the core decisions in Injects 1, 2 and 4.
  • Four owners, one question. In Inject 2, ask the CHRO, GC, CISO and Head of Internal Audit separately: "Who leads this investigation?" Record all four answers before any discussion.
  • The other side of the table. At least once, ask a leader to answer as the accused employee's lawyer would: "What would you say about how the company treated your client?"
  • The worker's view. Read the Inject 3 social media post aloud, then ask a leader to respond to that worker directly, as if on the phone.
  • Hold the line on details. Redirect technical debate: "Assume the data team is handling it. What do you need to decide?"
  • Keep it fictional. If anyone starts linking the suspect to a real person, stop and restate that the scenario is fictional.

7. Scenario Background

Read aloud or send as a pre-read.

The company runs payroll, tax filing and benefits administration for about 2,400 employers with roughly 380,000 workers between them. The company moves about $1.9 billion in wages every month and files payroll taxes on its clients' behalf. Many clients rely on the company's annual independent controls report (a SOC 1 and SOC 2) to satisfy their own auditors. The company's revenue is about $140M a year.

The company's databases are managed by a team of eight database administrators who have broad access to production data so they can fix problems quickly. Backups are taken nightly and kept for 90 days.

It is Tuesday at 8:00 a.m. Last night, the payroll reconciliation team noticed that the total tax withheld for one client didn't match the total filed with the tax authority. Overnight, the data team found similar mismatches across dozens of clients, going back at least nine weeks. Some workers' bank account details appear to have been changed and then changed back. Database audit logs for several of those changes are missing. You have been called into an emergency meeting.

8. Inject 1: Which Numbers Are True?

Time in scenario: Tuesday, 10:00 a.m. (2 hours after the meeting is called) | Facilitation time: 16 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • The data team has confirmed that records were deliberately altered: bank account details, tax withholding amounts and some year-to-date totals. This was not a software bug.
  • Changes started at least nine weeks ago. Every backup from the last nine weeks contains altered data. The oldest clean backup is from before then and is missing nine weeks of legitimate new hires, raises and terminations.
  • The next payroll run for about 210,000 workers is due Friday. Files must go to the banks by Thursday at 5:00 p.m.
  • The data team cannot yet say how many records were changed. Its best estimate is between 0.5% and 3% of workers.
  • The person or account responsible may still have access. Anything we change now may be changed again.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: data was altered deliberately, over nine weeks, and payroll is due in three days.
  • Unknown: how many records are wrong, who did it, whether they still have access, and how much money has left the company.

Decisions to force

  • Do we run Friday's payroll on data we know is partly wrong, delay it, or run it from the nine-week-old clean backup and correct later?
  • Do we activate the business continuity plan and freeze all non-essential changes to production data?
  • Do we preserve the evidence or start fixing the data? We can't fully do both at once.
  • Who leads the crisis team for an incident that is part security, part operations and part HR?

Silent vote

Each leader writes on a card: Run payroll as is / Delay payroll / Run from the old clean backup, and Preserve first / Fix first.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the COO: 210,000 people expect to be paid Friday. Which option do you choose, and how many people get paid the wrong amount under each?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: would you overrule that choice? Ask the CFO: what does each option cost us?
  • Ask the General Counsel: If we knowingly run payroll on data we believe is wrong, what is our exposure? If we delay pay, what is it then?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CHRO: what happens to workers, and to our clients' relationships with their workers, if pay is late?
  • Ask the CISO: If we start correcting records now, what happens to the evidence? How long do you need before the data can be touched?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the COO: can payroll wait that long? Ask the CEO to break the tie.
  • Ask the CFO: If we pay from nine-week-old data, some people are overpaid and some underpaid. Do we absorb the difference, and up to what amount without board approval?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the board liaison: at what amount does the audit committee need to be told?
  • Ask the CEO: This is a security incident, an operations crisis and possibly an employment matter. Who leads the crisis team?
    • Then cross-check: Ask each leader to write the name on a card without discussion. Record the spread.
  • Ask the Chief Customer Officer: 2,400 employers will need to be told something before Thursday. What do we say today, and to whom: their payroll contact, their CFO or their CEO?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC whether you would approve that message.

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • A large client's payroll manager calls: "Our CEO's direct deposit went to a different bank last month. Is this connected?"
  • The data team says that if we freeze changes, about 4,000 legitimate new hires and terminations entered this week won't be processed Friday.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • A named leader decides how payroll will run, with the number of workers affected under each option stated and accepted.
  • Evidence preservation is explicitly weighed against correction, with a time limit agreed rather than a default.
  • The crisis lead is named, and HR, Legal and Security understand their roles under that lead.
  • Clients hear from the company before they hear from their own workers.

9. Inject 2: One of Our Own

Time in scenario: Tuesday, 4:00 p.m. (8 hours in) | Facilitation time: 16 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • Nearly every altered record traces back to the administrator account of a senior database administrator with 11 years at the company. They are well liked, have been on vacation since Friday, and are scheduled to return Monday.
  • Most changes came from the employee's home internet connection. Three changes came from an overseas connection while the employee was known to be in the office. Their credentials may have been stolen, or they may have used a tool to hide their location.
  • Bank details for about 1,900 workers were changed to accounts at two online banks, then changed back after payday. Roughly $4.2M appears to have been diverted.
  • The other seven administrators have the same level of access. One of them is leading the data recovery work.
  • the company has a cyber insurance policy and a separate crime (employee dishonesty) policy. They have different deductibles, and each insurer may argue the other should pay.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the account used, the amount diverted, the number of workers affected, the overseas logins.
  • Unknown: whether the employee did it, acted under pressure, or was framed; whether anyone else is involved; whether more money is still moving.

Decisions to force

  • Who leads the investigation into the employee: HR, Legal, Security or Internal Audit?
  • Do we contact the employee now, suspend them, or wait until they return? Do we suspend access for all administrators?
  • When do we involve law enforcement, and who makes that call?
  • Which insurance policy do we notify, and do we notify both?
  • What do we tell staff, especially the employee's own team?

Silent vote

Before discussion, have the CHRO, GC, CISO and Head of Internal Audit each write who leads the investigation. Have every leader write Suspend the employee now / Wait / Suspend all administrators.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CHRO: The employee is on vacation and may be innocent. Do we call them, and if so, who calls and what do they say?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: is anything in that approach a legal risk? Ask the CISO: does calling them warn a guilty person?
  • Ask the CISO: Seven other administrators have the same access. Do we suspend them all? If we do, who recovers the data?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CIO: can recovery continue without them? Ask the Head of Internal Audit: who watches the person leading recovery?
  • Ask the General Counsel: When do we call law enforcement? Once we do, what control do we lose over the investigation and the timeline?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CEO: are you comfortable with that trade-off? Ask the Chief Communications Officer: does a police report change what becomes public?
  • Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: Cyber policy or crime policy? What do we lose if we notify the wrong one, or notify one too late?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: which outcome is better for us financially, and who decides?
  • Ask the Head of Internal Audit: How did one person make these changes for nine weeks without anyone noticing? Which control failed?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CIO: is that a gap you knew about? Ask the CEO: who owns fixing it?
  • Ask the CHRO: The employee's team is asking where their colleague is and why their access has changed. What do they hear?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Communications Officer whether that message is consistent with what clients are being told.

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • The employee emails the CEO from a personal account: "I've seen messages from my team. I didn't do anything. Please call me." Does the CEO reply?
  • A second administrator quietly tells the CISO they shared their password with the suspect last year "to cover a shift."

What good looks like (for observers)

  • One function leads the investigation, with the others in defined supporting roles.
  • Leaders treat the employee fairly and don't assume guilt, while still protecting evidence and limiting access.
  • Access decisions balance the risk of collusion against the need to keep recovery going, with independent oversight of the recovery team.
  • Both insurers are notified promptly and the decision is documented.

10. Inject 3: Paychecks Are Personal

Time in scenario: Thursday, 9:00 a.m. (about 49 hours in) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • Workers whose pay was diverted in past months have started to realize it. A social media post from a warehouse worker reads: "My paycheck vanished in September and my payroll company told my employer it was MY bank's fault. Now I find out it was THEM." It has been shared 40,000 times.
  • Several employers have told their workers to contact the company directly. The company has no relationship with workers and no call center for them.
  • A client with 18,000 workers says it will move to a competitor unless every affected worker is repaid by Friday's payday.
  • Repaying all 1,900 workers now would cost about $4.2M from the company's own funds. Neither insurer has confirmed coverage, and the investigation may later show some claims were not genuine.
  • A business reporter is asking for comment by noon. A state labor department official has emailed asking for "an explanation."

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: who was affected, how much, that some workers were wrongly told earlier that it was their bank's fault, and that the media and a regulator are asking.
  • Unknown: whether the insurers will pay, how many more workers will come forward, and how many clients will leave.

Decisions to force

  • Do we repay affected workers now, from our own funds, before insurance responds and before the investigation is complete?
  • Who speaks publicly, and do we acknowledge that some workers were wrongly told it was their bank's fault?
  • How do we communicate with workers who aren't our customers, without going around the employers who are?
  • What do we say about the employee? Anything at all?

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CFO: $4.2M from our own funds by Friday. Can we do it, should we do it, and who approves it?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Risk and Insurance Manager: does paying now affect our insurance claim? Ask the CEO to decide.
  • Ask the Chief Communications Officer: Who speaks, and what do we say about the worker who was told it was their bank's fault?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: can we admit that without creating more liability? Ask the CEO: should we?
  • Ask the Chief Customer Officer: Employers are sending their workers to us. Do we set up a worker hotline, or insist everything go through the employer?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the COO: can we stand up a hotline by Friday, and who staffs it?
  • Ask the General Counsel: A state labor department is asking for an explanation. Who responds, and what do we commit to in writing?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Head of Internal Audit: are the facts we would give them verified?
  • Ask the CHRO: If asked, what do we say publicly about the employee under investigation?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC whether that answer protects both the company and the employee's rights.
  • Ask the CEO: The client with 18,000 workers wants every worker repaid by Friday or it leaves. Do we let one client set our timeline?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Chief Customer Officer: if we do it for them, do we have to do it for everyone?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • An email that appears to come from the company's CFO tells clients to send affected workers' bank details to a new address "for fast repayment." It is fake.
  • The employee's name appears in a local news story. Nobody at the company admits to sharing it.

What good looks like (for observers)

  • A clear decision on repaying workers, made with the insurance and legal implications understood, not avoided.
  • One spokesperson and honest acknowledgment where the company got something wrong.
  • A way for affected workers to get help that respects the employer relationship.
  • Nothing is said publicly that names or prejudges the employee.

11. Inject 4: Restating the Record

Time in scenario: the following Wednesday, 9:00 a.m. (about 8 days in) | Facilitation time: 15 minutes

Situation (read aloud)

  • The data team presents two recovery options:
  • Targeted correction: fix only the records proven to be altered. Ready in 5 days. Confidence moderate, because the missing audit logs mean some changes may never be found.
  • Full rebuild: rebuild nine weeks of payroll records from clients' original source files and bank confirmations. 6 weeks of work, about $3M, and every client must re-confirm its data. Confidence high.
  • Payroll tax filings for about 600 clients over the past nine weeks were wrong. Amended filings will be needed, and tax authorities may apply penalties.
  • the company's controls report was issued to clients five weeks ago and states that access to payroll data is properly controlled. The audit firm says clients' auditors may no longer be able to rely on it.
  • The CFO says the company's own fee revenue for the quarter may also be misstated. The external auditor wants a meeting. The audit committee chair has called a special session.

What you know and what you don't

  • Known: the two recovery options, the filings that need amending, the controls report problem and that our own numbers may be affected.
  • Unknown: whether all altered records have been found, the final penalties, and how many clients' auditors will react.

Decisions to force

  • Targeted correction or full rebuild? Who approves the recovery plan and accepts the risk that remains?
  • Do we tell clients their auditors should not rely on our controls report, and how?
  • Who certifies that our data is trustworthy again, and what evidence must they see?
  • Is this material to our own financial statements, and what does the audit committee need to decide?

Silent vote

Each leader writes on a card: Targeted correction / Full rebuild, and the name of the person who should certify the data as trustworthy.

Question bank (direct, then cross-check)

  • Ask the CIO: Targeted correction or full rebuild? If we choose targeted correction and more altered records surface in six months, what happens?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the Head of Internal Audit: would you sign off on targeted correction? Ask the Chief Customer Officer: will clients accept re-confirming nine weeks of data?
  • Ask the Head of Internal Audit: Our controls report says access is properly controlled. Do we proactively tell clients it may not be reliable, or wait for the audit firm?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the GC: what is our exposure either way? Ask the CEO to decide.
  • Ask the CFO: Is this material? Who decides, and does it change what we tell the external auditor or lenders?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the board liaison: what does the audit committee want to decide itself?
  • Ask the CEO: Who signs a statement that our payroll data is trustworthy again? Would you sign it yourself? On what evidence?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CISO and the Head of Internal Audit separately whether they would co-sign.
  • Ask the COO: 600 clients need amended tax filings. Who tells them, who pays the penalties, and in what order do we work?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CFO: is there a limit on penalties we absorb without approval?
  • Ask the CISO: What has to be true about administrator access before we can say this can't happen again?
    • Then cross-check: Ask the CIO: what does that cost in speed and staff? Ask the CEO: are you willing to accept that cost?

Facilitator curveballs (optional)

  • Forensics now finds that the overseas logins came from malware on the employee's home computer. The employee may be a victim. How does that change our decisions and our communications?
  • A competitor's sales team is telling the company's clients that "their controls report is worthless."

What good looks like (for observers)

  • The recovery choice is a documented business decision with a named approver who explicitly accepts the remaining risk.
  • Clients and their auditors are told promptly and plainly about the reliability of the controls report.
  • The person certifying the data is independent of the people who had the access, and has the evidence.
  • Materiality is assessed by a defined group with a documented rationale.

12. Debrief

Allow at least 18 minutes. Restate that the suspect was fictional and that findings are about plans, controls and decision rights. This scenario touches on trust between colleagues, so keep the tone calm and constructive.

How to run it

  • Go around the room first, starting with the most junior leader and finishing with the CEO.
  • Revisit the silent votes, especially the four answers to "who leads the investigation."
  • Name an owner and a date for every gap, and map each decision into the playbook as it is captured.

The three core questions

1. What decisions did we struggle with?

  • Which decision took longest, or was never made?
  • Did HR, Legal, Security and Internal Audit agree on who leads an insider investigation?
  • Where did fairness to the employee and protection of the company pull in different directions?

2. What information did we need but didn't have?

  • Did we know when our data was last proven correct, and how we would prove it?
  • Did we know which insurance policy covers insider fraud, and what each requires of us?
  • Did we know what our controls report promises our clients and their auditors?

3. What should we formalize before a real event?

  • Who leads an insider investigation, and how HR, Legal, Security and Internal Audit work together.
  • Our rule for preserving evidence versus correcting data, including a time limit.
  • Our authority and limits for making affected people whole before insurance responds.
  • Who certifies data as trustworthy after an integrity incident.

Additional debrief questions for this scenario

  • How fairly did we treat the employee? Would we be comfortable if they read the transcript of today's discussion?
  • Which single person in our real organization could change critical data and hide it today?
  • How would we know our data is wrong if a reconciliation hadn't caught it?
  • Did we treat workers as people or as a liability? What would the worker who posted online say about our response?
  • What do our controls reports promise, and are we confident those promises are true right now?
  • Who watches the people who recover the data after an insider incident?
  • On a scale of 1 to 5, how confident are you that we could handle an insider incident well? Ask each leader individually.
  • What is the single most important action we should take in the next 30 days?

Closing statement (read aloud)

Thank you. Today we worked through one of the hardest kinds of incident: one where the threat may be a colleague, the records can't be trusted and real people's pay is affected. The suspect in this scenario was fictional. Every gap we found is one we won't have to discover in a real event. Nothing said here will be attributed to individuals. We will circulate the findings, the decisions we captured and the draft Data Integrity and Insider Edition of the Crisis Decision Playbook within five business days, with an owner and a date for every action.

13. After-Action Outputs

  • After-action summary within five business days: decisions made, decisions not made, and confidence scores.
  • Action register: every gap with an owner, target date and status.
  • Draft Crisis Decision Playbook (Data Integrity and Insider Edition) populated with the owners and rules agreed today.
  • Privileged access review: a list of roles that can change critical data without independent review, for Internal Audit and the CISO.
  • Insurance review: confirm how the cyber and crime policies interact for insider events.

Appendix B: Observer Checklist

  • Did leaders answer individually, or defer to the most senior person?
  • Did HR, Legal, Security and Internal Audit agree on who leads the investigation?
  • Was the employee treated fairly, without assuming guilt?
  • Was evidence preservation weighed deliberately against data correction?
  • Was independent oversight placed on the people recovering the data?
  • Were both insurers considered and notified?
  • Were affected workers treated as people who need help, not only as claims?
  • Was the person certifying the data independent of the people who had access?
