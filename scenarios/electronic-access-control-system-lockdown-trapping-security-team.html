Electronic Access Control System Lockdown Trapping Security Team

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines the failure of an electronic access control system (badge readers, door controllers and the management server) that triggers a facility-wide lockdown. Doors fail secure in several zones, and members of the security team, along with some staff, are trapped in controlled areas including a secure operations center, while other doors fail open and create a security exposure. The scenario tests how security, facilities and IT coordinate when the physical security system is itself the incident.

Participants are working-level: physical security operators, access control engineers, facilities and life safety staff, network and server operators, the service desk and application owners, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. Life safety and legal egress requirements frame every decision.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Westfield Data Services occupies a four-story campus with a 24-hour operations center, a data hall with mantraps and a security control room on the ground floor. The access control system consists of a central application and database server, 11 door controller panels in network closets and about 340 readers. Door hardware is a mix of fail-safe (unlocks on power loss) for egress doors and fail-secure for data hall, secure operations center and control room doors, with mechanical key overrides held in a key cabinet inside the control room.

At 03:30 on Sunday, a scheduled database maintenance job on the access control server runs an update script against an incorrect schema version. The system interprets a corrupted policy table as 'emergency lockdown' and pushes a lockdown state to all controllers. At 03:34, the server crashes on restart because of a license server dependency. The controllers hold the last state (lockdown) and are not accepting badge reads. Fail-secure doors remain locked; two security officers and three operations staff are in the control room and secure operations center; the key cabinet is locked with an electronic lock on the same system.

What the team does not yet know: some fail-safe doors on the second floor are held closed by a magnetic lock tied to the fire panel that did not release, the license server is a VM that is also down, the vendor's local override tool needs a laptop with a signed certificate that is in a vault accessible only via badge, and the offsite guard service has no up-to-date site plan.

Exercise Objectives

  • Prioritize life safety and egress. Participants confirm that no one is in danger, that legal egress requirements are met and that the fire alarm and evacuation procedures work, and decide when to involve emergency services.
  • Free trapped personnel. The team uses mechanical overrides, emergency release procedures and the fire service if necessary, and manages the key cabinet dependency.
  • Control the facility's security posture. Security decides how to protect the data hall and sensitive areas while doors fail open or locked, using guards, locks, logs and cameras.
  • Recover the access control system. The access control team restores the license server, repairs the policy table or restores from backup and brings controllers out of lockdown in a staged way.
  • Maintain operations in the facility. The team ensures that the operations center can continue its work, or hands over, and that staff can enter and leave safely.
  • Learn from design flaws. The team examines the key cabinet dependency, the license server, failure modes of doors and maintenance change control.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Life safety decisions follow your site emergency plan and local fire code. No simulated action may defeat emergency egress.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Alarms, trapped personnel and the lockdown prompt incident declaration.

T+00:20 — Life safety. Occupants are accounted for, egress paths are verified and trapped people are released.

T+01:00 — Security posture. Compensating security measures are in place for open and locked doors.

T+01:45 — System diagnosis. The policy table corruption and license dependency are identified.

T+03:00 — Recovery decision. Restore, repair or rebuild is chosen.

T+04:30 — Execution. Controllers are brought out of lockdown in a staged order.

T+06:00 — Validation. Doors, readers and logs are verified; security sweep is performed.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal access control resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: Lockdown state pushed to all doors and security staff are trapped

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 03:52. A security officer radios from the control room that the badge readers show red and the doors will not unlock. Two officers and three operations center staff are inside the secure areas; the exit door from the control room corridor is fail-secure and shows locked. The key cabinet on the control room wall is also locked electronically.

The access control server console is unresponsive after a crash during a database maintenance job at 03:30. Event logs show a policy table update that failed and a license check error. The controllers show 'LOCKDOWN ACTIVE' on their local status.

A staff member on the second floor reports that the fire stairs door is held closed and does not release. The facilities duty manager says the magnetic locks are connected to the fire panel and should release on alarm, but a test indicates that the release relay is not responding.

Facilitator provides: the camera view descriptions, the list of five trapped personnel, the access control server log and the door hardware list.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and set life safety as the first priority.
  • Decide whether to call the fire service for assistance.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Physical Security Operations & Control Room
  • Account for all personnel and their locations.
  • Deploy available guards to key points.
  • Establish a manual log.
Access Control System Engineering
  • Describe the state of the system and why the doors are locked.
  • Plan to restore from backup or to apply a local override.
  • Contact the vendor.
Facilities & Life Safety
  • Verify life safety egress and the fire panel interlock.
  • Fix the second-floor mag lock release.
  • Locate mechanical keys and force-entry tools.
Network & Server Operations
  • Check the license server VM and database state.
  • Isolate the access control server to preserve logs.
  • Provide network paths.
Service Desk & Application Owners
  • Service desk: handle staff calls.
  • Operations center: decide whether to continue or hand over.
  • List critical staff.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Activate continuity plan and consider handover.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about egress compliance.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What is your first action, and who authorizes forcing a door or calling emergency services?
  2. How do you free people from a control room whose key cabinet is locked by the same system?
  3. Which doors fail safe, which fail secure, and where are the exceptions?
  4. How do you protect the data hall while doors are in uncertain states?
  5. What are the risks of restoring the access database from backup, including stale permissions?
  6. How does the license server dependency affect recovery?
  7. Can the operations center continue and who decides?
  8. What should staff outside the building be told?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the mag lock fault until facilities test the second-floor door.
  • If players focus on IT recovery first, redirect them to life safety.
  • Note whether the team knows how to open the key cabinet mechanically.
  • Time-box to 30 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes life safety and approves emergency measures such as calling the fire service for forced entry.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Decides on engaging emergency guard services and on the level of facility closure.
  • Approves the staged recovery plan.

Physical Security Operations & Control Room

  • Accounts for all personnel and confirms the locations of those trapped using cameras and radios.
  • Organizes mechanical key access, guard deployment and patrols of exposed doors.
  • Maintains a log of all door overrides and events manually.
  • Coordinates with law enforcement and the fire service if needed.

Access Control System Engineering

  • Diagnoses the corrupted policy table, the license server dependency and the controller state.
  • Retrieves a backup of the database and plans restore, and verifies which backup is current.
  • Plans the staged release of controllers from lockdown and identifies doors that must be tested first.
  • Contacts the vendor for the local override tool and advice.

Facilities & Life Safety

  • Verifies fire panel interlocks, magnetic locks and emergency release devices, and ensures life safety egress.
  • Identifies fail-safe doors that did not release, such as the second-floor mag locks, and corrects them.
  • Provides tools and personnel to force or open doors if necessary.
  • Checks the key cabinet and alternate keys.

Network & Server Operations

  • Checks network and server state for the access control system, the license server VM and database.
  • Restores the license server and verifies network paths to controllers.
  • Preserves logs and checks for unauthorized changes.
  • Restricts access to the access control server during recovery.

Service Desk & Application Owners

  • The service desk handles calls from staff locked in or out of areas.
  • Application owners (operations center) determine whether the operations center can run with staff trapped inside or must hand over.
  • Owners identify critical staff who need entry.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity plans and, if needed, hands over operations center duties to the secondary site.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership.
  • Coordinates staffing and visitor handling.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 7.1 physical security perimeters, 7.2 physical entry; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Evaluates life safety and legal compliance, including egress and fire code.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and overrides.
  • Logs findings on key cabinet design, maintenance change control and failure modes.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Physical security systems are life-safety adjacent. Design failure modes deliberately: egress doors fail safe, high-security doors fail secure with mechanical override, and no one is ever trapped by the loss of a server.

Remove circular dependencies: keys to override doors must not depend on the access control system, and vendor tools, licenses and certificates must be available offline.

Control maintenance: test schema changes in staging, require backups and rollback, and make lockdown a deliberate, authenticated action rather than a possible side effect of corrupted policy.

Keep a tested manual procedure: guard assignments, key control, site plans for emergency services and a way to log entries when systems are down.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (7.1, 7.2, 7.4 physical security monitoring) and SOC 2 expect evidence of physical access control, review of access rights and tested continuity.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on life safety decisions and the dependency between overrides and the access system.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly were all personnel located and what tools helped?
  2. What mechanical overrides exist and who can use them?
  3. Why was the key cabinet dependent on the access system, and what changes?
  4. Which doors failed safe or secure and what exceptions did you find?
  5. How did the team protect the data hall and sensitive areas?
  6. What did the fire service need from you?
  7. How was the lockdown state triggered and how will it be prevented?
  8. How old was the backup and what permissions risks arose from restoring it?
  9. How did the license server dependency delay recovery?
  10. What did the operations center do and was handover considered?
  11. How accurate were communications to leadership and staff?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. What manual logging did the guards perform?
  14. What access or credentials were missing?
  15. What would you change in maintenance change control?
