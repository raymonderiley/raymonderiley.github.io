Cross-Cloud Backup Verification Failure Between AWS Backup and Azure Recovery Services

Hybrid Cloud (AWS & Azure)

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a discovery that backups reported as successful in AWS Backup and Azure Recovery Services cannot be restored because of encryption key changes and application inconsistency, found during a restore request after a data corruption event.

Participants are working-level: backup and recovery engineers, cloud platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS and Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of restore testing, key management, application-consistent backups and recovery point accounting.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Kestrel Wealth uses AWS Backup for databases and file systems in AWS and Azure Recovery Services for VMs and SQL in Azure. Dashboards show 99 percent job success. Restore tests are performed annually on a small sample. Backups are encrypted with customer managed keys that were rotated last quarter.

At 10:00 on Tuesday, a faulty release corrupts client portfolio records in both clouds. The team requests a restore from the previous night. In AWS, restores fail with key access errors because the vault key policy was altered during rotation. In Azure, SQL restores succeed but the application reports inconsistent data because the backups were crash-consistent without application quiescing. Advisors cannot see correct portfolios.

What the team does not yet know: backups for the last 3 weeks may share the key issue, older restore points have uncertain consistency, the corruption spread over 6 hours before detection, and clients are asking about positions.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize unproven backups. Participants understand job success is not recoverability.
  • Find a usable recovery point. The team tests restore points to find one that is both accessible and consistent.
  • Fix key access. Engineers repair key policies safely.
  • Plan recovery with data loss. Participants decide how to rebuild data from a point and from transaction logs.
  • Protect clients. The team communicates carefully about positions and trades.
  • Improve backup assurance. Participants review restore testing, key governance and consistency settings.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Do not announce a recovery point as usable until a restore from it has been tested and validated.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Failed restores and corrupted records lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Restore testing begins and priority set.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Key and consistency issues are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery and loss plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Recovery proceeds in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Records stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Data is validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes recoverability proven by restore, not by job status and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Backup & Recovery Engineering

  • Tests restore points in sequence and records results.
  • Repairs vault key policies.
  • Plans point-in-time recovery from logs.
  • Plans application-consistent backup settings.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Platform Engineering (AWS & Azure)

  • Validates restored systems in isolation.
  • Prepares clean rooms for validation.
  • Checks capacity for restores.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners define validation checks for portfolios.
  • The service desk logs advisor issues.
  • Owners rank clients and accounts.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Watches for further corruption.
  • Builds a recovery point status view.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Adds restore test alerts.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

AWS, Microsoft & Carrier Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with AWS and Microsoft on restore errors.
  • Asks about key policy recovery.
  • Requests guidance on application-consistent backups.
  • Escalates through both TAMs.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for advisory operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.30, 8.13 information backup, 8.24 cryptography; SOC 2 availability and processing integrity).
  • Assesses client record integrity and recoverability.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Measure recoverability by automated and regular restore tests, not job success.

Control and monitor key policies for backup vaults.

Use application-consistent backups for transactional systems.

Maintain isolated validation environments.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.13, 8.24, 5.30) and SOC 2 expect restore test evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on restore evidence before declaring recovery points usable.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

  2. How did you repair key access?
  3. How did you reach consistency?
  4. How did you speak to clients?
  5. How did you decide how far back to go?
  6. What assurance do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AWS, Microsoft & Carrier Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
