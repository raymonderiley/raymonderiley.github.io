GCP Google Kubernetes Engine (GKE) Autopilot Node Provisioning Freeze

Google Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a GKE Autopilot cluster that stops provisioning nodes during a demand spike because of a zonal capacity shortage and a misconfigured resource request, leaving pods Pending, autoscaling stalled and services degraded.

Participants are working-level: Kubernetes platform engineers, application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Google Cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of Autopilot scheduling, resource requests, compute classes, quotas, pod disruption budgets and priority.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Merrow Streaming serves video metadata and playback APIs from a regional GKE Autopilot cluster in europe-west1. Workloads request resources through deployments, and a horizontal pod autoscaler scales on CPU. A new spot compute class was introduced last week to reduce cost.

At 19:00 on Friday, a major premiere drives traffic to three times normal. The autoscaler requests new pods but Autopilot fails to provision nodes: events show 'GCE out of resources' in two zones and a recent change set a very large GPU-less memory request on the playback service. Pods remain Pending, latency rises from 120 ms to 4 seconds and the error rate reaches 20 percent.

What the team does not yet know: the spot compute class is being reclaimed under load, a regional quota for CPUs is close to its limit, the large memory request makes pods unschedulable in the remaining zone, and a rollback of the request change would restart all playback pods at once.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a provisioning freeze. Participants read events, pending pods and quota metrics to see that scheduling, not application code, is the bottleneck.
  • Free schedulable capacity. The team adjusts requests, priorities and compute classes to get pods running.
  • Protect the premiere. Engineers prioritize playback and shed lower-value workloads.
  • Roll out changes safely. Participants avoid restarting all pods at once and use budgets and surge settings.
  • Engage the provider. The team escalates capacity and quota issues with precise evidence.
  • Improve cluster resilience. Participants review multi-zone design, requests discipline, quotas and load tests.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Name the exact workload, request value or class being changed before any scheduling change.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Pending pods and rising errors lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Critical workloads are prioritized and requests are reviewed.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Capacity, quota and request causes are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A scheduling and rollout plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Changes are applied in controlled waves.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Latency and pod counts stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Services and cost are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: Pods Pending during the premiere

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 19:20. Hundreds of playback pods are Pending. Autopilot events show provisioning failures in two zones. Latency is 4 seconds.

The platform engineer finds a deployment change last week set a large memory request on playback pods. The spot class is being reclaimed. CPU quota is at 92 percent.

Rolling back the request change restarts all playback pods. The premiere peak is expected in 30 minutes.

Facilitator provides: pod events, quota table, deployment diff and zone status.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide on shedding workloads.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Kubernetes Platform Engineering (GKE)
  • Check quota and zones.
  • Plan class changes.
  • Plan priority.
Application Engineering
  • Review the request change.
  • Plan waves.
  • Scale down low value.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank services.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Agree degraded modes.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Watch quotas.
  • Preserve events.
Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about capacity.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan viewer messaging.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about SLA obligations.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you confirm scheduling, not code, is the problem?
  2. What can free capacity fastest?
  3. How does a large request affect schedulability?
  4. Which workloads can you shed?
  5. How do you roll out a fix without restarting everything?
  6. What does Google need to see in a case?
  7. What do viewers hear?
  8. Why did a request change pass review?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the quota level until T1 checks quotas.
  • If players restart all pods, ask about the peak.
  • Look for priorities first.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes customer-facing workloads and orderly scaling and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Kubernetes Platform Engineering (GKE)

  • Reads events, pending pods, quotas and zone status.
  • Plans compute class changes and quota requests.
  • Sets priority classes for critical workloads.
  • Plans cluster configuration changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Engineering

  • Reviews the resource request change and tests a correction.
  • Rolls out in small waves with surge limits.
  • Scales down low-value services.
  • Validates playback.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank services by premiere importance.
  • The service desk logs viewer contacts.
  • Owners agree degraded modes.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of pending pods, nodes, latency and errors.
  • Watches quotas and the status page.
  • Preserves events.
  • Adds scheduling alerts.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on zonal capacity and provisioning.
  • Asks about quota increases and alternate zones.
  • Requests guidance on compute classes.
  • Escalates through the TAM.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for streaming playback and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.6 capacity management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Assesses service availability during the premiere.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Plan capacity for known peaks: pre-scale, check quotas ahead and keep critical paths off preemptible classes.

Guard resource requests with policy checks and review so one deployment change cannot make pods unschedulable.

Design across zones and, for critical services, across regions with tested failover.

Alert on pending pods and provisioning errors early.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.6, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect capacity planning, load test and change evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on requests discipline and capacity planning.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you see scheduling as the cause?
  2. What freed capacity fastest?
  3. How did you handle quota?
  4. How did you roll out the fix?
  5. What did you shed and why?
  6. How did spot reclamation affect you?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the Google Cloud Support & TAM Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
