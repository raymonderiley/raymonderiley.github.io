AWS Redshift Cluster Node Failure During Complex ETL Batch

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Redshift cluster that loses a compute node during the nightly financial ETL batch. Queries abort, the cluster enters a recovery or resize state, transactions roll back, and downstream reports for the morning close are late or wrong.

Participants are working-level: data platform engineers, ETL and data engineers, business data consumers, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of Redshift node replacement, snapshots, WLM, transaction behavior and data validation.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Granite Mutual runs a Redshift RA3 cluster with eight nodes as its finance and risk warehouse. A nightly ETL batch built with Glue and stored procedures loads general ledger, claims and reserve tables starting at 01:00, with reports due to finance leadership and an actuarial team by 07:00. Automated snapshots are taken every eight hours and a manual snapshot is taken weekly. The cluster is single-AZ.

At 03:40, a compute node fails its health check. Redshift begins replacing the node and the cluster status changes to 'hardware-failure' then 'modifying'. Running queries abort, including a long multi-statement load into the reserves table. Stored procedure commits are partially applied because the ETL runs in autocommit for some steps. The retry logic in the orchestrator restarts the whole batch from the beginning, loading the first tables a second time.

What the team does not yet know: some tables now contain duplicate rows from the restarted batch, the failed step left a partial load in a staging table, the last automatic snapshot is five hours old, downstream BI tools cache results from the earlier partial run and the maintenance window was scheduled for the same night.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize and assess a node failure. Participants read cluster events, STL and SVL system tables, and CloudWatch metrics to understand status and expected recovery.
  • Prevent damage from automatic retries. The team stops the orchestrator, identifies partial and duplicate loads and avoids compounding errors.
  • Decide on recovery path. Engineers weigh waiting for node replacement, restoring from snapshot, or rebuilding tables and the data loss or time each involves.
  • Validate data integrity. Participants design reconciliation checks against source systems and control totals before releasing reports.
  • Manage business deadlines. The team communicates what will be late, what can be provisionally delivered and how it is labeled.
  • Improve resilience. Participants review batch idempotency, snapshots, Multi-AZ options and alerts.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Reference actual data controls such as row counts and control totals when proposing validation.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. The failed batch and cluster events lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. The orchestrator is paused and the cluster state is confirmed.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Partial and duplicate loads are identified.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery and validation plan is chosen.

T+03:00 — Execution. Recovery and reload are executed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Cluster is stable and reports are regenerated.

T+06:00 — Validation. Reconciliation results are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Reports are released and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Node failure aborts the ETL and the batch restarts

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 04:10. The on-call data engineer sees the cluster status 'hardware-failure' and then 'modifying'. CloudWatch shows one node unhealthy. The ETL orchestrator alerts that step 14 (reserves load) failed and automatically restarted the entire batch at 03:55.

The data platform engineer finds aborted queries in system tables and a partial load in the staging reserves table. Row counts for the general ledger fact table are about double the source count. The last automatic snapshot is from 23:00.

Redshift estimates replacement will take 20 to 40 minutes. Finance leadership expects the close dashboard at 07:00, and a maintenance window for patching starts at 05:00.

Facilitator provides: cluster event log, query abort list, row count comparison and snapshot list.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide to pause the orchestrator and the maintenance window.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Data Platform Engineering (Redshift)
  • Confirm cluster state and expected recovery.
  • Assess snapshot restore versus waiting.
  • Postpone patching.
Data Engineering & ETL Owners
  • Pause the orchestrator and identify duplicated and partial loads.
  • Explain autocommit and partial writes.
  • Plan idempotent reload.
Business Data Consumers & Service Desk
  • State report priorities and tolerance.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Plan provisional labeling.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Confirm notifications.
  • List missing alarms.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about root cause.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform finance leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for deadlines.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about financial reporting impact.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you stop the batch from doing more damage?
  2. How do you find which tables are duplicated or partial?
  3. Wait for node replacement or restore from snapshot, and how do you decide?
  4. What does a node failure mean for committed versus in-flight transactions?
  5. How do you validate data before releasing reports?
  6. What can be provisionally delivered at 07:00 and how is it labeled?
  7. Should the maintenance window proceed?
  8. What do finance leaders need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the duplicate rows until the ETL lead compares counts to source.
  • If players restart the batch, ask what happens to the duplicates.
  • Look for control totals as the validation approach.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes data correctness over speed and sets the reporting deadline plan.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes restore from snapshot or table rebuilds.
  • Decides on provisional report release.

Data Platform Engineering (Redshift)

  • Checks cluster events and node replacement status, and system tables for aborted queries.
  • Evaluates snapshot restore versus waiting and the maintenance implications.
  • Plans WLM and concurrency adjustments for the reload.
  • Checks cluster health and storage after recovery.

Data Engineering & ETL Owners

  • Pauses the orchestrator and identifies which steps completed, failed or ran twice.
  • Builds queries to find duplicates and partial loads.
  • Rewrites steps to be idempotent for the reload.
  • Prepares control total checks against source systems.

Business Data Consumers & Service Desk

  • Finance and actuarial leads state which reports are critical and what accuracy is needed.
  • The service desk handles user questions about stale or missing reports.
  • Consumers agree on provisional report labeling.
  • Prepare validation sign-off.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of cluster status, query aborts, disk space and WLM queues.
  • Confirms event notifications are working.
  • Checks AWS Health.
  • Adds alarms for node health and batch duration.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a case on the node failure and expected recovery time.
  • Asks about root cause and snapshot options.
  • Requests guidance on Multi-AZ.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for the month-end close and informs finance leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual workarounds.
  • Tracks against reporting deadlines.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.13 backup, 8.14 redundancy; SOC 2 availability and processing integrity).
  • Assesses financial reporting and audit implications.
  • Captures evidence of data validation.
  • Logs findings on batch design and recovery.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Make batches idempotent and restartable from checkpoints. Use transactions deliberately, avoid autocommit in multi-step loads, and design retries that cannot double-load.

Plan for node failure and cluster loss: snapshot cadence matched to RPO, cross-region snapshot copy, evaluated Multi-AZ options and tested restores.

Build validation into the pipeline: control totals, row counts and reconciliation to source before publishing, with provisional labeling when checks fail.

Align maintenance windows with batch schedules and use alarms for node health, disk and batch duration.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.13, 8.14) and SOC 2 expect tested restores, data integrity controls and documented sign-off.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on idempotency and data validation.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you stop the orchestrator and what slowed you?
  2. How did you identify duplicate and partial loads?
  3. How did you choose between waiting and restoring?
  4. What data would a restore have lost?
  5. How was disk pressure handled?
  6. How were data mismatches investigated?
  7. How did you handle cached BI results?
  8. What was provisional and how was it labeled?
  9. How accurate were updates to finance leadership?
  10. What did AWS support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How are restores tested?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. How will retries be redesigned?
  15. What would you change in batch design?
