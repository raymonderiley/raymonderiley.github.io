Cloud-Native Zero-Trust Policy Engine Denial of Service

Multi-Cloud (AWS & Azure, No On-Prem)

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a zero-trust policy decision engine, used by services in AWS and Azure to authorize every request, that is overwhelmed by a policy bundle growth and a request spike, so decisions time out and services either deny everything or fail open.

Participants are working-level: security engineers, cloud platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and monitoring staff, the AWS and Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of policy decision points, caching, fail-open versus fail-closed, bundle management and emergency policy rollback.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Thornbury Health Platform authorizes API calls through a central policy engine, deployed in both clouds, with services calling it for each request. Policies are distributed as bundles. A recent update tripled bundle size by adding a large attribute data set and a complex rule. Services have a 100 ms decision timeout; some fail closed and some fail open depending on team defaults.

At 09:00 on Monday, clinic opening hours, request volume peaks. The policy engine's CPU saturates because each decision scans the large data set. Decision latency exceeds timeouts. Services that fail closed deny all requests, including clinicians accessing patient records. Services that fail open allow requests without evaluation, including some that should be denied. Monitoring shows a mix of 403s and unexpected allows.

What the team does not yet know: which services fail open versus closed, whether any unauthorized data access occurred in the fail-open window, rolling back the bundle needs a deployment during peak, and emergency clinical access procedures are untested.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize policy engine overload. Participants link denials and latency to the policy engine.
  • Restore decisions. The team rolls back the bundle and scales the engine.
  • Handle clinical access. Engineers enable an emergency clinical access path with logging.
  • Assess fail-open exposure. Participants review access during the unevaluated window.
  • Stabilize and tune. The team caches decisions and tunes policies safely.
  • Improve policy platform resilience. Participants review failure modes, bundle size and rollout.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Restore policy evaluation before relaxing any control, and log all emergency access with the user, record and reason.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Denials, latency and fail-open behavior lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. The bundle is rolled back and emergency access enabled.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Overload and exposure are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A stabilization and review plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Changes are applied in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Decisions stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Access and logs are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: The policy engine is overloaded: clinicians are denied while others are allowed unchecked

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 09:30. Clinicians cannot open patient records. Some services return 403s while others allow requests unchecked.

The security engineer finds the policy engine saturated after a bundle tripled in size. Decisions exceed timeouts.

Services have inconsistent fail-open and fail-closed defaults. Emergency clinical access is untested.

Facilitator provides: engine metrics, decision latency graphs, bundle diff and service failure mode list.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize rolling back the bundle.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Security Engineering (Policy Platform)
  • Roll back.
  • Scale.
  • Identify failure modes.
Cloud Platform Engineering (AWS & Azure)
  • Deploy in stages.
  • Validate.
  • Handle capacity.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Define emergency access.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Confirm services.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Review fail-open window.
  • Monitor emergency access.
  • Preserve logs.
AWS & Microsoft Support Liaison
  • Open cases.
  • Ask about scaling.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan clinic notice.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about access control duties.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you distinguish denials from engine failure?
  2. What do you restore first?
  3. How do you give clinicians access safely?
  4. What happened during the fail-open window?
  5. How do you stage the rollback?
  6. What do clinics hear?
  7. Why did services have different failure defaults?
  8. What should the engine limit?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the fail-open exposure until OPS reviews logs.
  • If players disable policy checks everywhere, ask about patient privacy.
  • Look for restore-before-relax.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes policy decisions that are fast, correct and available and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Security Engineering (Policy Platform)

  • Rolls back the policy bundle and scales engines.
  • Identifies services that fail open or closed.
  • Plans decision caching.
  • Plans bundle size limits.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Cloud Platform Engineering (AWS & Azure)

  • Deploys the rollback in both clouds in stages.
  • Validates service behavior.
  • Handles capacity.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners and clinicians define emergency access needs.
  • The service desk logs access denials.
  • Owners confirm critical services.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Reviews access during the fail-open window for unauthorized access.
  • Monitors emergency access use.
  • Preserves logs.
  • Adds policy latency and fail-open alerts.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

AWS & Microsoft Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with AWS and Microsoft on compute capacity.
  • Asks about autoscaling limits.
  • Requests guidance on latency.
  • Escalates through both TAMs and the policy engine vendor.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for clinic operations and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 5.15 access control, 8.3 information access restriction, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses access control integrity and clinical availability.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Set bundle size and decision latency budgets and load test policy changes before rollout.

Cache decisions where safe and standardize failure behavior by risk tier.

Provide a tested, logged emergency access path for critical clinical use.

Alert on policy latency and fail-open events.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (5.15, 8.3, 8.32) and SOC 2 expect access control and change management evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on restore-before-relax and logged emergency access.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you recognize engine overload?
  2. What did you restore first?
  3. How did you enable emergency access?
  4. How did you assess the fail-open window?
  5. How did you stage the rollback?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AWS & Microsoft Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
