NTP Drift Causing Kerberos Ticket Validation Failures Enterprise-Wide

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an enterprise-wide time synchronization failure: the authoritative NTP source drifts and propagates incorrect time through the domain hierarchy, so clients and servers fall outside Kerberos' five-minute clock skew tolerance and authentication fails across the estate. Certificates, logs, scheduled jobs, database replication and cluster heartbeats are all affected, and the symptoms look like many unrelated problems.

Participants are working-level: directory services engineers, time service and network engineers, server and virtualization operators, application owners, the service desk, security and PKI staff, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The scenario highlights a dependency nobody monitors and that is rarely documented.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Delta Ridge Energy runs a single-forest Active Directory, with the PDC emulator of the forest root domain acting as the authoritative time source. It is configured to sync from two external NTP pool servers through a firewall, with a GPS-based Stratum 1 appliance in the data center intended as the preferred source but whose configuration was never completed. Virtual machines use a mix of host time sync (VMware Tools) and domain time sync, and a number of Linux hosts use chrony pointing at the domain controllers.

At 06:20 on Tuesday, a firewall policy update blocks outbound UDP 123, the PDC emulator loses its upstream sources, falls back to its local CMOS clock which had a 9-minute offset from a bad battery, and its time source flags are changed to 'Local CMOS Clock'. Domain controllers and member servers begin to follow the PDC emulator and shift by minutes over the next hours; at the same time the VMware tools periodic sync pushes the host time (accurate) back into guests, causing time to jump back and forth. By 07:45, Kerberos errors (KRB_AP_ERR_SKEW, Event ID 4 and 5) appear across the estate; at 08:10 the first shift handover of the control-room systems begins and operators cannot log in.

What the team does not yet know: the CMOS clock error is 9 minutes and drifting, the GPS appliance is powered but unconfigured, the SCADA historian timestamps data from the local clocks, certificate validation fails for certificates that appear not yet valid, and database transaction logs have entries out of order.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize time as the common cause. Participants connect diverse symptoms (login failures, certificate errors, job failures, cluster instability) to time skew by using w32tm /query /status, /monitor and /stripchart, and Event IDs 4, 5, 22, 36, 50 and 142.
  • Re-establish an authoritative time source. The team determines the correct time, selects and configures the authoritative source (GPS appliance, corrected firewall path or a trusted reference), and plans how to propagate it safely.
  • Avoid making things worse. Participants understand the risks of large time corrections: abrupt jumps affecting databases, clusters, Kerberos tickets and logs, and decide how to step or slew time.
  • Protect operations and data integrity. The team addresses operational technology systems, historian data, database logs and audit trails affected by erroneous timestamps.
  • Restore authentication and dependent services. The team prioritizes domain controllers, control-room access and critical applications, and handles cached tickets and certificate validity.
  • Prevent recurrence. Participants examine monitoring of time drift, source redundancy and VM time synchronization design.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Use actual commands and settings where you know them; where nobody can say how time is configured on a platform, record it as a gap.

Exercise Timelines

T+00:30 — Pattern recognition. Time skew is identified as the common cause.

T+01:15 — Source of truth. The correct time and authoritative source are established.

T+03:00 — Execution. PDC emulator, domain controllers and servers are corrected.

T+06:00 — Validation. Authentication, certificate, logs and job schedules are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Services released and incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Control-room logins fail and Kerberos errors spread

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 08:15. The control-room supervisor reports that operators cannot log in at shift handover. The service desk has 180 tickets with messages such as 'The security database on the server does not have a computer account for this workstation trust relationship', 'There is a time difference between the client and server', and certificate errors in browsers.

A directory engineer runs w32tm /query /status on the PDC emulator and sees the source listed as 'Local CMOS Clock' and a stratum of 1. Domain controllers show offsets from 4 to 11 minutes depending on the host. Event ID 4 (Kerberos) is flooding several servers.

The monitoring system itself shows odd timestamps, and a database cluster reports repeated failovers. The VMware environment has periodic time sync enabled on many guests, causing time to jump back after the domain time pushes forward.

Facilitator provides: the w32tm output, the event log samples, the offset table for six domain controllers and the firewall change record.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name leads for directory, time and communications.
  • Decide how control-room operations continue without logins.
  • Set the next update at T+00:30.
Identity & Directory Services (Active Directory)
  • Describe what the w32tm output tells you and which host is the authoritative source.
  • Check which domain controllers are skewed and by how much.
  • Recommend whether to widen skew tolerance temporarily.
Network Time Services & Network Engineering
  • Find the correct time and check the GPS appliance status.
  • Check the firewall change that blocked UDP 123.
  • Plan configuration of the authoritative source.
Server & Virtualization Operations
  • Identify VM time sync settings and conflicts.
  • Check ESXi host time sources.
  • Plan to correct guests without jumps.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • List the systems failing and rank them.
  • Service desk: issue guidance and use local accounts for control room if allowed.
  • Operations owner: describe manual procedures.
Security Operations & PKI
  • Assess whether the failure could be an attack.
  • Check certificate validity and logs.
  • Record timestamps accurately.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Activate the control-room continuity plan.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask whether time sources are documented and monitored.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What links the login failures, certificate errors and cluster failovers, and what single check proves it?
  2. What is the correct time and how do you know, given your sources are suspect?
  3. What are the risks of stepping time by 9 minutes on domain controllers, databases and clusters?
  4. What is the right order for correcting the PDC emulator, domain controllers and members?
  5. How do VM host time sync and domain time interact, and what is your policy?
  6. How will the control room operate while authentication is failing?
  7. What damage could incorrect timestamps do to historian and audit data?
  8. What should operations be told right now?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the firewall change until the network team is asked about outbound UDP 123.
  • If players want to step time immediately, ask about databases, clusters and historian effects.
  • Look for awareness that logs and evidence from the incident window may have shifted timestamps.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, sets priorities (control-room access, domain controllers, critical applications) and approves the correction plan.
  • Runs a 30-minute cadence, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Decides on the risk of a large time step during operating hours.
  • Authorizes emergency firewall changes.

Identity & Directory Services (Active Directory)

  • Verifies Kerberos failures and time source flags on the PDC emulator and domain controllers using w32tm.
  • Plans the correction of the PDC emulator's time source and the sequence for domain controllers.
  • Decides whether to temporarily widen Kerberos skew tolerance by policy and the risks of that.
  • Checks for replication errors caused by time differences.

Network Time Services & Network Engineering

  • Determines the correct time from the GPS appliance or another trusted reference.
  • Finishes configuring the Stratum 1 appliance and verifies stratum, jitter and reachability.
  • Restores the firewall rule for UDP 123 and reviews the change that blocked it.
  • Provides a time-source map for network devices and Linux hosts.

Server & Virtualization Operations

  • Identifies VMs with host time sync enabled and the conflict with domain time.
  • Corrects hypervisor host time and disables periodic sync where required.
  • Plans a safe correction for clusters and database VMs without abrupt jumps.
  • Checks snapshots, restores and backup jobs for timestamp problems.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners report failures: ERP, historian, scheduling and authentication-dependent systems.
  • The service desk handles control-room login incidents and guidance.
  • Operations owners identify manual operating procedures for the control room.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Security Operations & PKI

  • Considers whether the time attack could be malicious, such as NTP spoofing or tampering with the firewall.
  • Assesses the validity of certificates, tokens and logs with incorrect timestamps.
  • Preserves logs and notes the time offset for forensic accuracy.
  • Verifies that the SIEM timestamps are consistent.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity plans for the control room and operations.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership and operations.
  • Coordinates manual logs and paper procedures.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.17 clock synchronization; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates regulatory implications for energy operations records and logs.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and corrected times.
  • Logs findings on time source design, monitoring and firewall change control.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Time is a dependency for authentication, certificates, clustering and forensics. Document the time hierarchy, use at least three independent upstream sources plus a GPS Stratum 1 appliance and monitor offset continuously with alerts well below the Kerberos tolerance.

Make sources redundant and tested: confirm firewall rules for UDP 123, protect the authoritative time hierarchy from drift to local clocks, and avoid configuring the PDC emulator to fall back to CMOS.

Define VM time policy: choose one authority (domain time or host sync), disable conflicting periodic sync, and verify after cloning, snapshots and restores.

Plan the correction procedure: step versus slew, order of operations and system-specific cautions for databases, clusters and operational technology.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.17) and SOC 2 expect evidence of clock synchronization configuration, monitoring and incident handling.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on how long it took to recognize time as the common cause and the order of corrections.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. Which symptom first suggested time and how long until it was named as the cause?
  2. What is your documented time hierarchy and was it correct?
  3. How did you determine correct time and what sources did you trust?
  4. What were the risks of stepping time and how did you decide step versus slew?
  5. How are VMs configured for time sync and how did that interact with domain time?
  6. How did the control room operate during the authentication failures?
  7. What was the effect on certificates and PKI?
  8. What happened to databases, clusters and the historian?
  9. How did the team handle Linux and network devices?
  10. What did security conclude about attack or error?
  11. How was the firewall change reviewed and who owns it?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. What monitoring should have caught the drift?
  14. What access or credentials were missing?
  15. What will you change about time design?
