Autonomous Red-Team AI Agent Hijacked by Threat Actors to Accelerate Payload Deployment

AI Failure & Autonomous Agent Disruption

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an internal autonomous red-team AI agent with broad network access that is hijacked through its control channel and used by an attacker to map the environment and deploy payloads faster than defenders can react.

Participants are working-level: offensive security engineers, security platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and threat hunting analysts, the AI platform and cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of test tool governance, kill switches, credential scope and distinguishing authorized from hostile activity.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Thornwell Credit Union deployed an autonomous red-team agent that runs scheduled attack simulations with a service account that has broad network and domain privileges. Defenders are told the schedule but not the specific techniques. The agent takes instructions from a management console reachable from the corporate network.

At 22:00 on Friday, the agent's activity appears off schedule. It maps servers, tests credentials and then begins copying tools to file servers, matching the usual simulation signature, so analysts initially assume it is the test. At 23:30, the agent deploys files unlike its normal simulation payloads and disables logging on three servers. The red team lead confirms no scheduled test is running.

What the team does not yet know: how the console was reached, what the agent's credentials can still do, whether payloads are inert or real, and how many hosts are touched.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize hijacked test tooling. Participants distinguish authorized simulation from hostile use.
  • Stop the agent. The team cuts the control channel and disables its credentials.
  • Scope activity. Engineers list hosts reached and files placed.
  • Remove payloads and restore logging. Participants clean hosts and verify logging.
  • Investigate the control compromise. The team traces access to the console.
  • Improve test tool governance. Participants review isolation, scoping and kill switches.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Disable the agent's credentials and control channel before analyzing, and confirm activity against the authorized test calendar with the red team lead before any dismissal of alerts.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Off-schedule activity and disabled logging lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Agent stopped and hosts contained.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Scope and control compromise are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A cleanup and review plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Cleanup proceeds in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Monitoring stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Hosts are validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes testing tools that cannot be turned into attack tools and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.

  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Give testing agents scoped, time-boxed credentials and isolate their control consoles.

Mark authorized test activity with unique tokens that defenders can verify and attackers cannot copy.

Maintain a kill switch and test it.

Never dismiss alerts as a test without confirmation from the test owner.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.2, 8.34, 8.16, 5.15) and SOC 2 expect privileged access and monitoring evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on disable-first and confirmed test status.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you tell test from attack?
  2. What did you disable first?
  3. How did you scope activity?
  4. How did you restore logging?
  5. What governance do you want?
  6. What markers do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
