Azure Storage Account Geo-Replication Lag During Ransomware Event

Azure Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a ransomware event in which an attacker encrypts and deletes blobs in a GRS or RA-GRS storage account. Geo-replication copies the damage to the secondary region, and the team discovers that replication lag, soft delete settings and missing immutability shape what can still be recovered.

Participants are working-level: storage and backup engineers, cloud security and identity engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and forensics staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of geo-replication behavior, blob versioning, soft delete, point-in-time restore, immutable storage and account failover.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Cormorant Healthcare Billing stores 180 TB of claims documents and exports in Azure Storage accounts configured as RA-GRS. Blob soft delete is enabled with a 7-day retention on one account and 1 day on another. Versioning is enabled on only some containers. Azure Backup for blobs covers a subset. Storage keys are used by a legacy export service and two scripts, and shared access signatures (SAS) were issued with long expiry.

At 00:30 on Saturday, an attacker with a leaked storage account key begins overwriting blobs with encrypted copies and deleting originals. Because RA-GRS replicates writes asynchronously, the secondary region receives the encrypted versions with a lag of about 15 minutes. By 01:15, 40 percent of blobs in the primary account are encrypted. An engineer proposes an account failover to the secondary to 'get the clean data', unaware that the secondary is receiving the same changes.

What the team does not yet know: the attacker also disabled soft delete retention on one account and purged some deleted blobs, the other account has versioning on, so earlier versions remain, the account failover would break the geo-redundancy and take hours, and the leaked key is also used by an export service that supports a payer deadline Monday.

Exercise Objectives

  • Understand what geo-replication does and does not protect. Participants explain why replication propagates corruption and how lag affects recovery.
  • Contain the attacker. The team rotates keys, revokes SAS tokens and restricts network access without breaking critical exports unnecessarily.
  • Identify recoverable data. Engineers inventory soft-deleted blobs, versions, point-in-time restore options and backups.
  • Choose a recovery path. Participants decide between version restore, soft-delete undelete, backup restore and account failover and understand the data loss each implies.
  • Handle regulatory obligations. The team assesses breach notification duties for protected health information.
  • Strengthen storage resilience. Participants review immutability, versioning, key disablement and monitoring.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Treat every storage key and SAS as compromised until rotated; do not propose recovery steps that rely on them.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Encrypted blobs and alerts lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Keys are rotated and the account is isolated.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Recoverable data sources are inventoried.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery plan and regulatory analysis are agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Restores are performed in a clean environment.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Data integrity and access are verified.

T+06:00 — Validation. Regulatory decisions are made.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Blobs encrypted and the secondary region mirrors the damage

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 01:30. Defender for Storage alerts on mass blob overwrites. Billing staff cannot open claim documents, many of which now have a ransom extension. The secondary region shows the same encrypted blobs with a lag of about 15 minutes.

The storage engineer finds soft delete disabled on one account and some deleted blobs purged. The other account has versioning on, so earlier versions exist. The attacker used a storage account key; SAS tokens with 90-day expiry also exist.

An engineer proposes account failover to the secondary. Azure Service Health shows no incident. The export service using the same key supports a Monday payer deadline.

Facilitator provides: storage logs, retention settings, backup coverage and the key usage summary.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize key rotation and isolation.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Storage & Backup Engineering
  • Explain why failover will not help.
  • Inventory versions, soft delete and backups.
  • Plan isolated restore.
Cloud Security & Identity Engineering
  • Rotate keys and revoke SAS.
  • Restrict network access.
  • Review key leakage.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Identify critical exports and deadlines.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Plan manual processes.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Analyze storage logs.
  • Preserve evidence.
  • Hunt for other accounts.
Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about recovery options.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform payer relations.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan manual submission.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Start regulatory clock analysis.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. Why does geo-replication not protect against this attack?
  2. How do you stop the attacker without breaking the export deadline?
  3. What recovery sources exist and which can you trust?
  4. Should you fail over the account and what would that do?
  5. How do you restore without reusing compromised keys?
  6. What does PHI exposure mean for notification?
  7. How do you verify restored data?
  8. What do leaders and payers need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the purged blobs until T1 checks soft delete retention.
  • If players fail over, ask what they gain.
  • Look for containment before recovery.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes containment and preserving recoverable data and sets priorities for payer deadlines.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes key rotation that may break exports and any account failover.
  • Decides on forensics and law enforcement.

Storage & Backup Engineering

  • Inventories soft-deleted blobs, versions and backups and checks retention settings.
  • Plans version restore and point-in-time restore in isolation.
  • Evaluates account failover and its consequences.
  • Validates restored data.

Cloud Security & Identity Engineering

  • Rotates keys, disables shared key access and revokes SAS tokens.
  • Restricts network access with firewalls and private endpoints.
  • Reviews how the key leaked.
  • Plans managed identity migration.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners identify critical exports and Monday deadlines.
  • The service desk logs billing staff reports.
  • Owners prepare manual processes.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Analyzes storage logs and Defender alerts for attacker activity and persistence.
  • Preserves evidence.
  • Hunts for other compromised accounts.
  • Sets alerts.

Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on recovery options and geo-replication state.
  • Asks about account failover impacts.
  • Requests help with soft-delete and purge events.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for billing operations and informs payer relations.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual claims submission.
  • Tracks deadlines.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.13 backup, 5.34 privacy; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses HIPAA and state notification obligations.
  • Captures evidence and chain of custody.
  • Logs findings on immutability.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Do not mistake replication for backup. Use versioning, soft delete with adequate retention, point-in-time restore and immutable (WORM) storage with legal hold or time-based retention for critical containers.

Remove shared key access: use Entra ID authorization and managed identities, short-lived user delegation SAS, network restrictions and private endpoints.

Detect and respond: Defender for Storage, alerts on mass deletes or overwrites and monitored logs.

Test recovery to a clean account, including integrity verification and reconciliation with databases.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.13, 5.34) and SOC 2 expect tested restores, immutability evidence and monitored access.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on replication versus backup and key hygiene.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you detect the attack and what showed it?
  2. Why did geo-replication not help?
  3. How did you stop the attacker and weigh the export deadline?
  4. What recovery sources existed and what was lost?
  5. How did you choose versions to restore?
  6. How did you handle the ransom demand?
  7. What did logs show about exfiltration?
  8. How were notification obligations assessed?
  9. How accurate were updates to leadership and payers?
  10. What did Microsoft support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How often are restores tested?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alarms are missing?
  15. What would you change in storage design?
