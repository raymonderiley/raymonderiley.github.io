AWS Backup Vault Immutable Lock Tampering and Restore Block

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an attack on backup integrity: an adversary with compromised cloud administrator credentials changes AWS Backup vault access policies, modifies lifecycle and retention, deletes recovery points that are not protected by Vault Lock, and attempts to block restores by altering KMS key policies and cross-account copy settings. The team must determine what backups remain trustworthy and whether recovery is possible.

Participants are working-level: backup and recovery engineers, cloud security and identity engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and forensics staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of Backup Vault Lock modes, logically air-gapped vaults, cross-account copies, KMS dependencies and recovery sequencing.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Cobalt Financial protects 600 production resources with AWS Backup in a central backup account. A primary vault in the production account has a lock in governance mode, a copy rule sends recovery points daily to a vault in a separate backup account, and a compliance vault has Vault Lock in compliance mode with a 35-day minimum retention but only receives monthly copies. Backup vaults use customer managed KMS keys.

At 01:15 on Saturday, the security team receives a GuardDuty finding for unusual API activity from an administrator role: iam:CreateAccessKey, backup:PutBackupVaultAccessPolicy, backup:DeleteBackupVaultLockConfiguration (governance mode) and backup:DeleteRecoveryPoint calls against the production vault. By 01:50, 18 days of recovery points in the production vault are gone, the KMS key policy for the vault keys has been changed to remove the backup service role, and the cross-account copy job has been disabled. In parallel, EC2 instances in production show signs of ransomware encryption.

What the team does not yet know: the central backup account's vault still has recent copies but its KMS key is owned by the production account's key policy, the compliance vault holds only monthly points up to 29 days old, the adversary may still hold valid credentials, and some database snapshots were also deleted outside AWS Backup.

Exercise Objectives

  • Detect and understand backup tampering. Participants correlate GuardDuty, CloudTrail and AWS Backup events to determine what the adversary changed and deleted.
  • Contain the adversary. The team revokes credentials, locks down roles and protects the surviving backups.
  • Inventory what is recoverable. Engineers list intact recovery points across vaults, accounts and regions, and verify KMS access and integrity.
  • Choose a recovery path. Participants decide between restoring from the cross-account vault, the compliance vault or rebuilding, accepting data loss defined by available points.
  • Restore keys and access safely. The team repairs KMS key policies and role access without trusting compromised identities.
  • Strengthen backup architecture. Participants review Vault Lock compliance mode, logically air-gapped vaults, separate accounts, key ownership and monitoring.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Treat every credential and role in the affected account as untrusted until verified; do not recover using identities that may be compromised.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. The GuardDuty finding and missing recovery points lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Credentials are revoked and surviving backups are protected.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. The tampering scope and recoverable points are inventoried.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery and key access plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Clean recovery is executed in an isolated environment.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Restored systems are validated and hardened.

T+06:00 — Validation. Data loss and integrity are assessed.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Services return and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Recovery points deleted and vault lock removed

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 02:00. A GuardDuty finding shows unusual activity from an administrator role. Security Operations finds CloudTrail events that changed the production vault access policy, deleted the governance mode lock and deleted recovery points covering 18 days. Several production instances show signs of file encryption.

The backup engineer sees the production vault now has only 12 days of points. The cross-account copy rule was disabled at 01:42. The central backup account's vault holds copies up to last night, but its KMS key policy references the production account.

The compliance vault holds monthly points up to 29 days old under compliance mode and cannot be altered. The adversary's credentials may still be active. The AWS Health Dashboard shows no events.

Facilitator provides: the GuardDuty finding, the CloudTrail event list, the vault inventory and the key policy summary.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize emergency containment of the administrator role.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Backup & Recovery Engineering (AWS Backup)
  • Inventory recovery points and lock states.
  • Verify the central vault copies and KMS access.
  • Plan isolated restore.
Cloud Security & Identity Engineering
  • Revoke the credentials and sessions.
  • Plan KMS policy repair using trusted roles.
  • Apply deny policies.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank systems for recovery.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Define acceptable data loss.
Security Operations & Forensics
  • Analyze CloudTrail for adversary actions.
  • Preserve evidence.
  • Hunt for persistence.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about deleted recovery points.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform business leaders.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan manual operations.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Start regulatory clock analysis.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What did the adversary change and in what order?
  2. Which backups do you still trust and why?
  3. How do you protect surviving backups while the adversary may have access?
  4. Why does compliance mode matter compared with governance mode?
  5. Which recovery path do you choose and what data loss does it imply?
  6. How do you repair KMS access without trusting the compromised role?
  7. How do you restore without reinfection?
  8. What do leaders and regulators need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the KMS key policy issue until BKP checks the central vault.
  • If players restore into the production account, ask about trust.
  • Look for containment before recovery.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes containment and preserving surviving backups and sets recovery priorities.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes use of break-glass identities and recovery decisions.
  • Decides on engagement of forensics and law enforcement.

Backup & Recovery Engineering (AWS Backup)

  • Inventories recovery points across all vaults, accounts and regions and verifies lock states.
  • Checks restore testing history and integrity of surviving points.
  • Plans restores into an isolated account and sequencing by priority.
  • Repairs vault policies and copy rules.

Cloud Security & Identity Engineering

  • Revokes keys and sessions, applies deny policies and rotates credentials.
  • Repairs KMS key policies using trusted break-glass roles.
  • Reviews how credentials were compromised.
  • Hardens roles and enables protections.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank systems for recovery and define acceptable data loss.
  • The service desk handles user reports.
  • Owners prepare validation tests.
  • Plan manual processes.

Security Operations & Forensics

  • Analyzes CloudTrail for the adversary's actions and persistence.
  • Preserves logs and evidence with integrity.
  • Hunts for other tampering and access paths.
  • Coordinates with forensics.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a high-severity case and engages the AWS security team.
  • Asks about recovery options for deleted recovery points and KMS issues.
  • Requests guidance on isolation.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for financial services and informs business leaders.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual operations and customer messaging.
  • Tracks the impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.13 backup, 8.15 logging; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses regulatory notification obligations.
  • Captures evidence and chain of custody.
  • Logs findings on backup immutability.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Make backups immutable and independent. Use Vault Lock in compliance mode, logically air-gapped vaults and copies in a separate account whose controls cannot be changed from production credentials.

Separate key ownership and trust: backup keys should be controlled in the backup account with policies that production administrators cannot alter.

Detect tampering: alarms on vault policy changes, lock changes, recovery point deletions and disabled copy rules, routed to a monitored on-call team.

Test recovery in a clean room with documented dependencies, integrity verification and sequence, including recovery when production identities are untrusted.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.13) and SOC 2 expect tested restores, immutability evidence and monitored backup controls.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on backup immutability and recovery without trusting compromised identities.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you detect the tampering and what showed it?
  2. What did the adversary change and in what order?
  3. How did you protect surviving backups?
  4. How did you decide which recovery points to trust?
  5. How did you handle KMS access?
  6. What did compliance mode versus governance mode mean here?
  7. How did you plan the clean room restore?
  8. How did you handle persistence in restored systems?
  9. How accurate were updates to leadership and regulators?
  10. What did AWS support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How often are restores tested?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alarms are missing?
  15. What would you change in backup architecture?
