SAN Array Dual Controller Firmware Deadlock During Peak Batch Processing

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines how an infrastructure organization responds when both controllers of a primary enterprise SAN array deadlock at the same moment because of a latent firmware defect, in the middle of the nightly peak batch window. Because both controllers are affected, the usual assumption that the second controller will absorb the load does not hold, and every host attached to the array loses its storage paths at once.

The exercise is built for working-level participants: storage engineers, database and batch operators, server and virtualization administrators, SAN fabric and network staff, the vendor liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. It tests how quickly the team distinguishes a controller deadlock from a fabric or host problem, whether runbooks cover a total array outage, how recovery sequencing protects data consistency, and how well vendor escalation works at 01:00.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours. The twelve-inject library maps to time frames with A/B variants, so the same scenario can be run repeatedly with different injects. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Harborline Financial Services runs its core transaction, general ledger and reporting systems on a pair of enterprise block storage arrays: ARRAY-A in the primary data center holds about 410 TB across flash and nearline tiers, and ARRAY-B in the secondary data center is the asynchronous replication target with a recovery point of roughly 15 minutes. ARRAY-A has two active-active controllers, firmware 9.4.2, patched to that level six weeks ago. Hosts connect through two dual-fabric Fibre Channel SANs with multipathing.

At 01:12, during the end-of-day settlement batch and the overnight ledger consolidation, the array begins logging internal queue timeouts. At 01:19, both controllers stop serving I/O within 40 seconds of each other. Multipath software on 180 hosts reports all paths dead. Databases freeze or crash, virtual machine datastores go offline, and the batch scheduler shows 62 jobs in a hung state. The array management interface is unreachable, and the front-end ports show link-up but no response.

What the team does not yet know: the firmware defect is triggered by a specific combination of a snapshot-delete operation and a large write burst, the vendor has an advisory that was issued three weeks ago but not triaged, the replicated copy on ARRAY-B is 22 minutes behind because of a link throttle that was set during a network maintenance, and some databases were mid-commit when the array froze.

Exercise Objectives

  • Differentiate a dual-controller deadlock from fabric, host or application causes. Participants use multipath state, array port logs, fabric switch counters (portshow, porterrshow, nameserver registration) and host HBA events to isolate the array as the fault domain quickly and avoid unnecessary fabric changes.
  • Protect data consistency before restoring service. The team decides how to treat databases and file systems that lost storage mid-write, and sequences restarts to avoid corruption, including crash recovery, log replay and consistency checks.
  • Decide between recovering ARRAY-A and failing over to ARRAY-B. Participants evaluate the 22-minute replication lag, application consistency groups, DNS and host re-pointing effort, and the loss of data from the lag against the risk of a prolonged outage.
  • Execute effective vendor escalation. The vendor liaison opens a severity-one case with the right evidence (support bundle, controller logs, firmware version, timeline) and the team agrees on what actions require vendor approval, such as controller reboot, failback or firmware rollback.
  • Prioritize business services for restoration. Participants rank the order of recovery using documented business impact analysis, including settlement, payments, ledger, regulatory reporting and customer channels.
  • Handle secondary effects. The team addresses hung batch dependencies, replication consistency, backup job failures, monitoring blind spots and the risk that other arrays at the same firmware level will deadlock.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Do not assume the vendor can fix the problem remotely or quickly. Use only the support terms and contact details that your organization actually holds.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Multipath alarms, database freezes and batch failures reach the on-call team. The incident is declared and the bridge opened.

T+00:30 — Fault domain isolation. Storage, SAN and server teams determine whether the fault is in the array, the fabric or the hosts.

T+01:15 — Cause and vendor engagement. The deadlock is identified, the vendor case is opened and the firmware advisory is discovered.

T+02:00 — Consistency assessment. Databases, virtual machines and file systems are assessed for corruption risk and recovery order.

T+03:00 — Recovery path decision. Recover ARRAY-A in place, fail over to ARRAY-B, or hybrid, with documented approvals.

T+04:30 — Execution and sequencing. Controller recovery or failover, host re-attachment, database recovery and batch restart are sequenced.

T+06:00 — Validation. Integrity checks, reconciliation of batch outputs, replication re-establishment and monitoring are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Services are released in priority order, stakeholders are informed and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Both controllers stop serving I/O during the settlement batch

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 01:19. The on-call storage engineer is paged by the monitoring system: 180 hosts report all paths down to ARRAY-A. The database team's pager shows the ledger database frozen and the settlement database crashed with I/O errors. 62 batch jobs are in a hung state and the scheduler has started to flag missed dependencies.

The array management GUI times out. Over the serial console, the storage engineer can see both controllers in a state that reports 'internal watchdog timeout' but not whether they will recover. The front-end ports show link-up but do not answer. Fabric switch port counters show no CRC or link errors increase in the last 30 minutes.

The vendor support portal shows a firmware advisory titled 'Controller watchdog timeout under concurrent snapshot delete and high write throughput' published three weeks ago for firmware 9.4.x. No one on the team has seen it. A snapshot cleanup job began at 01:08.

Facilitator provides: the multipath alert summary, a fabric port statistics screenshot, the array serial console excerpt, and the batch scheduler view with hung jobs.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and set severity. Name the technical lead, the scribe and the vendor liaison.
  • Decide immediately whether to freeze all storage change and the snapshot and replication policies.
  • Set the next update for T+00:30 and the escalation threshold to the CIO and the head of operations.
Storage Engineering
  • Confirm via serial console whether both controllers are deadlocked or one is rebooting.
  • Identify the snapshot cleanup job and check whether it can be stopped when the array returns.
  • Gather logs and the support bundle before anything is rebooted; if not possible, document why.
Database & Batch Operations
  • List databases that were mid-commit and the transaction log state at 01:19.
  • Hold the batch scheduler and flag all in-flight jobs.
  • Estimate crash recovery time for the ledger and settlement databases.
Server & Virtualization Operations
  • Pause automatic HA restarts and confirm which virtual machines are in all-paths-down state.
  • Check boot-from-SAN hosts, and whether any hosts have also panicked.
  • Prepare the list of hosts for rescanning in a controlled order.
Network & SAN Fabric Operations
  • Demonstrate that the fabric is healthy and the fault is on the array side, using port counters and name server entries.
  • Do not reset ports or switches; preserve the logs.
  • Check the status and throttle on the replication link to ARRAY-B and report the lag.
Vendor & Support Liaison
  • Open a severity-one case, provide serial numbers and the firmware level, and request the engineer on call.
  • Ask whether the advisory matches the symptoms and what is the recommended recovery.
  • Record the vendor's commitment on response time.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Identify which processes depend on the settlement batch and the ledger, and the next hard deadline.
  • Prepare the first stakeholder message and hold wording until facts are confirmed.
  • Ask for the documented RTO of the array and the settlement service.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record and capture the detection time and declaration time.
  • Ask whether the firmware advisory was in the vulnerability and patch management intake.
  • Start the evidence log of decisions, approvals and vendor communications.

Discussion questions

  1. Based on the evidence, what is your leading hypothesis? What would convince you that this is a fabric or host problem instead?
  2. Both controllers failed. Which of your documented procedures assumes one controller would survive, and what do you do instead?
  3. Is it safe to power cycle the controllers right now? What evidence would you lose and what write cache risk exists?
  4. What does your runbook say about stopping the snapshot cleanup job, and who can do it without a vendor?
  5. How do you handle the databases that were mid-commit, and which should be recovered first?
  6. The replicated copy on ARRAY-B appears to be 22 minutes behind. How would you verify this, and who decides to accept that data loss?
  7. Who owns triage of vendor advisories, and why did this one not reach the storage team?
  8. What should the first message to the business say, and what must it not claim?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the replication lag unless the team checks it. Note how long it takes players to ask.
  • If the team moves to failover too early, ask what evidence supports abandoning ARRAY-A before the vendor responds, and what is the failback plan.
  • If the team wants to reboot both controllers immediately, ask who decides and whether write cache is preserved. Many teams discover that this was never documented.
  • Time-box discussion to about 35 minutes and capture decisions and open questions on the flip chart.

Inject #2: Controller A reboots but Controller B stays hung

Time frame T+00:30 · Variant A

Situation

Controller A reboots and rejoins but reports a degraded write cache. Controller B is still unresponsive. Hosts begin seeing intermittent paths but I/O is failing with timeouts.

Role actions

Storage Engineering
  • Decide whether to run on a single controller and what performance and risk that implies.

  • Decide whether to rescan hosts now or wait.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, owns severity and the decision between recovering ARRAY-A and failing over to ARRAY-B, using the data loss and time estimates supplied by storage and database teams.
  • Runs a 30-minute update cadence, assigns a scribe and keeps a time-stamped decision log.
  • Authorizes any controller power cycle, which could destroy in-flight cache evidence and must be vendor-coordinated.
  • Freezes all other storage changes, including firmware updates on other arrays, until the defect is understood.

Storage Engineering

  • Reads array status from out-of-band paths: serial console, service processor, and the vendor remote support gateway, since the management GUI is unreachable.
  • Determines whether both controllers are deadlocked, whether write cache has been preserved by battery or flash-backed memory, and which operations (snapshot delete, replication, data reduction) were running at the time.
  • Collects the support bundle and logs before any reboot, and compares firmware version 9.4.2 against the vendor advisory.
  • Checks the state and age of replicated volumes on ARRAY-B and the consistency groups they belong to.

Database & Batch Operations

  • Identifies which databases and batch jobs were running at 01:19 and which were mid-commit, using the scheduler, transaction logs and checkpoint states.
  • Plans crash recovery order for the ledger, settlement and reporting databases, and decides whether to restore from log shipping or replicated copy.
  • Holds the batch scheduler so that no job restarts against unverified data.
  • Prepares reconciliation of the batch outputs after recovery, including partial file outputs.

Server & Virtualization Operations

  • Inventories hosts and datastores attached to ARRAY-A, including virtual machines in an all-paths-down or permanent-device-loss state.
  • Prevents automatic restart storms by pausing HA responses until the storage state is known.
  • Prepares to remount datastores or rescan paths in the order the incident commander approves.
  • Verifies which hosts have local boot disks and which boot from SAN.

Network & SAN Fabric Operations

  • Confirms the fabric is healthy: no ISL failure, zoning change or switch fault, using port statistics and name server registrations.
  • Provides a baseline comparison of the fabric before the incident to rule out a fabric cause and to support the vendor case.
  • Reconfigures zoning or paths for ARRAY-B access if failover is chosen, and checks the replication link and its throttle.
  • Ensures that no one resets fabric ports or hosts in a way that destroys evidence.

Vendor & Support Liaison

  • Opens a severity-one case with the correct contract, serial numbers and entitlement, and requests a named engineer and a recovery plan.
  • Records every vendor instruction, the time and who approved it.
  • Asks the vendor whether the defect is known, which workaround exists and whether other arrays at the same firmware level are at risk.
  • Obtains written confirmation before any controller reboot, rollback or hotfix is applied.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Identifies the business processes affected during the settlement window and the regulatory or customer deadlines at risk.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership, operations and customer support using approved wording.
  • Coordinates manual workarounds and customer notification, if required, with relationship managers.
  • Tracks actual downtime versus the documented RTO and RPO for each affected service.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is classified and recorded under the incident management process (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 controls 5.24 to 5.28) and logged for SOC 2 availability evidence.
  • Evaluates whether regulatory notification or customer contractual obligations are triggered.
  • Captures evidence of emergency change approvals and vendor communications.
  • Opens findings for any missed advisory triage, replication lag or untested failover.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Design for correlated failure, not just single component failure. Active-active controllers share firmware, so a defect can take out both at once. Treat the firmware level as a fault domain: stagger firmware versions across arrays where possible and never apply the same new firmware to primary and replica on the same day.

Make vendor advisories a managed intake. Assign an owner, a triage deadline and a risk rating, and record the decision to apply or defer. A three-week-old advisory that matched this outage exactly is a process failure, not a technology failure.

Know your real RPO. Measure replication lag continuously and alert on thresholds; throttles set during maintenance must expire automatically. A 15-minute design RPO that is silently 38 minutes is worse than a documented 60 minutes.

Prepare an out-of-band management path and a rehearsed recovery sequence for a full array outage: who may stop jobs, who may reboot controllers, who approves failover, and who verifies consistency before applications restart.

Test failover with application-level consistency, not just volume-level replication. Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and SOC 2 will look for evidence of restore and failover tests and of vendor patch management.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Pay specific attention to the time to isolate the array as the fault domain and to the decision on controller reboot.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How long did it take to establish that the fault was the array and not the fabric? What data convinced you and what was missing?
  2. Did the team know the replication lag before it was asked, and how is lag monitored today?
  3. Who has the authority to approve a controller power cycle and was that authority exercised within your documented time?
  4. Was the firmware advisory known to anyone, and what is the process that should have routed it to the storage team?
  5. How would you have recovered databases that were mid-commit, and in what order? Where is that documented?
  6. Is the failover to ARRAY-B documented to the level of zoning, LUN mapping and application re-pointing, and when was it last tested?
  7. What was the actual age of the last good backup for the ledger database, and was that a surprise?
  8. How did the team control the batch scheduler to avoid restarting jobs against unverified data?
  9. Which other arrays share the same firmware level, and what did the team do about them?
  10. What would the clearing house and customers have been told, and by whom?
  11. What evidence would an auditor want from this incident that the scribe did not capture?
  12. Which monitoring alerts should have fired before 01:19 (queue depth, latency, watchdog counters) and who owns creating them?
  13. What access or credentials did people lack for the out-of-band management path?
  14. How do you decide when failback is safe, and who signs off?
  15. If this happened on a holiday weekend, who would be unreachable?
