Symantec/CrowdStrike Endpoint Protection Engine Corrupt Definition Rollout

On-Premises Infrastructure

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a faulty content or definition update pushed by an endpoint protection platform that causes systems to crash or boot loop. The update is distributed automatically to thousands of endpoints and servers within minutes, and the security tool that is supposed to protect the estate becomes the cause of an outage. Recovery requires hands-on work on each device in many cases, because affected machines cannot reach the network or the management console.

Participants are working-level: endpoint security engineers, desktop, server and imaging operators, network and cloud management staff, security operations analysts, the service desk and application owners, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The scenario highlights the tension between rapid protection and controlled change, and how recovery scales when machines cannot boot.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Torrance Airlines Ground Services runs 9,200 Windows endpoints and 1,400 Windows servers (including check-in kiosks, gate systems, baggage handling and crew scheduling) protected by a leading endpoint protection platform with a kernel-mode sensor. Content updates, which include detection logic and definitions, are pushed from the vendor cloud to the sensors automatically; the organization has an 'auto-update all' policy because security wanted maximum speed.

At 04:10 UTC (23:10 local) on Thursday, the vendor releases a content file that contains malformed data. The kernel-mode sensor parses it, causes a memory access violation and the machines crash with a blue screen. They reboot, load the sensor early and crash again. By 23:40, 3,100 endpoints and 640 servers, including domain controllers, hypervisor management VMs and the gate and baggage systems, are in boot loops. Some machines on the overnight shift remain up because they had not yet pulled the update. The vendor's console is slow, and the vendor publishes a notice at 00:15 that a defective content update has been withdrawn and workaround steps (boot into Safe Mode or Recovery and delete a specific file) are available.

What the team does not yet know: BitLocker recovery keys for many machines are stored in Active Directory, which is itself down because the domain controllers are affected, servers on virtualization platforms can be fixed by attaching disks to a recovery VM but only if storage and vCenter are available, remote staff and field kiosks need physical visits, and the morning bank of flights is at 05:30.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize that the security tool is the cause. Participants correlate crash dumps, timing and vendor notices, and halt further distribution and automated actions that could spread the defect.
  • Prioritize recovery by operational criticality. The team defines the order: identity (domain controllers), virtualization and storage management, then gate, baggage and crew scheduling systems, then desktops.
  • Execute scalable recovery. Participants plan Safe Mode and Recovery Environment steps, boot media, scripted remediation, disk attach methods for VMs and field deployment of technicians.
  • Solve key and credential dependencies. The team addresses BitLocker recovery keys, local administrator credentials, offline access and the circular dependency on AD.
  • Maintain operations manually. Operations managers use manual procedures for check-in, boarding and baggage handling while systems are rebuilt.
  • Reconsider update governance. The team examines staged rollouts, rings, content update controls and vendor risk.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Do not assume the vendor will provide a quick automated fix. Use the vendor's published workaround steps only as described by the facilitator.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Mass blue screens and boot loops lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment and cause. Distribution is halted; cause is tied to the content update.

T+01:15 — Recovery prioritization. Order of recovery and resource plan are set.

T+02:00 — Identity and keys. Domain controllers and key access are restored.

T+03:00 — Critical operational systems. Gate, baggage and scheduling servers are recovered.

T+04:30 — Scaled endpoint recovery. Technicians and scripts recover endpoints in waves.

T+06:00 — Validation. Sensor state, protection and services are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Operations resume and incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Content update crashes thousands of Windows systems

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 23:50 local time. The NOC dashboard turns red as hundreds of hosts drop off the network. The service desk receives calls from the gate agents: kiosks and gate readers show a blue screen and reboot repeatedly. Two of four domain controllers are in a boot loop, and the vCenter management VM is down.

The endpoint security engineer sees in the console that a content update was pushed at 23:08 local time to all sensor groups under the 'auto-update all' policy. Crash dumps show a fault in the sensor driver's parsing routine. A vendor status page notice says 'a defective content update has been withdrawn; Safe Mode workaround steps are available.'

The IT operations lead estimates 3,100 endpoints and 640 servers are affected. BitLocker is enabled on the endpoints and servers; recovery keys are stored in AD, which is partially down. The first flight departs at 05:30, and the baggage system servers are among those affected.

Facilitator provides: the crash dump summary, the vendor notice text, the asset class counts and the gate and baggage system list.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident, set recovery priorities and name leads for identity, operations and endpoints.
  • Decide to disable auto-update immediately and halt any further content.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Endpoint Security Engineering
  • Confirm the cause and halt distribution.
  • Describe the workaround and who tests it.
  • Plan a safe version pin and staged rollout.
Desktop, Server & Imaging Operations
  • Prioritize domain controllers and key servers.
  • Plan how to access BitLocker keys without AD.
  • Organize recovery teams and logistics.
Network & Cloud Management Operations
  • Prepare recovery boot and OOB access.
  • Verify the content cannot redeploy.
  • Monitor network load.
Security Operations & Threat Analysis
  • Confirm that this is not a cyber attack.
  • Watch for opportunistic attacks.
  • Assess the risk of reduced protection.
Service Desk & Application Owners
  • Issue guidance and prioritize critical users.
  • Gate and baggage owners: identify manual fallback.
  • Prepare validation tests.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Activate manual procedures for morning flights.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask why auto-update all was approved.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What is your evidence that the security tool is the cause, and what is your first containment action?
  2. In what order do you recover systems, and why must identity come first?
  3. How do you access BitLocker recovery keys if AD is down, and where else are keys stored?
  4. How would you recover a server VM without booting it, and which tools do you need?
  5. How do you scale the workaround to 3,100 endpoints with limited technicians?
  6. Is it acceptable to run systems without endpoint protection during recovery, and who accepts that risk?
  7. How will flights operate at 05:30 if baggage and gate systems are not back?
  8. What do airline customers and staff need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the BitLocker key dependency until OPS asks how recovery keys are retrieved.
  • If the team assumes the vendor will fix everything, ask what they will do in the next three hours regardless.
  • Look for the circular dependencies: AD, vCenter, keys, and sensor console.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, sets recovery priorities and approves manual operating procedures for the morning flights.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency resources such as call-in of staff, contractors and overtime.
  • Decides on risk acceptance for running without endpoint protection during recovery.

Endpoint Security Engineering

  • Confirms the faulty content update and halts further rollout, changes policy to disable auto-update and pins the safe content version.
  • Interprets the vendor workaround and tests it on a small set of machines.
  • Prepares a script or media for remediation at scale.
  • Coordinates with the vendor on the corrected content and its staged deployment.

Desktop, Server & Imaging Operations

  • Recovers domain controllers and key servers first, using Safe Mode, recovery media or disk attach.
  • Plans the use of BitLocker recovery keys, and manages the dependency on AD.
  • Assembles technician teams and logistics for field and gate recovery.
  • Maintains a recovery tracker by asset class and location.

Network & Cloud Management Operations

  • Provides network and PXE or recovery boot infrastructure and out-of-band access to servers.
  • Supports management consoles and remote access for recovery tools.
  • Ensures security tools are not blocked but that the faulty content cannot redeploy.
  • Monitors for network issues from machines in boot loops.

Security Operations & Threat Analysis

  • Verifies that the event is a vendor defect and not an attack, reviewing telemetry, hashes and vendor statements.
  • Watches for opportunistic attacks and social engineering during the outage.
  • Assesses the risk of running with reduced protection.
  • Preserves evidence and logs.

Service Desk & Application Owners

  • The service desk handles surge calls, issues instructions and prioritizes critical users.
  • Application owners (gate, baggage, crew scheduling) identify manual fallbacks and sequence of recovery.
  • Owners validate their systems after recovery.
  • Prepare test scripts.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates manual procedures for check-in, boarding and baggage and coordinates with airline customers.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership, airline partners and staff.
  • Coordinates regulatory notices where required.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 5.19-5.22 supplier management, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 availability).
  • Evaluates contractual obligations with airline customers and the vendor.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and vendor notices.
  • Logs findings on update governance, staged rollout and key escrow.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Security tooling is part of your attack surface and your availability risk. Use ring-based deployment for content updates, with a canary group, a delay for critical servers and automatic halt on health signals.

Protect recovery: store BitLocker recovery keys in at least two places reachable without AD, keep offline local administrator credentials and maintain recovery media and procedures.

Design for recovery at scale: scripted remediation, PXE recovery, disk attach procedures for VMs and a field technician plan. Practice on a small set of machines quarterly.

Break dependencies: domain controllers, virtualization management and the security console should not all fail from one cause. Diversify, and keep at least one identity source on a separate protection policy.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.32, 5.22 monitoring of supplier services) and SOC 2 expect change management for security tools, supplier risk reviews and tested recovery.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on recovery prioritization, key availability and update governance.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did the team link the outage to the content update and halt distribution?
  2. Why was auto-update all approved and who owns that decision?
  3. In what order did you recover systems and was it right?
  4. How did you obtain BitLocker keys and what was missing?
  5. How did you recover virtual servers without booting them?
  6. How did you scale recovery to thousands of endpoints?
  7. Who accepted running without protection and for how long?
  8. How were morning flights handled manually?
  9. What did you tell airline customers and the public?
  10. How did SOC separate defect from attack?
  11. How did the vendor communication help or hinder?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  13. What monitoring or canary would have caught the defect?
  14. What access or credentials were missing?
  15. What will change in your update rings?
