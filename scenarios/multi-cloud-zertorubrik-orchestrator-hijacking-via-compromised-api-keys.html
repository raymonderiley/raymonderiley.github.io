Multi-Cloud Zerto/Rubrik Orchestrator Hijacking via Compromised API Keys

AI Failure & Autonomous Agent Disruption

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a compromised API key used to hijack the disaster recovery and backup orchestration platform that manages replication and recovery across multiple clouds, allowing the attacker to alter failover plans, shorten retention and delete recovery points.

Participants are working-level: backup and recovery engineers, cloud platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations analysts, the AI platform and cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of orchestrator trust, credential rotation, immutable copies, recovery plan integrity and safe testing.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Ironwood Pharma uses a commercial replication and backup orchestrator across two clouds. API keys used by automation were created two years ago and stored in a shared repository. The platform has a single admin role for plan changes and retention. Immutable copies exist for only some tiers.

At 03:30 on Wednesday, the orchestrator logs show API calls from an unfamiliar address using an automation key. The calls edit three recovery plans to point at a staging network, reduce retention from 30 days to 2 days on key datasets, and begin deleting older recovery points. A scheduled test failover is queued for noon that would run the altered plan.

What the team does not yet know: how many recovery points are deleted, whether replicas are also affected, how the key was obtained, and whether the attacker has other footholds.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize orchestrator compromise. Participants link plan changes and deletions to a compromised key.
  • Stop the attacker. The team revokes keys and isolates the orchestrator.
  • Protect surviving recovery points. Engineers secure immutable and offline copies.
  • Verify recovery plans. Participants compare plans to approved baselines.
  • Restore trust. The team rotates credentials and rebuilds plan integrity.
  • Improve orchestrator security. Participants review keys, roles and immutable design.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Revoke the compromised key and block orchestrator write actions before reviewing any plan, and preserve all API logs unchanged.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Unusual API calls and plan changes lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Keys are revoked and recovery points protected.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Scope and integrity are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A trust and recovery plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Rotation and plan verification proceed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Operations stabilize.

T+06:00 — Validation. Recoverability is validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes recovery orchestration that an attacker cannot control and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

  • Revokes the key and blocks write actions.
  • Compares plans to baselines.

  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 8.13 backup, 5.17 authentication information, 8.2 privileged access, 8.24 cryptography, 5.24-5.28 incident management; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses data recoverability and retention integrity.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Vault orchestrator keys, rotate them regularly and limit them to least privilege.

Separate plan change and retention roles and require approval for retention reductions.

Keep immutable and offline copies independent of the orchestrator.

Alert on plan, retention and deletion changes.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.13, 5.17, 8.2, 8.24) and SOC 2 expect backup, authentication and key management evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on revoke-first and independent recovery points.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you recognize compromise?
  2. What did you revoke first?
  3. How did you protect copies?
  4. How did you verify plans?
  5. How did you rotate keys?
  6. What design do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
