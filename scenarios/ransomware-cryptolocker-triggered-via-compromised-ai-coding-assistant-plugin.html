Ransomware Cryptolocker Triggered via Compromised AI Coding Assistant Plugin

AI Failure & Autonomous Agent Disruption

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a compromised plugin for an AI coding assistant that runs with developer privileges, steals tokens, spreads to build servers and shared repositories, and deploys encryption malware on developer and build systems.

Participants are working-level: endpoint security engineers, developer platform engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations analysts, the AI platform and cloud support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of extension governance, token revocation, build server isolation, restoration of source and artifacts, and extortion handling.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Willow Analytics lets developers install extensions for an AI coding assistant from a public marketplace. Extensions run with the developer's local permissions. Build servers pull source with long-lived tokens. Source repositories are hosted in a managed service with branch protection but without immutable backups.

At 11:00 on Friday, a popular extension receives an update that includes a downloader. Within two hours, 90 developer machines run the payload. It harvests tokens, pushes malicious workflow files to repositories and encrypts local project folders on 40 machines. By 14:00 two build servers are encrypted and a ransom note demands payment in 48 hours.

What the team does not yet know: which tokens were stolen, whether repository history was altered, which artifacts are trustworthy, and whether the extension also reached customer-facing build pipelines.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize extension-based compromise. Participants link encryption to the extension update.
  • Contain spread. The team blocks the extension, revokes tokens and isolates machines.
  • Protect source and artifacts. Engineers verify repository integrity and artifact trust.
  • Recover systems. Participants restore build servers and developer machines.
  • Handle extortion. The team assesses the ransom note and legal obligations.
  • Improve developer tool governance. Participants review extension controls and token hygiene.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Block the extension and revoke all developer and build tokens before restoring any system, and preserve one affected machine for forensics.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Encrypted machines and malicious commits lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Extension is blocked and tokens revoked.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Scope and integrity are diagnosed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A recovery plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Systems and artifacts are restored in order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Development stabilizes.

T+06:00 — Validation. Integrity is validated.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Inject #1: A compromised coding assistant plugin encrypts developer and build systems

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 14:15. 40 developer machines and two build servers are encrypted. Malicious workflow files appeared in repositories.

The endpoint engineer finds the payload came with an extension update on 90 machines.

Tokens may have been stolen. A ransom demand sets a 48 hour deadline.

Facilitator provides: extension telemetry, repository audit logs, encrypted system list and the ransom note.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Authorize blocking the extension and revoking tokens.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Endpoint Security Engineering
  • Block extension.
  • Revoke tokens.
  • Review repos.
Developer Platform Engineering
  • Isolate machines.
  • Rebuild build servers.
  • Validate.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Define releases.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Decide holds.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Analyze payload.
  • Hunt persistence.
  • Preserve image.
AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison
  • Open cases.
  • Ask about takedown.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan customer notice.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about extortion and notice duties.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you confirm the extension is the source?
  2. What do you block first?
  3. Why revoke tokens before rebuilding?
  4. How do you trust repository content?
  5. How do you handle the ransom demand?
  6. What do customers hear?
  7. What do leaders hear?
  8. Why could extensions run with full developer rights?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal altered workflow files until T1 reviews repository logs.
  • If players pay or negotiate first, ask about options and law.
  • Look for block-and-revoke discipline.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes developer tools that cannot become a ransomware path and sets approval rules for risky changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes emergency changes, exceptions and spend.
  • Decides on stakeholder communications and the end of the incident.

Endpoint Security Engineering

  • Blocks the extension and revokes tokens.
  • Reviews repository history and workflow files.
  • Plans artifact verification.
  • Plans extension allowlist.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Developer Platform Engineering

  • Isolates machines and rebuilds build servers.
  • Restores from backups.
  • Validates systems.
  • Tracks changes.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners define critical releases and trusted artifacts.
  • The service desk logs developer contacts.
  • Owners decide release holds.
  • Prepare validation checks.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Analyzes the payload and spread.
  • Hunts for persistence.
  • Preserves a forensic image.
  • Adds detections for mass file encryption.
  • Records actions and results for the scribe.

AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison

  • Opens cases with the marketplace and repository host.
  • Asks about extension takedown.
  • Requests guidance on audit logs.
  • Escalates through account teams.
  • Records Google Cloud guidance and case numbers.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for analytics engineering and informs leadership.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates workarounds and external contacts.
  • Tracks business impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 8.19 software installation, 8.7 malware protection, 8.13 backup, 8.4 source code access, 5.24-5.28 incident management; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses software integrity and ransomware resilience.
  • Captures evidence of decisions and approvals.
  • Logs findings for the debrief.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Allowlist extensions, review updates and sandbox them from tokens and sensitive paths.

Use short-lived, scoped tokens for repositories and builds.

Keep immutable backups of source and artifacts and signed builds.

Detect mass file changes on developer machines.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.19, 8.7, 8.13, 8.4) and SOC 2 expect software, malware and backup control evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on block-and-revoke and integrity verification.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How did you recognize the source?
  2. What did you block first?
  3. How did you protect source?
  4. How did you handle the ransom?
  5. How did you recover?
  6. What governance do you want?
  7. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  8. What did the AI Platform Vendor & Cloud Support Liaison add?
  9. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  10. What access or permissions were missing?
  11. What alerts are missing?
  12. What temporary changes remain and who owns them?
  13. What would you change in the design?
  14. What did the team do well?
  15. What surprised you?
