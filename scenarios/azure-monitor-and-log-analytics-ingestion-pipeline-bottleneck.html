Azure Monitor and Log Analytics Ingestion Pipeline Bottleneck

Azure Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an ingestion bottleneck in Azure Monitor and Log Analytics. A chatty application release and a misconfigured diagnostic setting flood a workspace, ingestion is throttled and delayed, alerts based on logs fire late or not at all, security detections lag, and the daily cap stops ingestion for the rest of the day.

Participants are working-level: observability and Log Analytics engineers, cloud platform and SRE engineers, application owners, the service desk, security operations and monitoring staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of data collection rules, ingestion limits, daily caps, alert latency, cost controls and monitoring blind spots.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Fenwick Telecom sends logs from about 2,000 resources to a central Log Analytics workspace that feeds Microsoft Sentinel, application alerts and operational dashboards. A daily cap of 800 GB is set to control cost. Diagnostic settings were bulk-applied through Azure Policy to all resources.

At 08:30 on Wednesday, a new release of the billing application writes verbose debug logs, and a policy update enables 'allLogs' for firewall and storage diagnostic settings across 300 resources. Ingestion jumps from 400 GB per day to 5 TB per hour. By 09:15, ingestion latency rises from 2 minutes to 45 minutes, the daily cap is reached at 09:40, and ingestion stops until midnight UTC. Log-based alerts and Sentinel analytic rules stop seeing new data.

What the team does not yet know: security detections including impossible travel and privileged role changes are blind, metric alerts still work but cover only some conditions, an ongoing password spray attack began at 09:20, and raising the cap will allow ingestion but will also generate a large cost and still not fix the root causes.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize a monitoring blind spot. Participants read workspace usage, ingestion latency and cap status to realize alerts and detections are not reliable.
  • Restore critical visibility. The team prioritizes security and critical application logs, uses alternative signals and metrics, and decides on raising the cap.
  • Stop the flood. Engineers identify top tables and sources and remove or filter the offending diagnostic settings and debug logging.
  • Maintain security coverage. Security operations uses other sources and manual hunting while Sentinel data is delayed.
  • Control costs. Participants set data collection rules, table plans and caps with approvals.
  • Improve observability governance. Participants review log tiers, policy rollout testing, cap behavior and independent alerting.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Quantify GB per day or per hour when discussing ingestion and cost.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Missing alerts and delayed logs lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Critical visibility is restored and the flood is identified.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Top sources and causes are confirmed.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A filtering, cap and cost plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Offending settings are changed and the cap decision is executed.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Ingestion latency returns to normal.

T+06:00 — Validation. Alerts and detections are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Ingestion stops at the daily cap and alerts go quiet

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 10:05. The monitoring team notices that log-based alerts have not fired for 25 minutes and Sentinel dashboards show no new events. The workspace reports the daily cap reached at 09:40.

The observability engineer finds ingestion at 5 TB per hour from firewall diagnostics, storage logs and billing application debug logs. Ingestion latency before the cap was 45 minutes. Nobody changed the cap policy.

Security operations notes a password spray began at 09:20 but no detections have fired. Azure Service Health shows no incident. Raising the cap would add large costs.

Facilitator provides: usage table summary, cap status, policy change history and the release notes.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Decide on raising the cap with a ceiling.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Observability & Log Analytics Engineering
  • Identify top sources and volumes.
  • Plan DCR and diagnostic changes.
  • Plan cap and table plan decisions.
Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering
  • Roll back debug logging and the policy change.
  • Provide metric-based alerts.
  • Check application health.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Confirm critical alerts.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Fix logging levels.
Security Operations & Monitoring
  • Hunt using native logs.
  • Investigate password spray.
  • Prioritize connectors.
Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about latency.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform leadership of reduced visibility.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan manual checks.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask about retention obligations.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you know monitoring is blind and for what?
  2. What is the order of restoring visibility: security first or applications?
  3. How do you find and remove the flood?
  4. Do you raise the cap and with what ceiling?
  5. What alternative signals can you use now?
  6. How do you hunt during a detection gap?
  7. Why did one policy update affect 300 resources?
  8. What do leaders need to hear about visibility?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the password spray until OPS checks native logs.
  • If players raise the cap without limit, ask about cost and root cause.
  • Look for prioritization of security visibility.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes security visibility and critical application alerts and sets cost approval limits.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes raising the cap and emergency spend.
  • Decides on risk acceptance for blind spots.

Observability & Log Analytics Engineering

  • Queries the Usage table to find top tables, resources and ingestion volume.
  • Changes data collection rules and diagnostic settings, and applies transformations to drop noise.
  • Plans cap changes and basic-tier table plans.
  • Monitors ingestion latency.

Cloud Platform & SRE Engineering

  • Rolls back the debug logging release and policy changes.
  • Provides metric-based and synthetic alternatives for alerts.
  • Checks application health independently of logs.
  • Validates alert delivery.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners confirm which alerts are critical and what they can monitor otherwise.
  • The service desk logs reports of missing alerts.
  • Owners fix logging levels.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Security Operations & Monitoring

  • Hunts using native service logs and alternative sources while Sentinel is blind.
  • Investigates the password spray indicators.
  • Prioritizes data connectors for restoration.
  • Documents the detection gap.

Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on ingestion latency and the cap.
  • Asks about throttling limits and pipeline status.
  • Requests guidance on data collection rules.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for security and operations monitoring and informs leadership of reduced visibility.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual checks.
  • Tracks impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.15 logging, 8.16 monitoring; SOC 2 availability and security).
  • Assesses log retention obligations.
  • Captures evidence of decisions.
  • Logs findings on monitoring dependency.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Make monitoring resilient and not self-dependent: critical alerts must use metrics and synthetic checks that do not rely on the same ingestion pipeline, and the cap must have its own alert before it is hit.

Govern log volume: data collection rules with transformations, table plans by value, cost budgets by source and review of every policy that applies diagnostics at scale.

Test policy rollouts on a small scope and measure ingestion impact before expanding.

Protect security detections with priority data connectors and out-of-band hunting runbooks for gaps.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.15, 8.16) and SOC 2 expect monitoring evidence, retention controls and documented gaps.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on independence of alerting and ingestion governance.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How long before anyone noticed the blind spot?
  2. What caused the flood?
  3. How did you decide whether to raise the cap?
  4. What was your order of restoring visibility?
  5. How did you hunt during the gap?
  6. What did the security findings show?
  7. How did the policy remediation behave?
  8. What happened to application alerts?
  9. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  10. What did Microsoft support add?
  11. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  12. How are policy rollouts tested?
  13. What access or permissions were missing?
  14. What alerts are independent of logs?
  15. What would you change in observability design?
