AWS Aurora Global Database Replication Lag and Split-Brain

AWS Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines an Aurora Global Database whose cross-region replication lag grows from milliseconds to minutes, followed by an operator-driven failover that leaves application writers in two regions both believing they own the data. The result is divergent data (a split-brain at the application layer) even though Aurora itself enforces a single writer cluster.

Participants are working-level: database reliability engineers, cloud platform and network engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the AWS support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of managed versus unplanned global database failover, the meaning of RPO under lag and how application connection handling can cause writes to the wrong place.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Sterling Insurance Group runs its policy platform on an Aurora PostgreSQL Global Database with the primary cluster in us-east-1 and a secondary cluster in us-west-2, used for disaster recovery and some regional reads. Applications connect through the cluster endpoint in each region; a Route 53 failover record points the writer hostname to the active region, with a 60-second TTL. The documented RPO is 5 seconds and RTO 30 minutes.

At 13:10 on Thursday, a bulk policy renewal job generates a large write volume on the primary. The AuroraGlobalDBReplicationLag metric climbs to 40 seconds, then 3 minutes by 13:25. At 13:30, a network disturbance between the regions degrades replication further. The on-call engineer, seeing alarms and a request from leadership to 'fail over to be safe', triggers an unplanned failover (detach and promote) of the us-west-2 cluster at 13:42 without confirming the lag or stopping the primary writers.

What the team does not yet know: the us-east-1 cluster is still healthy and still accepting writes from application instances that cached its endpoint or whose DNS has not yet changed, the promoted us-west-2 cluster is now an independent writer missing the last 3 minutes of data, a batch scheduler in us-east-1 is still running, and the detached primary will need to be rebuilt as a new secondary, discarding any data it holds that the new primary lacks.

Exercise Objectives

  • Interpret replication lag and its consequences. Participants read AuroraGlobalDBReplicationLag, AuroraGlobalDBRPOLag and write metrics, and translate lag into potential data loss.
  • Choose between managed and unplanned failover. The team explains the difference, the preconditions and the effect on RPO, and who may approve an unplanned detach.
  • Detect and contain dual-writer conditions. Participants identify that both regions are accepting writes, stop writers on the old primary and fence application traffic.
  • Reconcile divergent data. The team plans how to compare and merge transactions written to the old and new primary, and who decides which wins on conflict.
  • Control DNS, endpoints and clients. Platform engineers manage Route 53 records, TTLs, connection pool refresh and caching in application clients.
  • Improve failover governance. The team examines runbooks, approval thresholds and automation guardrails.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Use the real Aurora Global Database terms and the actual actions available to you; if the runbook is silent, record that as a gap.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Dual-region symptoms and data inconsistencies lead to incident declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Writes to the old primary are stopped and clients are fenced.

T+01:15 — Scoping the divergence. Time window and tables with divergent writes are identified.

T+02:00 — Reconciliation strategy. Merge approach and conflict rules are agreed.

T+03:00 — Decision on authoritative source. The authoritative database is chosen and approved.

T+04:30 — Execution. Reconciliation is performed and the old primary is rebuilt as a secondary.

T+06:00 — Validation. Business reconciliations and data checks are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to service and close-out. Normal operations resume and incident closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Both regions accept writes after an unplanned failover

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 13:55. The operations center sees the us-west-2 cluster promoted to a standalone writer at 13:42. Application teams report that some policy records saved in the last 13 minutes are missing and others appear twice. Customer service sees stale premium amounts.

The database engineer checks CloudWatch: AuroraGlobalDBReplicationLag was above 170 seconds at the time of failover. The us-east-1 cluster is still reachable and shows active write connections from at least 40 application instances. The Route 53 writer hostname now points to us-west-2, but its TTL is 60 seconds and many clients cache for longer.

The renewal batch job is still running in us-east-1. Both clusters have inserted rows with the same sequence values for the policy number table. The failover was run by the on-call engineer without a second approver and without checking lag.

Facilitator provides: the lag graph, the connection counts by cluster, the failover event log and the policy number sequence status.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and set data integrity as the priority.
  • Decide to stop all writes to the old primary immediately.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Database Reliability Engineering (Aurora)
  • Confirm which cluster is accepting writes and quantify the window.
  • Describe how to find divergent transactions.
  • Warn about sequence collisions.
Cloud Platform & Network Engineering
  • Fence the old primary using security groups or load balancer rules.
  • Check Route 53 behavior and TTL caching.
  • Verify network health.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Stop the renewal batch and writers on the old endpoint.
  • Restart connection pools with the right endpoint.
  • List high-risk transactions.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dual-writer dashboard.
  • Confirm timelines.
  • List missing alarms.
AWS Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case and ask about the old primary.
  • Ask whether the lag cause is known.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Hold policy issuance with underwriting.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Ask for documented RTO and RPO.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask who approved the unplanned failover.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. What tells you both regions are accepting writes, and how quickly can you stop one?
  2. What did the lag mean for data loss at the moment of failover?
  3. Why did unplanned failover happen without checks, and what approval should it need?
  4. Which database is authoritative and on what basis?
  5. How do you find and merge divergent transactions, including sequence collisions?
  6. How do clients end up writing to the old endpoint after a DNS change?
  7. What do underwriting and customers need to know and what must not be issued?
  8. What guardrails would have prevented this?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the sequence collision until the DBA checks sequences.
  • If the team picks an authoritative database immediately, ask what evidence supports the choice.
  • Look for the idea that integrity outranks availability here.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Inject #2: Fencing blocks legitimate reads

Time frame T+00:20 · Variant A

Situation

A security group rule added to block the old primary also blocks read-only reporting that claims adjusters use. Claims processing stops, and the claims manager calls the service desk.

The network engineer used a broad deny because it was fastest.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Weigh claims continuity against risk of further writes.
Database Reliability Engineering (Aurora)
  • Offer a read-only user or reader endpoint path for claims.
Cloud Platform & Network Engineering
  • Replace the broad rule with one that blocks write-capable application subnets only, or use a read-only role.
  • Verify that the change took effect.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Confirm which claims queries are safe to run.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you fence writes but allow reads?
  2. What is the risk of an overly broad emergency rule?
  3. Who approves exceptions?
  4. How is the fence removed later?

Facilitator notes

  • Look for precision over speed.
  • Ask how the fence is documented.
  • Tests understanding of reader endpoints.

Inject #3: Old primary shows lingering sessions

Time frame T+00:20 · Variant B

Situation

Even after network fencing, the old primary shows 60 established sessions from instances that were already connected. Some are executing writes inside open transactions.

The DBA is unsure whether to terminate sessions, since terminating will roll back in-flight work.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Accept rollback of in-flight work as the cost of integrity.
Database Reliability Engineering (Aurora)
  • Identify sessions by application and age, and terminate in a controlled order.
  • Decide whether to set the cluster read-only if the option exists.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Stop the application instances holding the sessions.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Record the decision.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you end sessions safely?
  2. What happens to in-flight transactions?
  3. Can the cluster be made read-only?
  4. Who owns the loss of in-flight work?

Facilitator notes

  • Tests containment depth.
  • Ask how sessions are identified.
  • Look for acceptance of rollback.

Inject #4: Divergent window larger than thought

Situation

Log analysis shows writes were landing in both clusters nine minutes before the promotion, because a regional load balancer routed a share of traffic to a stale endpoint after an earlier DNS change.

The first estimate of the window was three minutes, so more tables and transactions are affected.

Role actions

Database Reliability Engineering (Aurora)
  • Rebuild the timeline from transaction logs and identify affected tables.
  • Re-estimate the volume of divergent transactions.
Cloud Platform & Network Engineering
  • Explain the stale endpoint and routing behavior.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Provide connection counts by endpoint over time.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Update the impact assessment.

Discussion questions

  1. How was the window underestimated?
  2. How do you verify the true window?
  3. Which tables are affected?
  4. How does this change the reconciliation plan?

Facilitator notes

  • Link to client endpoint design.

Inject #7: Replication cause remains unknown

Situation

AWS support has not yet provided a root cause.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Decide whether to delay rebuilding.

  • Plan rebuild timing and consider a snapshot-based approach.
Cloud Platform & Network Engineering
  • Diagnose with path metrics and compare with AWS Health information.

Discussion questions

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes stopping dual writes and protecting data integrity above availability.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes the choice of the authoritative database and acceptance of any data loss.
  • Approves a temporary business freeze on policy issuance.

Database Reliability Engineering (Aurora)

  • Verifies which cluster is writer, checks replication lag history and determines the data window at risk.
  • Identifies divergent writes by comparing transaction logs, sequences and timestamps.
  • Plans the reconciliation approach and the rebuild of the detached cluster as a secondary.
  • Checks for sequence collisions and unique key conflicts.

Cloud Platform & Network Engineering

  • Controls Route 53 failover records and TTLs, and checks health checks that may have flapped.
  • Blocks application traffic to the old primary at security groups or load balancers as an emergency fence.
  • Verifies cross-region network health and the cause of the disturbance.
  • Plans staged traffic restoration.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners stop batch jobs and writers pointed at the old endpoint, and restart pools with the right endpoint.
  • Identify transactions at risk: policy issuance, payments and claims.
  • The service desk issues guidance and tracks tickets.
  • Prepare reconciliation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of writes per cluster, replication lag, connections by endpoint and application errors.
  • Confirms alarms and checks for silent failures.
  • Records exact timelines.
  • Creates the missing alarms for dual-writer detection.

AWS Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a high-severity case and asks about the state of the detached cluster and recommended rebuild.
  • Asks about safe re-attachment and the lag cause.
  • Requests confirmation of Aurora behavior in the failover.
  • Records the guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates the continuity plan and informs underwriting and customer service of a policy issuance hold.
  • Issues time-stamped updates to leadership.
  • Coordinates manual policy issuance if needed.
  • Tracks downtime against RTO and RPO.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.13 backup, 8.14 redundancy; SOC 2 availability and processing integrity).
  • Evaluates regulatory implications of data integrity issues for insurance records.
  • Captures evidence of the failover decision and approvals.
  • Logs findings on failover approval and fencing.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Separate lag from failure. Alarm on AuroraGlobalDBRPOLag against the documented RPO and require evidence of lag and a second approver before an unplanned failover; prefer managed planned failover when both regions are healthy.

Fence before you promote. A runbook step that stops writers or blocks the old endpoint at the network layer must come first, because clients, caches and batch jobs will not follow DNS promptly.

Design clients for region change: short DNS TTLs, connection pool refresh, endpoint discovery through a single control point and write fencing tokens that prevent writes to a demoted cluster.

Plan reconciliation: globally unique keys (ULIDs or region-prefixed sequences), change data capture for comparison and a documented rule for conflict resolution owned by the business.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.14) and SOC 2 expect tested failover with data integrity checks, approval records and monitoring of replication against stated RPO.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on the failover approval and the time to stop dual writes.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. What was the replication lag when the failover was triggered and who knew?
  2. What approvals does an unplanned failover require and were they followed?
  3. How did the team discover both clusters were accepting writes?
  4. How quickly were writes to the old primary stopped and how?
  5. How was the authoritative database chosen and what evidence supported it?
  6. How were divergent transactions identified and reconciled?
  7. How did sequence collisions affect the merge?
  8. What did DNS TTL and client caching do to the incident?
  9. What happened to payments and policy issuance during the window?
  10. How did underwriting and customers receive information?
  11. What did the AWS support case add?
  12. What evidence did the scribe capture that auditors would need?
  13. What alarms for dual-writer detection should exist?
  14. What access or permissions were missing?
  15. What will change in the failover runbook?
