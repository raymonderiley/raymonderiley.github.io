Azure Key Vault Access Policy Revocation and Secret Lockout

Azure Cloud Resiliency

Exercise Overview

This tabletop exercise examines a Key Vault governance change that revokes access policies or RBAC role assignments for application identities, removing their ability to read secrets, keys and certificates. Applications fail to start or refresh configuration, database connections and signing operations stop, and the people who could restore access are themselves locked out.

Participants are working-level: cloud security and key management engineers, cloud platform and application engineers, application owners, the service desk, cloud operations and monitoring staff, the Microsoft support liaison, business continuity coordinators and compliance staff. The exercise tests understanding of access policies versus Azure RBAC, soft delete and purge protection, managed identities and break-glass recovery.

A full run takes 3 to 4 hours, and the twelve-inject library supports repeated sessions. Inject #1 alone supports a 90-minute session.

Scenario

Wolfpine Retail Banking stores connection strings, API keys and signing certificates in 14 Azure Key Vaults. A governance initiative migrates vaults from access policies to Azure RBAC. A script removes legacy access policies on 9 vaults at 14:00 and assigns RBAC roles from a CSV file that contains outdated principal names. The vault administrators group used for recovery was assigned through an access policy that has now been deleted.

By 14:20, applications that restart or rotate tokens fail to retrieve secrets. App Service apps show 'Key Vault reference' errors, AKS pods using the CSI driver fail to mount secrets, and an automated certificate renewal fails. The mobile banking backend cannot sign tokens because its key reference returns Forbidden. Attempting to fix the roles from the portal requires 'Owner' or 'User Access Administrator' on the vault scope, which only two people hold, and one is traveling.

What the team does not yet know: some applications cache secrets until restart, so more failures will appear as instances recycle, purge protection is off on three vaults and an engineer began deleting a vault to recreate it, the RBAC assignment CSV omitted the payments service identity, and the subscription owner role is held by a shared account whose MFA is tied to a retired phone.

Exercise Objectives

  • Recognize RBAC and access policy lockout. Participants interpret Forbidden errors, Key Vault logs and activity logs to find the removed assignments.
  • Restore access without widening risk. The team grants least-privilege roles to the right identities and avoids granting broad owner rights.
  • Protect the vaults. Engineers stop deletion attempts, check soft delete and purge protection and avoid recreating vaults.
  • Handle recovery of administrative access. Participants use break-glass roles and subscription owners, with approvals and evidence.
  • Control cascading failures. The team anticipates restarts and token rotations that will reveal more failures and pauses disruptive actions.
  • Improve key management governance. Participants review migration testing, role design, break-glass and monitoring.
  • Communicate precisely and document for audit. Updates to leadership, site managers and users are accurate and time-stamped, and the evidence collected would satisfy ISO/IEC 27001:2022 incident management and SOC 2 availability reviewers.

Ground Rules

  • This is a discussion-based exercise. No production systems, accounts, configurations or backups will be touched. Commands and consoles are discussed, not executed.
  • Participants answer as they would in their real roles, using the runbooks, contact lists and tools that exist today. If a document or control does not exist, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.
  • Treat every inject as true unless the facilitator states otherwise. Do not argue the premise. Work the problem.
  • Time moves on the facilitator's clock. When an inject lands, the exercise clock is the time stated in the inject, regardless of how long discussion took.
  • Decisions must be stated aloud and recorded by the scribe with the decision maker's name and the exercise-clock time.
  • No blame. The exercise evaluates systems, process and documentation, not individuals.
  • Observers and evaluators record but do not answer questions for players unless the facilitator asks.
  • Never print, share or paste secret values during the exercise; refer to secret names only.

Exercise Timelines

Timeline and Escalation Triggers

T+00:00 — Detection and declaration. Application failures and Forbidden errors lead to declaration.

T+00:30 — Containment. Deletion actions are frozen and admin access is verified.

T+01:15 — Diagnosis. Missing assignments are identified.

T+02:00 — Remediation planning. A restoration plan is agreed.

T+03:00 — Execution. Roles are restored in priority order.

T+04:30 — Stabilization. Applications recover and caches refresh.

T+06:00 — Validation. Access and integrity are verified.

T+07:30 — Return to normal and close-out. Normal operation resumes and the incident is closed for debrief.

Injects

Inject #1: Applications cannot read secrets after vault policy removal

Time frame T+00:00 · Variant Primary

Situation

It is 14:35. Mobile banking token signing fails with 'Forbidden' from Key Vault. App Service apps show Key Vault reference errors and AKS pods fail to mount secrets. The certificate renewal job failed.

The security engineer finds that nine vaults lost their access policies at 14:00 and that RBAC assignments came from a CSV with outdated principal names. The vault administrators group lost its access with the policy deletion.

An engineer has started deleting one vault to recreate it, and purge protection is off. Only two people have Owner rights; one is traveling. Azure Service Health shows no incident.

Facilitator provides: the vault activity log, role assignment diff, application error summary and the break-glass procedure.

Role actions

Incident Commander
  • Declare the incident and name the technical lead and scribe.
  • Stop the vault deletion and freeze changes.
  • Set the next update at T+00:20.
Cloud Security & Key Management Engineering
  • Identify missing assignments and restore by priority.
  • Check soft delete and purge protection.
  • Use break-glass for admin access.
Cloud Platform & Application Engineering
  • Plan restart order after access returns.
  • Check secret caching.
  • Validate signing.
Application Owners & Service Desk
  • Rank services and list needed secrets.
  • Service desk: issue the holding message.
  • Find identities missing from the CSV.
Cloud Operations & Monitoring
  • Build the dashboard.
  • Confirm logs.
  • List missing alerts.
Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison
  • Open a case.
  • Ask about owner recovery.
  • Record guidance.
Business Continuity & Communications
  • Inform branches.
  • Prepare the first update.
  • Plan manual operations.
GRC, Audit & Compliance
  • Open the incident record.
  • Ask how the migration was tested.
  • Start the evidence log.

Discussion questions

  1. How do you recognize an RBAC or access policy lockout from the errors?
  2. Why is deleting and recreating the vault dangerous?
  3. Who can restore access and how fast?
  4. Which identities get roles first and with what least privilege?
  5. What does cached secret behavior mean for later failures?
  6. How do you use break-glass without creating new risk?
  7. Why did the CSV omit identities?
  8. What do branches and customers need to hear?

Facilitator notes

  • Do not reveal the traveling owner until T1 tries to elevate.
  • If players grant Owner broadly, ask about least privilege.
  • Look for the deletion freeze first.
  • Time-box to 35 minutes.

Roles and Responsibilities Summary

Incident Commander

  • Declares the incident, prioritizes payment signing and mobile banking and sets a freeze on vault changes.
  • Runs a 20-minute cadence at first, assigns the scribe and records decisions.
  • Authorizes break-glass access and temporary elevated roles.
  • Decides on customer messaging.

Cloud Security & Key Management Engineering

  • Reads vault diagnostic logs, activity logs and role assignment history.
  • Restores least-privilege roles for application identities by priority.
  • Checks soft delete, purge protection and any deletion in progress.
  • Plans access review afterward.

Cloud Platform & Application Engineering

  • Restarts or refreshes applications in a safe order after access returns.
  • Checks managed identity configuration and secret reference caching.
  • Runs validation for signing and connections.
  • Prepares fallbacks.

Application Owners & Service Desk

  • Application owners rank services and list the secrets they need.
  • The service desk logs failures by service.
  • Owners identify identities missing from the CSV.
  • Prepare validation tests.

Cloud Operations & Monitoring

  • Builds a dashboard of Key Vault 403s and application errors.
  • Confirms logging to a protected workspace.
  • Checks Azure Service Health.
  • Adds alerts on role removal.

Microsoft Support & TAM Liaison

  • Opens a Sev A case on access recovery and subscription owner recovery.
  • Asks about vault recovery with soft delete.
  • Requests guidance on emergency elevation.
  • Records guidance.

Business Continuity & Communications

  • Activates continuity for digital banking and informs branches.
  • Issues time-stamped updates.
  • Coordinates manual operations.
  • Tracks impact.

GRC, Audit & Compliance

  • Ensures the incident is recorded and classified (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 5.24-5.28, 8.2 privileged access, 8.24 cryptography, 8.32 change management; SOC 2 security and availability).
  • Assesses regulatory impact.
  • Captures evidence of emergency access.
  • Logs findings on migration governance.

Resilience Architect Guidance

Test access model migrations in a non-production vault with representative identities, staged by vault tier, with automated comparison of effective permissions before and after.

Enable soft delete and purge protection on every vault and block deletion without approval through Azure Policy.

Design for recovery: tested break-glass, at least two subscription owners with working MFA, and alerts on role assignment removal.

Use managed identities with least-privilege roles and reduce reliance on cached secrets by supporting refresh without restart.

Auditors for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (8.2, 8.24) and SOC 2 expect tested privileged access, key management controls and change evidence.

Post-Exercise Debrief Guide

  1. Close the exercise formally. The facilitator announces the end of the exercise, reminds participants that all actions were simulated, and thanks the players. Nobody leaves before the hot wash.
  2. Take five minutes of silent individual notes. Each participant writes three things that worked, three that did not, and any gap in tools, access or documentation, before group discussion begins.
  3. Run the hot wash with the full group. Use the Hot wash questions below, going role by role for the first pass and then opening the floor. Keep to 45 to 60 minutes. Focus on break-glass access and vault protections.
  4. Hold evaluator readouts. Evaluators share timing observations, decision-log quality, communication accuracy and gaps against each objective.
  5. Prioritize findings. Group findings into people, process, technology and documentation, rate each by impact and effort, and select no more than ten for the corrective action plan.
  6. Assign corrective actions. Each action has an owner, a due date and a verification method. Record them in the GRC tracker as evidence for ISO/IEC 27001:2022 management review and SOC 2 reviews.
  7. Publish the after-action report within 10 business days. Include the timeline, decisions, findings, root causes, corrective actions and runbook updates, and circulate for review.
  8. Schedule the follow-up. Plan a validation drill in 90 days for the highest-priority gaps and rerun the exercise with different injects within 6 to 12 months.

Hot wash questions

Use these with the full group of participants after the individual note-taking step.

  1. How quickly did you identify the missing roles?
  2. What happened with the vault deletion?
  3. Who could restore access and how long did it take?
  4. How did you scope least-privilege roles?
  5. How did cached secrets affect the timeline?
  6. How did you handle broad access granted in haste?
  7. What happened with the certificate renewal?
  8. How accurate were updates to leadership?
  9. What did Microsoft support add?
  10. What evidence did the scribe capture for auditors?
  11. How was the migration tested?
  12. What access or permissions were missing?
  13. What alerts are missing?
  14. How is break-glass tested?
  15. What would you change in key governance?
